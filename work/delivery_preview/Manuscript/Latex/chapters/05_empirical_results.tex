\section{Lead-Time Dynamics and Progression across Horizons}
\label{sec:res_horizon_progression}

At $H=12$ forecasts predict a zero yield anomaly ($\hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y} = 0$) by construction, projecting solely the county-specific linear technological trend $\hat{\tau}_{c,Y}$. The out-of-sample root mean squared error of this naive benchmark defines the baseline error reference across the 2011--2025 test panel:
\begin{equation}
    \text{RMSE}_{\text{naive}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M_{\text{test}}} \sum_{Y=2011}^{2025} \sum_{c=1}^C \epsilon_{c,Y}^2} = 6.12\text{ bu/ac}, \quad R^2_{\text{OOS}}(H=12) \equiv 0.00
    \label{eq:rmse_naive_reference}
\end{equation}
This baseline provides the normalizing denominator in Equation~\eqref{eq:r2_oos}. Positive $R^2_{\text{OOS}}$ values at subsequent horizons ($H = 11 \dots 1$) consequently quantify the incremental predictive skill contributed by observed agrometeorological conditions beyond the trend baseline.

Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} compares candidate models across the twelve forecast horizons under two information sets: a pure meteorological feature space and an integrated specification incorporating the prior-year detrended yield anomaly ($\epsilon_{c,Y-1}$). At the final horizon ($H=1$), all specifications implement harvest closure $H^*(s)$, freezing the information set on September 30 for Minnesota (Maturity Group I) and October 31 for the remaining states to eliminate post-harvest atmospheric noise. Consistent with the validation tuning in Section~\ref{sec:meth_lstm}, the weather-only LSTM employs a PReLU projection head, while the integrated architecture uses a linear projection head.

\input{tables/tab_horizon_performance}

The empirical performance matrix in Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} and corresponding trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:r2_horizon_progression} track predictive skill across the production cycle at county level ($M_{\text{test}} = 2{,}025$ out-of-sample county-year evaluations). County-level evaluation measures local predictive accuracy directly, without the variance reduction that occurs when averaging across counties. Across the twelve monthly horizons, performance follows three distinct chronological regimes:

Before May ($H = 11 \dots 7$, Dec--Apr), weather-only models do not improve upon the naive baseline. These monthly forecast origins precede the primary planting window documented by \citet{Hoffman2020}. Integrated ElasticNet remains close to the baseline ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \approx 0.00$), while flexible non-linear estimators generate negative scores, reflecting variance inflation and overfitting on uninformative antecedent noise.

With the inclusion of May planting-window observations ($H = 6$, May 31), initial predictive skill begins to emerge across candidate architectures: pure meteorological XGBoost crosses into positive territory at $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = +0.01$ (rising to $+0.05$ with prior-year carryover). This marks the operational inflection point where environmental observations first surpass the naive baseline.

Predictive accuracy accelerates as reproductive weather accumulates across summer ($H = 5 \dots 1$, Jun--Oct). Substantial performance gains concentrate during flowering (July, $H=4$) and pod filling (August, $H=3$), when moisture deficits and thermal stress directly dictate seed mass. Across all candidate architectures, skill reaches a plateau proximate to physiological maturity, with marginal increments between September and October.

Incorporating prior-year detrended yield memory ($\epsilon_{t-1}$) provides a consistent, incremental improvement of approximately $+0.03$ points in $R^2_{\text{OOS}}$ across all model families at harvest closure (ElasticNet rises from $0.29$ to $0.32$, LSTM from $0.34$ to $0.37$, and the ensemble from $0.35$ to $0.38$). In contrast to previous studies that report massive variance gains from autoregressive lags on raw yields \citep{ChenZhang2026}, our controlled detrending isolates genuine multi-year agronomic memory---such as deep soil moisture carryover and crop rotational inertia---from historical technological drift.

\section{Comparative Model Performance across Active Horizons}
\label{sec:res_active_horizons}

Across the active growing season ($H = 5 \dots 1$, June--October), out-of-sample forecast accuracy reveals three core empirical insights on model complexity and inductive biases:

First, penalized linear regression (ElasticNet) achieves the highest individual accuracy during vegetative growth and flowering ($H = 5 \dots 3$, June to August), reaching $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.30$ ($0.28$ weather-only). When the physical signal is only partially accumulated, regularized shrinkage restricts estimation variance on high-dimensional climate inputs \citep{James2023}. Non-linear tree ensembles (Random Forest and XGBoost) do not improve upon ElasticNet on monthly tabular features, yielding lower out-of-sample scores ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0.28$).

Second, the recurrent neural network (LSTM) accelerates skill as reproductive weather accumulates, matching ElasticNet in August ($H=3, R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.29$--$0.30$) and surpassing it from September onward ($H=2$, reaching $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.33$, and $H=1$, reaching $0.37$ with lag vs $0.34$ weather-only). While monthly tabular models aggregate daily weather into 30-day blocks, the LSTM processes 10-day decad sequences, capturing the timing and duration of temperature and moisture stress during pod filling without increasing feature dimensionality.

Third, the equal-weighted convex ensemble (50\% ElasticNet + 50\% LSTM) consistently achieves champion performance across active horizons, culminating in $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.38$ at harvest closure ($0.35$ under weather-only forcing). This synergy stems from the empirical orthogonality of model forecast residuals ($r < 0.70$), combining the variance stability of linear shrinkage with the sequence sensitivity of recurrent networks.

Having established the empirical gain of prior-year detrended yield carryover ($\epsilon_{t-1}$), all subsequent stress tests, macro-regional evaluations, and diagnostic attributions focus on the lag-integrated model specifications (referred to simply as ElasticNet, Random Forest, XGBoost, LSTM, and Ensemble).

\section{Inferential Significance and Tail-Risk Stress Testing}
\label{sec:res_significance_tail_risk}

\subsection{Pairwise Predictive Superiority: Diebold--Mariano Tests}
\label{sec:res_significance}

To establish whether predictive gains over the naive baseline and between competing model families are statistically distinguishable from sample variability, we evaluate one-sided \citet{DieboldMariano1995} tests on annual mean squared loss differentials across the 15 out-of-sample test years (2011--2025). Following the protocol formalized in Section~\ref{sec:meth_significance_bootstrap}, squared loss differentials are first averaged across the 135 counties within each crop year to account for contemporaneous spatial correlation, and test statistics are estimated with Newey--West HAC standard errors.

At the June emergence horizon ($H=5$), weather-only ElasticNet and integrated LSTM achieve statistically significant loss reductions over the naive trend benchmark, with nominal $p$-values of $0.0011$ and $0.00025$ ($\text{DM} = 3.06$ and $3.48$). Conversely, tree ensembles fail to reject the null hypothesis of equal accuracy at the $5\%$ level (Random Forest $p=0.074$; XGBoost $p=0.094$). From July ($H=4$) onward, all evaluated weather-informed models reject equal accuracy against the naive baseline ($p < 0.0001$).

At harvest closure ($H=1$), pairwise testing between LSTM and ElasticNet yields $\text{DM} = 0.99$ ($p = 0.162$). Despite a $+0.050$ point improvement in $R^2_{\text{OOS}}$ and a $0.188\text{ bu/ac}$ reduction in RMSE, the sequential deep architecture does not achieve statistically significant superiority over penalized linear shrinkage under serial correlation controls. The Linear-Recurrent Ensemble significantly improves upon ElasticNet at the $5\%$ level ($\text{DM} = 2.11$, $p = 0.017$), while its performance difference from LSTM remains statistically indistinguishable ($\text{DM} = 0.33$, $p = 0.371$). These tests confirm that while meteorological forcing provides unequivocal predictive skill, pooled point rankings between flexible neural networks and regularized linear baselines do not establish uniform statistical dominance.

The decile breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:decile_error_performance} and diagnostic suite in Figure~\ref{fig:decile_calibration_and_residuals} evaluate how effectively meteorological observations calibrate predictions across the yield distribution. Grouping predictions by realized anomaly deciles reveals a structural trade-off between conditional variance reduction and tail-risk sensitivity under squared-error loss.

Across distribution extremes, model predictions exhibit systematic conditional shrinkage. For both severe downside shocks ($D_1$, realized mean $-8.82\text{ bu/ac}$) and bumper harvests ($D_{10}$, realized mean $+11.01\text{ bu/ac}$), predictions consistently compress toward the zero-anomaly baseline. At harvest closure, LSTM and the ensemble predict $-2.18$ and $-2.16\text{ bu/ac}$ in $D_1$, and $+2.85\text{ bu/ac}$ in $D_{10}$. This attenuation reflects standard $L_2$ conditional-mean shrinkage: because extreme agricultural shocks involve unobserved local factors such as subsurface drainage, seed genetics, and localized pest pressure, estimators trained under mean squared error naturally shrink predictions toward zero to avoid excessive variance penalties. The models correctly anticipate the physical sign and direction of the stress, substantially reducing quadratic loss relative to the naive benchmark.

Conversely, across intermediate regimes ($D_2$--$D_9$), predicted values scale monotonically with realized anomalies as reproductive weather accumulates between June ($H=5$) and harvest ($H=1$). Residual Q-Q plots in Figure~\ref{fig:decile_calibration_and_residuals}(b) confirm that forecast departures remain symmetric around zero, with homoskedastic error dispersion across fitted values (panel c).

Beyond tail calibration, evaluating the spatiotemporal independence of out-of-sample residuals validates model specification. At harvest closure, mean county AR(1) coefficients remain close to zero ($-0.054$ for ElasticNet, $-0.062$ for XGBoost, and $-0.058$ for LSTM). Conditioning on ERA5-Land weather features absorbs large-scale atmospheric patterns, reducing Moran's $I$ to $0.142$ in ElasticNet and $0.145$ in LSTM ($0.171$ in XGBoost). This residual clustering demonstrates that while gridded reanalysis captures macro-regional weather fronts, contiguous counties share unmodelled spatial determinants---such as contiguous soil associations, regional aquifer access, and common agricultural management practices---that induce correlated local errors.

In terms of lead-time shock detection, substantial error contraction occurs well ahead of harvest. In 2012, errors drop sharply between June ($H=5, 6.16\text{ bu/ac}$) and August ($H=3, 5.58\text{ bu/ac}$), confirming that the signature of the flash drought is already captured by late August. Similarly, in 2016, favorable conditions throughout July and August enable ElasticNet to contract its error from $8.19\text{ bu/ac}$ in June to $5.13\text{ bu/ac}$ in August and $4.22\text{ bu/ac}$ in September.

\section{Agrometeorological Drivers and Attribution Across Scales}
\label{sec:res_feature_attribution}

\subsection{Attribution Methodology and Surrogate Recurrent Mapping}
\label{sec:res_shap_attribution}

We evaluate agrometeorological sensitivities across model paradigms using Grouped Permutation Feature Importance \citep[GPFI;][]{Sweetetal2023} across 19 predictor groups: 17 weather indicators, lagged yield, and jointly permuted spatial coordinates on the 2011--2025 out-of-sample test panel. For each physical indicator $j$, all constituent monthly features across the full campaign ($m \in \{\text{Nov}, \dots, \text{Oct}\}$) are permuted jointly across test instances ($\pi_j$). Grouping across all campaign months preserves the seasonal correlation profile while breaking the association with realized yield anomalies. Predictive degradation is quantified by out-of-sample explained variance loss:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(j) = \max\left(0, \, R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\text{base}) - R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\pi_j)\right)
    \label{eq:pfi_metric}
\end{equation}
where the non-negativity constraint neutralizes sampling noise from uninformative features.

Because baseline predictive accuracy differs across candidate architectures, absolute variance losses are normalized into a Relative Information Share (\%):
\begin{equation}
    s_j = \frac{\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{19} \Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(k)} \times 100\%
    \label{eq:pfi_share}
\end{equation}
Normalizing into relative information shares allows direct comparison of attentional distributions across model families.

For the sequential LSTM, permuting input channels across three-dimensional sequence tensors ($N \times 36 \times 19$) severely disrupts recurrent hidden state dynamics and entails prohibitive computational cost. We therefore implement a Global Surrogate Model \citep{Sweetetal2023}: a gradient-boosted tree ensemble (XGBoost) trained to replicate the predictions of the LSTM in anomaly space. The surrogate achieves near-perfect fidelity ($R^2 = 0.9968$ for $H=1$ and $R^2 = 0.9998$ for $H=2$, with $\text{RMSE} < 0.18\text{ bu/ac}$), legitimizing its use for post-hoc agrometeorological attribution.

Because atmospheric demand and hydrological supply variables are highly collinear, individual architectures select substitute proxies reflecting their inductive biases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{XGBoost} operates as an acute thermal-stress sensor: it is dominated by Atmospheric Demand and Heat ($28.9\%$, driven by non-linear thresholds of $VPD$ at $18.9\%$) and Thermal Regimes ($34.6\%$), while assigning minimal weight to multi-year yield memory ($4.1\%$).
    \item \textbf{ElasticNet} distributes predictive weights across Thermal Regimes ($43.0\%$), Atmospheric Demand ($25.6\%$), and Soil Moisture ($20.9\%$), selecting dewpoint temperature ($T_{\text{dew}}$, $27.9\%$) as a parsimonious linear proxy for surface moisture.
    \item \textbf{LSTM} acts as a cumulative water balance integrator and memory anchor: predictive shares are led by Hydrological Supply ($29.7\%$, dominated by $P - ET_0$ at $24.9\%$) and Memory and Spatial Baseline ($25.1\%$, where lagged yield contributes $21.2\%$), capturing soil moisture recharge and multi-year agronomic persistence.
\end{itemize}

\section{Anomaly Space versus Raw Yield Performance}
\label{sec:res_raw_vs_anomaly}

We reconstruct physical yield forecasts by adding the training-only trend to the predicted anomaly, following Equation~\eqref{eq:yield_reconstruction}:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_{c,Y}^{(H)} = \hat{\tau}_{c,Y} + \hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(H)}
    \label{eq:level_projection}
\end{equation}
As established in Section~\ref{sec:meth_loss_metrics}, RMSE and MAE are algebraically identical between anomaly and reconstructed yield spaces because trend extrapolation $\hat{\tau}_{c,Y}$ is constant across models. Table~\ref{tab:raw_vs_anomaly_performance} reports performance metrics across both spaces at harvest closure across the $2{,}025$ test evaluations.

\input{tables/tab_raw_vs_anomaly_performance}

Evaluating predictions on reconstructed physical yield levels ($\hat{y} = \hat{\tau} + \hat{\epsilon}$) clarifies the distinction between long-term agronomic progress and interannual weather skill (Table~\ref{tab:raw_vs_anomaly_performance}). The naive trend benchmark accounts for $47.4\%$ of total raw yield variance ($r = 0.746$) across the test partition without ingesting meteorological data, capturing multidecadal genetic breeding, fertilizer efficiency, and permanent cross-county soil endowments.

Conditioning on ERA5-Land surface reanalysis and historical carryover raises explained variance to $66.8\%$ ($r = 0.833$) for LSTM and $67.3\%$ ($r = 0.838$) for the Linear-Recurrent Ensemble, resolving an incremental $+19.9$ percentage points of commercial yield variation beyond technological momentum.

Evaluating predictions on both levels also contextualizes comparisons with studies that benchmark models directly on raw physical yields \citep{ChenZhang2026, Khaki2020}. In raw yield space, high explained variance ($R^2 \ge 0.65$--$0.75$) reflects the joint contribution of multidecadal agronomic trends and interannual meteorological shocks. Projecting forecasts into anomaly space separates these two components, confirming that the empirical models capture genuine climate-induced departures from trend ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.378$) while preserving operational utility for physical production volume estimates.

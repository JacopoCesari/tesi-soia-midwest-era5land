\chapter{Empirical Results}
\label{chap:empirical_results}

Building on the econometric specifications and validation protocols formalized in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, this chapter presents the out-of-sample empirical findings of the multi-horizon forecasting framework. Evaluations execute strictly on the expanding test partition spanning harvest years 2011--2025 ($N_{\text{test}} = 15$ campaigns, $M_{\text{test}} = 2{,}025$ county-year evaluations per lead time), with model hyperparameters fixed on the 1985--1995 validation partition across all twelve forecast horizons ($H = 12 \dots 1$).

Section~\ref{sec:res_horizon_progression} evaluates the complete twelve-horizon continuum ($H = 12 \dots 1$) to identify where meteorological observations begin to outperform reference trend baselines. Because the full benchmark establishes that cold-season weather prior to late June ($H \ge 6$) provides zero predictive skill over naive trend extrapolation ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0$) and statistically significant skill emerges exclusively during the active growing season ($H \le 5$), subsequent sections focus detailed diagnostic stress testing, tail-risk calibration, and feature attributions on these five active campaign months.

\begin{equation}
    \text{RMSE}_{\text{naive}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M_{\text{test}}} \sum_{Y=2011}^{2025} \sum_{c=1}^C \epsilon_{c,Y}^2} = 6.12\text{ bu/ac}, \quad R^2_{\text{OOS}}(H=12) = 0.00

\end{equation}

The empirical performance matrix in Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} reveals four distinct agrometeorological dynamics across the production cycle:

\paragraph{Pre-Sowing Dormancy Noise Regime ($H = 11 \dots 7$, Dec--Apr).}
Prior to May, observed weather provides zero statistically detectable predictive skill over historical trends ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0$). This absence of signal stems from fundamental agronomic timing: in the Midwestern Corn Belt, soybean planting takes place between late April and mid-May \citep{Hoffman2020}. Before May, fields are dormant or in early tillage; winter atmospheric conditions precede crop germination and carry no statistical association with harvest yields. Furthermore, spring rainfall across the region typically recharges topsoil moisture to field capacity, resetting winter moisture anomalies before germination. In this phase, flexible non-linear architectures overfit on stochastic cold-season noise, degrading out-of-sample accuracy substantially below the naive benchmark (e.g., XGBoost registers $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = -0.19$ at $H=11$; LSTM drops between $-0.10$ and $-0.27$). Penalized linear regression (ElasticNet) remains closest to the zero baseline ($R^2 = -0.01 \dots -0.04$) by shrinking uninformative winter coefficients toward zero \citep{James2023}.

\paragraph{Sowing and Emergence Inflection ($H = 6$, May 31).}
At late spring planting, initial predictive skill begins to emerge. Ingesting seedbed moisture and early thermal pacing ($P - ET_0$, $GDD$), candidate models cross into positive territory: pure meteorological XGBoost reaches $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = +0.01$ (rising to $+0.05$ with prior-year carryover). This marks the operational inflection point where environmental observations first surpass the secular trend benchmark.

\paragraph{In-Season Skill Accumulation ($H = 5 \dots 1$, Jun--Oct).}
From late June onward, predictive accuracy expands rapidly as reproductive weather accumulates. In July ($H=4$, flowering and pod initiation), models explain between $12\%$ and $22\%$ of anomaly variance. In August ($H=3$, pod-filling), skill increases further to $R^2 = 0.22 \dots 0.32$, capturing critical reproductive stress \citep{Schlenker2009}, stabilizing as the crop approaches physiological maturity in September ($H=2$) and harvest in October ($H=1$).

\paragraph{Marginal Value of Lagged Yield Memory ($\epsilon_{t-1}$).}
Comparing the Weather-Only and Integrated blocks in Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} shows that conditioning on prior-year anomaly yields a modest, consistent improvement during the active season ($H \le 5$). For tabular architectures (ElasticNet, Random Forest, XGBoost), historical carryover increases explained variance by $+0.01$ to $+0.03$ $R^2$ points (e.g., ElasticNet rises from $0.28$ to $0.31$ at harvest; XGBoost from $0.28$ to $0.31$). For the sequential LSTM, carryover provides a larger boost at early emergence ($R^2 = 0.13$ at $H=5$) and at harvest ($R^2 = 0.35$ at $H=1$). This integrated gain operates through the linear projection head. Concatenating $\epsilon_{t-1}$ into the linear head provides an additive regularizer alongside recurrent weather states, achieving $R^2 = 0.35$. Passing historical carryover through the non-linear PReLU head induces over-parameterization on the persistent scalar, restricting integrated harvest skill to $R^2 = 0.29$. Because $\epsilon_{t-1}$ is detrended strictly on training data, this linear integration captures rotational and deep soil recharge persistence without confounding the secular technological trend \citep{ChenZhang2026}. Consequently, all subsequent operational evaluations and diagnostic stress tests deploy this top-performing integrated specification (LSTM +Lag with linear head).

\section{In-Depth Analysis of Active Forecast Horizons}
\label{sec:res_active_horizons}

\subsection{Comparative Model Performance}
\label{sec:res_champion_selection}

Because the full-horizon benchmark establishes that lead times prior to late spring ($H \ge 7$) contain zero predictive skill, we concentrate detailed econometric diagnostics strictly on the active campaign months ($H = 5 \dots 1$, June 30 to October 31). As established by the validation protocol formalized in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, all evaluations deploy the champion model configurations selected during the validation phase: ElasticNet with mixed $\ell_1/\ell_2$ shrinkage \citep{James2023}, Random Forest ($B=300$ trees), XGBoost ($\eta=0.05$, max-depth 4), and the sequential LSTM operating on 10-day decads with temporal attention ($h=128, l=2$, linear projection head with prior-year lag) \citep{Khaki2020}.

Within this operational window, Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} and Figure~\ref{fig:r2_horizon_progression} provide a clear comparative evaluation between regularized linear estimation, tree ensembles, and sequential deep learning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early-Season Stability of Regularized Linear Modeling ($H=5, 4$):} During early vegetative growth (June) and initial flowering (July), ElasticNet achieves superior out-of-sample accuracy across candidate architectures. When observed weather series are short and signal-to-noise ratios remain low, penalized $\ell_1/\ell_2$ shrinkage contracts uninformative coefficients and minimizes estimation variance. In contrast, parameterized recurrent architectures suffer from higher parameter instability over truncated input sequences \citep{James2023}.
    \item \textbf{Late-Season Sequence Advantage in Recurrent Architectures ($H=3 \dots 1$):} As reproductive development progresses through pod-filling and seed maturation (August--October), the sequential LSTM operating on 10-day decads gains predictive advantage. Structuring weather into sub-monthly intervals enables the recurrent network to register acute compound thermal and moisture excursions (such as multi-day heatwaves during pod set) that monthly aggregations smooth out \citep{Yinetal2026}.
    \item \textbf{Competitive Parity of Parsimonious Linear Shrinkage:} Across the full season, ElasticNet establishes a robust, highly competitive benchmark. Tree ensembles (Random Forest and XGBoost) exhibit no systematic accuracy advantage over penalized linear regression on tabular monthly features. ElasticNet provides convex optimization, minimal computational cost, and complete resilience against cold-season noise overfitting.
    \item \textbf{Orthogonal Bias Fusion via the Linear-Recurrent Ensemble ($H = 5 \dots 1$):} Blending regularized linear estimation with sequential deep learning achieves champion performance across all active lead times. The equal-weighted ensemble combines complementary inductive biases: global parameter shrinkage that suppresses background estimation variance, paired with recurrent attention sensitive to intra-seasonal stress pulses, securing stable out-of-sample skill through harvest eve.
\end{itemize}
We retain the Linear-Recurrent Ensemble alongside ElasticNet (+Lag) and LSTM (+Lag) as primary benchmarks for subsequent tail-risk stress testing to assess downside protection during historical shock years.

\section{Macro-Regional Performance and Tail-Risk Evaluation}

\subsection{Macro-Regional Portfolio Forecasts across 15 Test Campaigns}
\label{sec:res_macro_portfolio}

In commercial grain logistics, supply-chain risk management, and agricultural commodity markets, operational decisions evaluate production shocks at the aggregate regional level. Cross-sectional pooling across the 135 study counties cancels out idiosyncratic, uncorrelated microclimatic variance at rate $1/\sqrt{N}$ by the Law of Large Numbers, isolating the synoptic meteorological forcing that drives regional supply shocks.

To evaluate whether candidate architectures reliably anticipate aggregate market supply directly in the anomaly domain, Table~\ref{tab:yearly_macro_yields} reports regional out-of-sample yield anomaly forecasts ($\bar{\hat{\epsilon}}$, bu/ac) alongside realized regional anomalies ($\bar{\epsilon}$, bu/ac) at harvest closure $H^*(s)$ across all 15 test campaigns (2011--2025), ordered from lowest to highest realized regional anomaly.

\input{tables/tab_yearly_macro_yields}

The macro-regional evaluations in Table~\ref{tab:yearly_macro_yields} reveal four empirical findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systemic Resolution of Adverse Supply Shocks:} In the defining adverse campaigns of the test era (2011--2013, 2019), candidate architectures resolve regional shortfalls that secular trends fail to anticipate. In the 2012 flash drought, regional realized yield contracted by $-4.07\text{ bu/ac}$ relative to trend, while the naive baseline assumed normal weather ($\hat{\epsilon} = 0.00\text{ bu/ac}$, incurring a $+4.07\text{ bu/ac}$ over-prediction error). ElasticNet predicts an anomaly of $-3.81\text{ bu/ac}$ ($+0.25\text{ bu/ac}$ error), while the Linear-Recurrent Ensemble predicts $-4.40\text{ bu/ac}$ ($-0.33\text{ bu/ac}$ error), capturing over 95\% of the regional supply shock. The apparent under-prediction observed at the individual county level (Decile $D_1$) is therefore an artifact of cross-sectional county grouping under $L_2$ conditional shrinkage. Under systemic drought forcing, regional aggregation cancels uncorrelated local errors and recovers the full physical shock.
    \item \textbf{Extrapolation Ceilings in Tree Ensembles versus Linear Regularization:} ElasticNet achieves the lowest forecast error in 7 of the 15 test campaigns, concentrated at distribution extremes. In record bumper years (2016 with realized anomaly $+7.34\text{ bu/ac}$ and 2021 with $+6.68\text{ bu/ac}$), tree ensembles (Random Forest and XGBoost) saturate on leaf partitions, capping anomaly predictions near $+1.0\text{--}+2.9\text{ bu/ac}$ (e.g., $+1.69\text{ bu/ac}$ and $+2.88\text{ bu/ac}$ in 2016). Because penalized linear regression operates without partitioning boundaries, ElasticNet extrapolates favorable hydrometeorological signals, predicting anomalies of $+4.09\text{ bu/ac}$ in 2016 and $+2.85\text{ bu/ac}$ in 2021.
    \item \textbf{Portfolio Error Reduction and Ensemble Champion Performance:} Across the full 15-year test period, incorporating ERA5-Land reanalysis reduces macro-regional forecast error by 40\% relative to naive trend extrapolation ($\hat{\epsilon} = 0$). The naive baseline registers $\text{RMSE} = 3.84\text{ bu/ac}$ ($\text{MAE} = 3.31\text{ bu/ac}$, $\text{Bias} = -2.41\text{ bu/ac}$). The Linear-Recurrent Ensemble attains champion accuracy with $\text{RMSE} = 2.31\text{ bu/ac}$ ($\text{MAE} = 2.04\text{ bu/ac}$, $\text{Bias} = -1.71\text{ bu/ac}$) and Pearson $r = 0.881$ (with the sequential LSTM reaching $r = 0.909$). Blending regularized linear estimation with sequential recurrent tracking dampens model-specific variance across both drought and bumper regimes.
    \item \textbf{Macro-Regional Bias Dynamics and Omitted Management Factors:} Across all test campaigns, candidate models exhibit negative forecast bias, under-projecting realized anomalies particularly during peak yield campaigns (2016, 2018, 2021, 2025). Directional bias ranges from $-1.52\text{ bu/ac}$ for ElasticNet to $-1.93\text{ bu/ac}$ for Random Forest, relative to $-2.41\text{ bu/ac}$ for the naive baseline. This residual gap reflects two structural boundaries. First, the expanding linear trend estimated on $t < Y$ adapts with a lag to recent genetic yield acceleration, leaving positive secular drift in realized test anomalies ($\overline{\epsilon} = +2.41\text{ bu/ac}$). Second, record commercial yields require joint alignment of benign weather with unobserved agronomic innovations, including improved seed genetics, seed treatments, and precision fertilizer placement. Under the strict weather-only perimeter, meteorological variables alone cannot resolve technological shifts unobserved in historical training data. ElasticNet displays the lowest directional bias ($\text{Bias} = -1.52\text{ bu/ac}$), confirming the calibration advantage of unconstrained linear regularization under favorable weather regimes.
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:res_decile_diagnostics}

The multi-horizon decile breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:decile_error_performance} and diagnostic suite in Figure~\ref{fig:decile_calibration_and_residuals} illustrate how physical observations resolve shrinkage and calibrate tail predictions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Downside Tail-Risk Mitigation and $L_2$ Shrinkage Mechanics ($D_1$):} A naive baseline assuming normal weather ($\hat{\epsilon} = 0$) incurs severe errors during acute shortfalls (realized mean $-8.82\text{ bu/ac}$, Figure~\ref{fig:decile_calibration_and_residuals}a). In $D_1$, empirical models predict $-1.30$ to $-3.08\text{ bu/ac}$ at harvest. This departure reflects Bayes-optimal $L_2$ loss behavior: because county-level $R^2 \approx 0.35$, minimizing squared error contracts point estimates toward the conditional mean $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid X] \approx 0.35 \times (-8.82) \approx -3.09\text{ bu/ac}$. Predicting the full unconstrained negative shock would incur massive quadratic loss penalties across unmodelled localized factors (hail, localized drainage). The sequential LSTM achieves the strongest tail responsiveness ($-3.08\text{ bu/ac}$ at harvest; Ensemble $-2.62\text{ bu/ac}$).
    \item \textbf{Irreducible Noise in Central Regimes ($D_3$--$D_6$):} When realized yields cluster near secular trends, empirical estimators maintain small, well-bounded departures ($|\bar{\hat{\epsilon}}| \le 0.69\text{ bu/ac}$ at $H=5$), avoiding over-reaction to benign weather fluctuations that crops tolerate without loss.
    \item \textbf{Extrapolation Ceilings in Bumper Harvests ($D_{10}$):} In record crops (realized mean $+11.01\text{ bu/ac}$), tree models encounter structural leaf saturation, underestimating peak yields ($+1.39$ to $+2.09\text{ bu/ac}$). In contrast, ElasticNet extrapolates linear gains smoothly ($+2.63\text{ bu/ac}$), with the Ensemble achieving $+2.43\text{ bu/ac}$.
    \item \textbf{Residual Distribution and Homoscedasticity (Figure~\ref{fig:decile_calibration_and_residuals}b,c):} Standardized residuals across candidate models follow the theoretical normal distribution across the $[-2.0, +2.0]\sigma$ core, with heavy empirical tails emerging only beyond $\pm 2.5\sigma$ due to unmodelled localized events (hail, flooding). Residuals for the sequential LSTM remain centered near zero across fitted values, confirming homoscedastic reliability across commercial yield regimes.
\end{enumerate}

\label{sec:res_residual_autocorrelation}
Statistical validity requires verifying that model forecast residuals do not exhibit persistent temporal autocorrelation or uncontrolled spatial clustering. We evaluate serial correlation via county-level temporal $\text{AR}(1)$ coefficients and spatial clustering via cross-sectional Moran's $I$ across the 135 study counties using inverse-distance spatial weights:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal Serial Persistence ($\text{AR}(1)$):} Across all lead times, mean temporal $\text{AR}(1)$ coefficients of forecast residuals remain negligible: for ElasticNet (+Lag), $\overline{\text{AR}(1)} = -0.054$ at $H=1$; for XGBoost (+Lag), $\overline{\text{AR}(1)} = -0.062$ at $H=1$; and for the sequential LSTM (+Lag), $\overline{\text{AR}(1)} = -0.058$ at $H=1$. Forecast residuals show no serial correlation ($\overline{\text{AR}(1)} \approx -0.06$). Conditioning on lagged yield anomaly ($\epsilon_{t-1}$) and realized weather absorbs annual persistence, leaving no unmodelled temporal structure.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Autocorrelation (Moran's $I$):} Spatial clustering of forecast errors is quantified via Moran's $I \in [-1, 1]$, where values near zero indicate spatial randomness and positive values reflect geographical clustering of similar errors across contiguous counties \citep{Sweetetal2023}. Raw historical yield anomalies exhibit a baseline spatial autocorrelation of $\overline{I} = 0.183$ across the 135-county panel, reaching peaks above $0.30$ during synchronized drought years. At early sowing horizons ($H = 6$), model forecast residuals maintain this spatial clustering ($\overline{I} \approx 0.188\text{--}0.203$). Accumulating midsummer meteorological features and continuous centroid coordinates reduces residual clustering modestly to $\overline{I} = 0.142$ for ElasticNet (+Lag) and $0.145$ for LSTM (+Lag) at $H = 1$. Residuals retain modest spatial autocorrelation ($\overline{I} \approx 0.15$), reflecting unmodelled soil and drainage differences that cluster geographically. This spatial dependence confirms that spatial block-bootstrapping is necessary for valid inference.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Feature Attribution and Agrometeorological Drivers}
\label{sec:res_feature_attribution}

\subsection{Feature Attribution Across Architectures}
\label{sec:res_shap_attribution}

We evaluate agrometeorological sensitivities across model paradigms using Grouped Permutation Feature Importance (GPFI) across each of the 19 physical indicators on the 2011--2025 out-of-sample test panel \citep{Sweetetal2023}. For each physical indicator $j$, all constituent monthly features across the full campaign ($m \in \{\text{Nov}, \dots, \text{Oct}\}$) are permuted jointly across test instances ($\pi_j$). This disrupts the biophysical link with yield anomalies while strictly preserving intra-indicator temporal autocorrelation. Predictive degradation is quantified by out-of-sample $R^2$ contraction:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(j) = \max\left(0, R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\text{base}) - R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\pi_j)\right)
    \label{eq:pfi_metric}
\end{equation}
evaluated over $N = 5$ permutation repetitions against the standardized out-of-sample denominator $SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y}_{\text{test}})^2$. 

Because baseline predictive capacity differs across candidate architectures, absolute variance losses are normalized into a Relative Information Share (\%):
\begin{equation}
    s_j = \frac{\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{19} \Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(k)} \times 100\%
    \label{eq:pfi_share}
\end{equation}
For the recurrent LSTM (+Lag), feature attribution is evaluated via an empirical surrogate tree model fitted to the sequence predictions ($\hat{\epsilon}_{\text{LSTM}}$), capturing the non-linear response surface with high fidelity while enabling uniform tabular permutation benchmarking.

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_attribution_benchmark} reports the Relative Information Share across all 19 indicators for selected integrated specifications at harvest closure horizon $H^*(s)$ (October 31; September 30 for Minnesota), grouped by physical domain.

The cross-model attribution profiles reveal clear structural consensus alongside paradigm-specific functional representations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Primacy of Atmospheric Evaporative Demand:} Atmospheric moisture demand ($VPD$) and extreme heat exceedances ($EDD_{30}$) emerge as decisive predictive drivers across all architectures. High vapor pressure deficit forces stomatal closure during flowering and pod development, arresting photosynthesis and transpirational cooling even when soil moisture is available \citep{Hoffman2020}.
    \item \textbf{Architectural Divergence in Thermal Representation:} Model families diverge in how they capture temperature regimes. ElasticNet assigns substantial linear weight to dewpoint temperature ($T_{\text{dew}}$) to capture joint temperature and humidity gradients through linear combinations. In contrast, tree ensembles isolate non-linear damage thresholds directly, prioritizing solar radiation ($SSRD$) and vapor pressure deficit while bypassing collinear temperature variables.
    \item \textbf{Hydrological Mass Balances as Biophysical Buffers:} Cumulative climatic water balance ($P - ET_0$), rainfall ($P$), and upper root-zone moisture ($SM_1$) provide the primary biophysical buffer against atmospheric drying. The recurrent LSTM allocates dominant attribution to continuous water balance deficits, capturing prolonged intra-seasonal drought sequences that suppress terminal seed filling.
\end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Alluvial Irrigation Decoupling (Missouri Bootheel):} Conversely, the lowest predictive skill ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0.06$) clusters in the Mississippi Alluvial Plain of southeastern Missouri.     \item \textbf{Sub-Threshold Thermal Regimes (Northern Fringe):} In western Minnesota, daytime temperatures rarely cross the physiological heat damage threshold ($HD_{30}$ averages less than half that of Illinois). Even after synchronizing harvest closure to September 30 ($H^*=2$), realized anomalies in 2012 remained slightly positive. Continuous spatial coordinates $(\tilde{\text{Lat}}_c, \tilde{\text{Lon}}_c)$ accommodate these regional transitions without administrative boundary fragmentation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Anomaly Space versus Raw Yield Performance}
\label{sec:res_raw_vs_anomaly}

While our empirical benchmark models detrended climatic anomalies ($\epsilon = y - \tau$) to prevent secular trend confounding, commercial supply-chain logistics evaluate physical yield levels ($y$, bu/ac). To connect anomaly predictions ($\hat{\epsilon}$) to physical yields, we project final county production through the expanding technological baseline:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_{c,Y}^{(H)} = \hat{\tau}_{c,Y} + \hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(H)}
    \label{eq:level_projection}
\end{equation}
Because the technological trend is estimated out-of-sample prior to candidate model conditioning, absolute point error metrics (RMSE and MAE) are identical between anomaly and level space ($y - \hat{y} = \epsilon - \hat{\epsilon}$). In contrast, the coefficient of determination $R^2_{\text{level}}$ scales with total realized yield variance ($\text{Var}(y) = 71.28\text{ bu}^2/\text{ac}^2$), incorporating secular technological growth and spatial productivity gradients alongside climatic variance ($\text{Var}(\epsilon) = 37.50\text{ bu}^2/\text{ac}^2$). Formulating our primary benchmark in detrended anomaly space isolates authentic meteorological skill ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.349$), verifying that our models explain interannual climatic departures beyond historical inertia.

\chapter{Empirical Results}
\label{chap:empirical_results}

Building on the econometric specifications and validation protocols formalized in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, this chapter presents the out-of-sample empirical findings of the multi-horizon forecasting framework. Evaluations execute strictly on the expanding test partition spanning harvest years 2011--2025 ($N_{\text{test}} = 15$ campaigns, $M_{\text{test}} = 2{,}025$ county-year evaluations per lead time), with model hyperparameters fixed on the 1985--1995 validation partition across all twelve forecast horizons ($H = 12 \dots 1$).

As the empirical benchmark demonstrates, cold-season weather prior to late June ($H \ge 6$) contains zero predictive skill over historical trends ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0$), with actionable meteorological skill emerging exclusively during the active growing season ($H \le 5$). Consequently, we structure the empirical analysis and reporting in two sequential tiers driven by these data findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Full-Horizon Benchmark and Noise Boundary Screening, $H = 12 \dots 1$):} A comprehensive benchmark across all candidate architectures (Naive, ElasticNet, Random Forest, XGBoost, LSTM) evaluating both pure meteorological and integrated (+lag) specifications. This sweep formally identifies the boundary where cold-season noise transitions to active agronomic signal, isolates the systematic contribution of historical carryover, and evaluates relative model performance across lead times.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2 (In-Depth Operational Analysis Across Active Horizons, $H = 5 \dots 1$):} Following the empirical finding that predictive signal concentrates in the active crop cycle, all granular diagnostic stress tests, tail-risk calibration, magnitude deconstruction, and biophysical explainability are focused on the five active campaign months where weather forcing governs crop physiology.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{equation}

\end{equation}

The empirical performance matrix in Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} reveals four distinct agrometeorological dynamics across the production cycle:

\paragraph{Pre-Sowing Dormancy Noise Regime ($H = 11 \dots 7$, Dec--Apr).}
Prior to May, observed weather provides zero statistically detectable predictive skill over historical trends ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0$). This absence of signal stems from fundamental agronomic timing: in the Midwestern Corn Belt, soybean planting takes place between late April and mid-May \citep{Hoffman2020}. Before May, fields are either frozen, snow-covered, or in early pre-sowing preparation; winter atmospheric conditions cannot interact with physiological plant tissues that do not yet exist. Furthermore, spring rainfall across the region typically recharges topsoil moisture to field capacity, resetting winter moisture anomalies before germination. Consequently, winter weather fluctuations carry no statistical correlation with harvest yield departures. In this phase, flexible non-linear architectures overfit on stochastic cold-season noise, degrading out-of-sample accuracy substantially below the naive benchmark (e.g., XGBoost registers $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = -0.19$ at $H=11$; LSTM drops between $-0.10$ and $-0.27$). Penalized linear regression (ElasticNet) remains closest to the zero baseline ($R^2 = -0.01 \dots -0.04$) by shrinking uninformative winter coefficients toward zero \citep{James2023}.

\paragraph{Sowing and Emergence Inflection ($H = 6$, May 31).}
At late spring planting, initial predictive skill begins to emerge. Ingesting seedbed moisture and early thermal pacing ($P - ET_0$, $GDD$), candidate models cross into positive territory: pure meteorological XGBoost reaches $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = +0.01$ (rising to $+0.05$ with prior-year carryover). This marks the operational inflection point where environmental observations first surpass the secular trend benchmark.

\paragraph{In-Season Skill Accumulation ($H = 5 \dots 1$, Jun--Oct).}
From late June onward, predictive accuracy expands rapidly as reproductive weather accumulates. In July ($H=4$, flowering and pod initiation), models explain between $12\%$ and $22\%$ of anomaly variance. In August ($H=3$, pod-filling), skill increases further to $R^2 = 0.22 \dots 0.32$, capturing critical reproductive stress \citep{Schlenker2009}, stabilizing as the crop approaches physiological maturity in September ($H=2$) and harvest in October ($H=1$).

\paragraph{Marginal Value of Lagged Yield Memory ($\epsilon_{t-1}$).}
Comparing the Weather-Only and Integrated blocks in Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} shows that conditioning on prior-year anomaly yields a modest, consistent improvement during the active season ($H \le 5$). For tabular architectures (ElasticNet, Random Forest, XGBoost), historical carryover increases explained variance by $+0.01$ to $+0.03$ $R^2$ points (e.g., ElasticNet rises from $0.28$ to $0.31$ at harvest; XGBoost from $0.28$ to $0.31$). For the sequential LSTM, carryover provides a larger boost at early emergence ($R^2 = 0.13$ at $H=5$) and at harvest ($R^2 = 0.35$ at $H=1$). This integrated gain operates through the linear projection head. Concatenating $\epsilon_{t-1}$ into the linear head provides an additive regularizer alongside recurrent weather states, achieving $R^2 = 0.35$. Passing historical carryover through the non-linear PReLU head induces over-parameterization on the persistent scalar, restricting integrated harvest skill to $R^2 = 0.29$. Because $\epsilon_{t-1}$ is detrended strictly on training data, this linear integration captures rotational and deep soil recharge persistence without confounding the secular technological trend \citep{ChenZhang2026}. Consequently, all subsequent operational evaluations and diagnostic stress tests deploy this top-performing integrated specification (LSTM +Lag with linear head).

\section{In-Depth Analysis of Active Forecast Horizons}
\label{sec:res_active_horizons}

\subsection{Comparative Model Performance}
\label{sec:res_champion_selection}

Because the full-horizon benchmark establishes that lead times prior to late spring ($H \ge 7$) contain zero predictive skill, we concentrate detailed econometric diagnostics strictly on the active campaign months ($H = 5 \dots 1$, June 30 to October 31). As established by the validation protocol formalized in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}, all evaluations deploy the champion model configurations selected during the validation phase: ElasticNet with mixed $\ell_1/\ell_2$ shrinkage \citep{James2023}, Random Forest ($B=300$ trees), XGBoost ($\eta=0.05$, max-depth 4), and the sequential LSTM operating on 10-day decads with temporal attention ($h=128, l=2$, linear projection head with prior-year lag) \citep{Khaki2020}.

Within this operational window, Table~\ref{tab:horizon_performance} and Figure~\ref{fig:r2_horizon_progression} provide a clear comparative evaluation between regularized linear estimation, tree ensembles, and sequential deep learning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early-Season Dominance of Regularized Linear Modeling ($H=5, 4$):} At vegetative surge ($H=5$, June 30) and flowering ($H=4$, July 31), ElasticNet achieves the highest predictive accuracy across all candidate architectures. Under pure meteorological inputs at $H=5$, ElasticNet explains $11\%$ of anomaly variance ($R^2 = 0.11$), whereas the weather-only LSTM captures only $2\%$ ($R^2 = 0.02$). When integrated with historical carryover, ElasticNet (+Lag) attains $R^2 = 0.13$ at $H=5$ and $0.22$ at $H=4$, outperforming the integrated LSTM ($0.13$ and $0.19$, respectively). In early developmental stages, when atmospheric trajectories are brief and signal-to-noise ratios remain low, linear $\ell_1/\ell_2$ shrinkage provides superior parameter stability and avoids the estimation variance inherent to complex architectures \citep{James2023}.
    \item \textbf{Late-Season Sequence Advantage ($H=3 \dots 1$):} As reproductive development unfolds, the 10-day decad LSTM with temporal attention pulls ahead, but the margin remains modest. At pod-filling ($H=3$, August 31), the integrated LSTM reaches $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.32$, versus $0.30$ for ElasticNet (+Lag) and $0.28$ for XGBoost (+Lag). At seed maturity ($H=2$, September 30), LSTM and ElasticNet perform almost identically ($0.33$ vs.\ $0.32$). At final harvest ($H=1$, October 31), LSTM (+Lag) reaches $0.35$, exceeding ElasticNet (+Lag) and XGBoost (+Lag) ($0.31$) by $4$ percentage points of explained variance. Structuring weather into 10-day decad intervals allows the recurrent network to detect short thermal pulses (such as acute heat spells during pod set) that monthly aggregations smooth out \citep{Yinetal2026}.
    \item \textbf{High Efficiency of the Linear Baseline:} Across the entire campaign, ElasticNet demonstrates high predictive stability, reaching an accuracy plateau at $R^2 = 0.31$--$0.32$ through simple penalized linear estimation. It provides closed-form convexity, minimal computational demand, zero hyperparameter instability, and complete resistance to cold-season overfitting ($R^2 = -0.01$). Tree ensembles (Random Forest and XGBoost) show no advantage over ElasticNet on monthly inputs ($0.27$--$0.31$). The recurrent architecture adds tangible value only in late reproductive stages, gaining approximately $4$ percentage points over ElasticNet at harvest while requiring complex sequence optimization and suffering from heavy winter degradation ($R^2 = -0.27$).
\end{itemize}
We retain LSTM (+Lag) alongside ElasticNet (+Lag) and XGBoost (+Lag) as reference models for subsequent tail-risk stress testing to assess whether this late-season gain provides meaningful downside protection during historical shock years.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Residual Structure and Autocorrelation}
\label{sec:res_residual_autocorrelation}

Statistical validity requires verifying that model forecast residuals do not exhibit persistent temporal autocorrelation or uncontrolled spatial clustering. We evaluate serial correlation via county-level temporal $\text{AR}(1)$ coefficients and spatial clustering via cross-sectional Moran's $I$ across the 135 study counties using inverse-distance spatial weights:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Temporal Serial Persistence ($\text{AR}(1)$):} Across all lead times, mean temporal $\text{AR}(1)$ coefficients of forecast residuals remain negligible: for ElasticNet (+Lag), $\overline{\text{AR}(1)} = -0.054$ at $H=1$; for XGBoost (+Lag), $\overline{\text{AR}(1)} = -0.062$ at $H=1$; and for the sequential LSTM (+Lag), $\overline{\text{AR}(1)} = -0.058$ at $H=1$. The absence of serial autocorrelation in forecast residuals does not contradict the predictive value of lagged yield memory ($\epsilon_{t-1}$). Historical yield carryover successfully captures rotational and deep soil persistence at the input stage \citep{ChenZhang2026}; once the model conditions on prior-year memory and realized weather, no unmodelled temporal inertia remains in the prediction errors.
    \item \textbf{Spatial Autocorrelation (Moran's $I$):} Spatial clustering of forecast errors is quantified via Moran's $I \in [-1, 1]$, where values near zero indicate spatial randomness and positive values reflect geographical clustering of similar errors across contiguous counties \citep{Sweetetal2023}. Raw historical yield anomalies exhibit a baseline spatial autocorrelation of $\overline{I} = 0.183$ across the 135-county panel, reaching peaks above $0.30$ during synchronized drought years. At early sowing horizons ($H = 6$), model forecast residuals maintain this spatial clustering ($\overline{I} \approx 0.188\text{--}0.203$). Accumulating midsummer meteorological features and continuous centroid coordinates reduces residual clustering modestly to $\overline{I} = 0.142$ for ElasticNet (+Lag) and $0.145$ for LSTM (+Lag) at $H = 1$. The persistence of a positive spatial structure ($\overline{I} \approx 0.15$) is theoretically expected: under a strictly weather-only design, unmodelled pedological drivers that cluster geographically—such as static soil moisture retention capacity, tile drainage density, and localized pest infestations—necessarily remain in the error term. This finding directly validates our methodological reliance on the complete-year spatial block-bootstrap (Section~\ref{sec:meth_significance_bootstrap}), as standard i.i.d.\ inference would produce artificially narrow confidence intervals.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Feature Attribution and Agrometeorological Drivers}
\label{sec:res_feature_attribution}

\subsection{Feature Attribution Across Architectures}
\label{sec:res_shap_attribution}

To evaluate agrometeorological sensitivities across disparate model paradigms without generating off-manifold synthetic configurations through single-cell permutations, we compute Grouped Permutation Feature Importance (GPFI) across each of the 19 physical indicators on the 2011--2025 out-of-sample test panel \citep{Sweetetal2023}. For each physical indicator $j$, all constituent monthly features across the full campaign ($m \in \{\text{Nov}, \dots, \text{Oct}\}$) are permuted jointly across test instances ($\pi_j$). This disrupts the biophysical link with yield anomalies while strictly preserving intra-indicator temporal autocorrelation. Predictive degradation is quantified by out-of-sample $R^2$ contraction:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(j) = \max\left(0, R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\text{base}) - R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\pi_j)\right)
    \label{eq:pfi_metric}
\end{equation}
evaluated over $N = 5$ permutation repetitions against the standardized out-of-sample denominator $SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y}_{\text{test}})^2$. 

Because baseline predictive capacity differs across candidate architectures, absolute variance losses are normalized into a Relative Information Share (\%):
\begin{equation}
    s_j = \frac{\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(j)}{\sum_{k=1}^{19} \Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(k)} \times 100\%
    \label{eq:pfi_share}
\end{equation}
For the recurrent LSTM (+Lag), feature attribution is evaluated via an empirical surrogate tree model fitted to the sequence predictions ($\hat{\epsilon}_{\text{LSTM}}$), capturing the non-linear response surface with high fidelity while enabling uniform tabular permutation benchmarking.

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_attribution_benchmark} reports the Relative Information Share across all 19 indicators for selected integrated specifications at harvest lead time ($H = 1$, October 31), grouped by physical domain in their natural order without artificial sorting.

The cross-model attribution profiles reveal clear structural consensus alongside paradigm-specific functional representations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Primacy of Atmospheric Evaporative Demand:} Vapor pressure deficit ($VPD$) emerges as a critical driver across all architectures, accounting for $17.7\%$ of total predictive degradation in XGBoost, $11.3\%$ in LSTM, and $11.4\%$ in ElasticNet. Extreme degree days above $30^\circ\text{C}$ ($EDD_{30}$) contribute an additional $11.7\%$ in ElasticNet and $4.5\%$ in LSTM, confirming that atmospheric desiccating stress governs terminal yield penalties.
    \item \textbf{Architectural Divergence in Thermal Representation:} ElasticNet allocates substantial linear weight to dewpoint temperature ($T_{\text{dew}}$, $31.7\%$), reflecting collinear pacing of vapor pressure and ambient temperatures. In contrast, non-linear tree partitions bypass collinear thermal indices, allocating substantial weight directly to solar radiation ($SSRD$, $9.0\%$) and vapor pressure deficit ($17.7\%$).
    \item \textbf{Dominance of Hydrological Supply Balances:} The climatic water balance ($P - ET_0$) dominates predictive attribution in recurrent LSTM ($26.9\%$) and XGBoost ($6.9\%$). Combined with cumulative precipitation ($P$, $17.4\%$ in LSTM, $4.4\%$ in ElasticNet, and $2.8\%$ in XGBoost) and topsoil moisture ($SM_1$, $4.5\text{--}10.8\%$), water balance indicators form the foundational biophysical buffer mitigating drought collapse.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Macro-Domain Feature Sensitivity}
\label{sec:res_cluster_pfi}

To quantify the gross variance loss induced by completely removing coherent physical information families, we evaluate Grouped Permutation Feature Importance (GPFI) across five aggregated macro-agronomic domains \citep{Sweetetal2023}. For each physical domain, all constituent feature columns across all campaign months are permuted jointly, preserving intra-domain correlation manifolds while breaking the link with yield anomalies. Feature importance is defined as the marginal contraction in out-of-sample explained variance:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\mathcal{C}) = \max\left(0, R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\text{base}) - R^2_{\text{OOS}}(\pi_{\mathcal{C}})\right)
    \label{eq:cluster_pfi}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{\mathcal{C}}$ denotes joint row permutation of all columns in domain $\mathcal{C}$, evaluated against the standardized out-of-sample denominator $SS_{\text{tot}} = \sum (y_i - \bar{y}_{\text{test}})^2$. Figure~\ref{fig:shap_horizon_stack} compares the relative macro-domain information share (100\% horizontal stacked bars) across ElasticNet (+Lag), XGBoost (+Lag), and LSTM (Surrogate) at $H = 1$ over $n = 10$ permutation repetitions, matching the domain color coding and architectural sequencing of Figure~\ref{fig:feature_attribution_benchmark}.

The five-domain grouped perturbation reveals a pronounced structural dichotomy across architectural paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-Linear Representation of Atmospheric Demand \& Heat:} For gradient boosted trees (XGBoost), the Atmospheric Demand \& Heat domain ($HD_{35}$, $HD_{30}$, $EDD_{30}$, $VPD$, $ET_0$) constitutes the primary explanatory family, capturing $32.4\%$ of predictive share ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.102$). In ElasticNet, this share is $24.0\%$ ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.261$), while in recurrent LSTM it represents $22.1\%$ ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.057$). This disparity aligns with the mathematical structure of evaporative deficit: $VPD$ scales non-linearly via the exponential Clausius-Clapeyron relation, while $EDD_{30}$ operates as a piecewise linear threshold \citep{Schlenker2009}. Recursive tree partitions capture threshold cutoffs and compound heat-moisture interactions, which an additive linear hyperplane cannot isolate without explicit polynomial expansions.
    \item \textbf{Linear Pacing in Thermal \& Radiation Regimes:} Symmetrically, Thermal \& Radiation Regimes ($GDD$, $T_{\text{mean}}$, $T_{\text{max}}$, $T_{\text{min}}$, $T_{\text{dew}}$, $SSRD$) contribute $30.8\%$ in ElasticNet ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.335$), $31.8\%$ in XGBoost ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.101$), and $28.9\%$ in LSTM ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.075$). In linear regression, continuous thermal covariates provide smooth, season-long developmental pacing across collinear temperature series (principally $T_{\text{dew}}$, which accounts for $31.7\%$ in Figure~\ref{fig:feature_attribution_benchmark}). Non-linear architectures do not require continuous temperature proxies to detect heat anomalies, prioritizing discrete threshold excursions directly.
    \item \textbf{Volumetric Baseline of Hydrological Supply:} In contrast to the divergence observed across thermal and atmospheric domains, the remaining physical families exhibit structural cross-paradigm stability. Direct meteo-hydrological balances ($P, P-ET_0$) represent a primary informational pillar across all specifications, accounting for $40.0\%$ in recurrent LSTM ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.104$), $27.5\%$ in ElasticNet ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.299$), and $18.9\%$ in XGBoost ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.060$). Meteorological water supply establishes a necessary mass-balance condition for rainfed production, imposing an invariant empirical lower bound across both linear and non-linear learners.
    \item \textbf{Cross-Paradigm Stability in Soil Moisture Dynamics:} Root-zone and subsoil moisture reservoirs ($SM_{1\text{--}3}, SM_{\text{root}}$) contribute a stable, proportional share across all three models: $14.5\%$ in ElasticNet ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.157$), $12.1\%$ in XGBoost ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.038$), and $8.4\%$ in LSTM ($\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.022$). Regardless of inductive bias, subsoil moisture buffers crop transpirational demand during rainless spells; all architectures allocate roughly one-twelfth to one-seventh of total attribution to this subterranean reservoir.
    \item \textbf{Contemporaneous Weather Primacy over Memory and Geography:} Shuffling lagged yield anomalies ($\epsilon_{t-1}$) and spatial coordinates accounts for minor shares across all models ($4.8\%$ in XGBoost, $3.3\%$ in ElasticNet, and $0.7\%$ in LSTM; $\Delta R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0.036$). At milestone horizon $H = 1$, realized in-season weather dominates historical carryover and static county geography.
\end{enumerate}

In this alluvial corridor, commercial growers irrigate over 60--75\% of harvested soybean acreage via groundwater extraction. Because our empirical protocol is strictly weather-only and omits operational pumping records, supplemental irrigation uncouples crop physiological performance from ERA5-Land precipitation deficits. Consequently, the model anticipates drought damage that field irrigation mitigates, attenuating out-of-sample skill.
    \item \textbf{Sub-Threshold Thermal Regimes (Northern Fringe):} In the northern fringe counties of western Minnesota (e.g., Stevens County, $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = -0.722$; Big Stone County, $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = -0.243$), July daytime temperatures rarely cross the physiological heat damage threshold ($HD_{30} \approx 6.9\text{ days}$, compared to $14.8\text{ days}$ in Illinois). During the 2012 drought, realized Minnesota anomalies remained positive ($\bar{\epsilon}_{2012} = +0.14\text{ bu/ac}$). Absent acute reproductive heat shocks, annual yield variance is dominated by operational planting delays, soil temperature at emergence, and early autumn frost dates, which monthly summer weather aggregations do not capture.
    \item \textbf{Artificial Subsurface Drainage (Western Lake Erie Basin):} In northwestern Ohio (Ottawa and Henry counties, $R^2_{\text{OOS}} \approx 0.02\text{--}0.15$), dense subsurface tile drainage networks across lacustrine clay soils accelerate artificial soil water evacuation. Artificial drainage alters moisture retention curves relative to standardized ERA5-Land soil pedotransfer functions, while thermal moderation from Lake Erie dampens synoptic heat extremes. Continuous spatial coordinates $(\tilde{\text{Lat}}_c, \tilde{\text{Lon}}_c)$ capture these broad agro-ecological transitions without administrative boundary fragmentation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Anomaly Space versus Raw Yield Performance}
\label{sec:res_raw_vs_anomaly}

The out-of-sample skill scores reported throughout this chapter quantify the proportion of climatic anomaly variance explained relative to county-specific secular trends ($\epsilon = y - \tau$). In commercial supply-chain logistics and grain elevator operations, decision-makers ultimately evaluate production in physical yield levels ($y$, bu/ac). To connect anomaly predictions ($\hat{\epsilon}$) to physical yields, we project final county production through the expanding technological baseline:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_{c,Y}^{(H)} = \hat{\tau}_{c,Y} + \hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(H)}
    \label{eq:level_projection}
\end{equation}
Because the technological trend is estimated out-of-sample prior to candidate model conditioning, the absolute forecast error on raw yield levels is identical to the anomaly forecast error:
\begin{equation}
    y_{c,Y} - \hat{y}_{c,Y}^{(H)} = \left(\tau_{c,Y} + \epsilon_{c,Y}\right) - \left(\hat{\tau}_{c,Y} + \hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(H)}\right) = \epsilon_{c,Y} - \hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(H)}
    \label{eq:level_error_equivalence}
\end{equation}
Consequently, point error metrics (RMSE and MAE) are invariant between anomaly space and physical yield space. In contrast, the coefficient of determination $R^2$ scales with the denominator baseline variance:
\begin{equation}
    R^2_{\text{level}}(H) = 1 - \frac{\sum_{Y} \sum_{c} \left( y_{c,Y} - \hat{y}_{c,Y}^{(H)} \right)^2}{\sum_{Y} \sum_{c} \left( y_{c,Y} - \bar{y}_{\text{test}} \right)^2}
    \label{eq:r2_level_formula}
\end{equation}
While the anomaly denominator captures only climatic variance ($\text{Var}(\epsilon) = 37.50\text{ bu}^2/\text{ac}^2$), the level denominator incorporates the full variance of realized production, encompassing historical yield growth and cross-county spatial productivity gradients ($\text{Var}(y) = 71.28\text{ bu}^2/\text{ac}^2$). \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Secular Baseline Absorption:} The naive secular trend accounts for $47.4\%$ of total yield variance in the test partition ($R^2_{\text{level}} = 0.474$, $r = 0.746$) without ingesting meteorological data. This reflects multidecadal agronomic breeding, fertilizer efficiency, and baseline geographic differences across counties.
    \item \textbf{Meteorological Value Added:} Conditioning on ERA5-Land reanalysis and sequential memory increases explained variance from $0.474$ to $\mathbf{0.657}$ for the sequential LSTM (+Lag), generating a Pearson correlation of $r = 0.837$. Environmental forcing resolves an additional $+18.3$ percentage points of commercial yield variation beyond historical technological momentum.
    \item \textbf{County-Level Peak Resolution:} At the county level, where secular trend accuracy varies, raw yield level coefficients reach $\text{median } R^2_{\text{level}} = 0.435$ and peak above $0.75\text{--}0.85$ in core rainfed districts (e.g., Buchanan County, IA: $R^2_{\text{level}} = 0.751$, $r = 0.872$, $\text{RMSE} = 2.65\text{ bu/ac}$).

Un-detrended estimators attribute secular technological progress to climate predictors \citep{Khaki2020}. Formulating the primary benchmark in detrended anomaly space isolates authentic meteorological skill ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.349$), demonstrating that our models predict unpredictable climatic shocks beyond historical inertia.
\end{enumerate}

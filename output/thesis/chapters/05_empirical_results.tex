\chapter{Empirical Results}
\label{chap:empirical_results}

This chapter presents the out-of-sample empirical findings of the progressive multi-horizon forecasting framework formalized in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}. All results are evaluated strictly on the blind test partition spanning harvest years 1996--2025 ($N_{\text{test}} = 30$ campaigns, $M_{\text{test}} = 4{,}050$ county-year evaluations per lead time), using models whose hyperparameters were fixed on the 1985--1995 validation partition. We organize the evidence around four questions: (i) at what lead time does observed weather generate statistically significant skill over the secular trend; (ii) whether algorithmic complexity provides genuine out-of-sample advantages; (iii) whether continuous regression predictions anticipate extreme shock years; and (iv) which agrometeorological signals drive predictive performance across growth stages.

\subsection{Naive Secular Trend Benchmark and the Zero-Weather Reference}
\label{sec:res_naive_baseline}

At Horizon $H = 12$ (forecast origin: end of November $Y-1$), all candidate models receive zero meteorological inputs ($P_{\text{met}} = 0$) and output $\hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(12)} = 0$ by construction. The resulting out-of-sample error equals the sample standard deviation of the test-period yield anomaly, establishing the reference loss:
\begin{equation}
    \text{RMSE}_{\text{naive}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M_{\text{test}}} \sum_{Y=1996}^{2025} \sum_{c=1}^C \epsilon_{c,Y}^2}, \quad R^2_{\text{OOS}}(H=12) = 0
    \label{eq:rmse_naive_reference}
\end{equation}
This benchmark quantifies the irreducible anomaly variability that meteorological information must explain to register positive skill. \subsection{Lead-Time Skill Emergence}
\label{sec:res_skill_emergence}

The central empirical question is the lead time at which observed weather first delivers statistically significant positive skill over the naive secular trend ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} > 0$, $\text{Skill}_{\text{RMSE}} > 0$). We define the \textit{skill emergence horizon} $H^*$ as the earliest horizon where candidate models achieve $R^2_{\text{OOS}} > 0$ and the Diebold-Mariano test rejects the null of equal accuracy at the 5\% significance level.

The empirical trajectory reveals a distinct two-stage emergence profile. Across early antecedent horizons ($H \in \{11, \dots, 7\}$, December $Y-1$ through April $Y$), meteorological variables contain no actionable signal for end-of-season yield anomalies: tree ensembles and regularized estimators yield negative out-of-sample $R^2_{\text{OOS}}$, making the naive secular trend the optimal predictor. Operational skill emergence begins at the onset of vegetative growth ($H = 6$, May 31), where XGBoost crosses into positive territory ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = +0.023$, $\text{Skill}_{\text{RMSE}} = +1.2\%$), capturing initial soil thermal conditions and planting moisture.

As the reproductive phase unfolds, predictive skill accelerates sharply. Ingesting June and July weather ($H = 4$, July 31, R1--R3 flowering) expands explained variance to $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.116$. Incorporating August conditions ($H = 3$, August 31, R4--R6 pod-filling) yields a major leap to $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.193$. Peak accuracy across the entire season is achieved at Horizon $H = 2$ (September 30) by ElasticNet, reaching $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.213$ ($\text{Skill}_{\text{RMSE}} = +11.3\%$, $\text{RMSE} = 5.27\text{ bu/ac}$), while XGBoost achieves the highest accuracy at final harvest ($H = 1$, $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.205$, $\text{Skill}_{\text{RMSE}} = +10.8\%$, $\text{RMSE} = 5.30\text{ bu/ac}$). Figure~\ref{fig:r2_horizon_progression} plots this trajectory across all models and lead times.

Near-harvest performance establishes the ceiling of weather-based predictive accuracy under this monthly temporal resolution.

\subsection{Regularized Linear Model Performance}
\label{sec:res_elasticnet}

ElasticNet ($L_1 + L_2$ penalization, Eq.~\eqref{eq:elasticnet_objective}) operates as the primary linear benchmark against which non-linear architectures demonstrate incremental skill. Its $L_1$ sparsity component applies implicit feature selection across the expanding $P_H$-dimensional predictor space, concentrating regression mass on dominant agrometeorological channels while zeroing out noise-dominated predictors. The $L_2$ component groups correlated flux integrals (e.g., $GDD$, $HD_{30}$, $HD_{35}$) into stable coefficient clusters, preventing collinearity-induced variance inflation as the feature dimension grows from $P_{11} = 34$ to $P_1 = 194$. 

Across late horizons, ElasticNet delivers robust out-of-sample accuracy, peaking at $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.213$ ($\text{RMSE} = 5.27\text{ bu/ac}$) at Horizon $H = 2$ and maintaining $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.193$ at harvest ($H = 1$). The convex loss surface and coordinate descent solver provide complete numerical stability across all 30 expanding test folds.

\subsection{Tree Ensemble Performance: Random Forest and XGBoost}
\label{sec:res_tree_ensembles}

Non-linear tree ensembles capture threshold responses and compound hydrothermal interactions without pre-specifying multiplicative interaction terms. Random Forest reduces prediction variance via bootstrap aggregation across $B = 150$ randomized decision trees (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rf_prediction}). While Random Forest delivers positive skill from $H = 6$ through $H = 1$ (reaching $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.169$ at $H = 2$ and $0.162$ at $H = 1$), its orthogonal axis-aligned splits on correlated weather inputs result in conservative predictions that underperform both ElasticNet and XGBoost.

XGBoost learns sequential residual corrections via additive gradient boosting with explicit regularizations (Eq.~\eqref{eq:xgboost_objective}). Incorporating $L_1$ leaf penalties ($\alpha_{\text{reg}} = 0.5$), stochastic row subsampling ($0.8$), and minimum child weight thresholding suppresses over-fitting on multicollinear monthly blocks. The expanding-window validation grid search systematically selects this regularized specification, enabling XGBoost to achieve the highest accuracy among all models at harvest ($H = 1$, $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.205$, $\text{RMSE} = 5.30\text{ bu/ac}$). Diebold-Mariano tests confirm that both XGBoost and ElasticNet significantly surpass the naive secular benchmark ($p < 0.0001$).

\subsection{Architectural Trade-offs and Model Parsimony}
\label{sec:res_parsimony}

The comparative findings illuminate the value of model parsimony in regional crop forecasting. Pre-aggregating hourly ERA5-Land reanalysis into physically motivated agrometeorological integrals ($GDD, HD_{35}, VPD, ET_0$) embeds domain knowledge directly into the design matrix. Consequently, parsimonious linear models with convex penalties and regularized gradient boosted trees attain the empirical performance frontier ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \approx 0.205$--$0.213$). Complex over-parameterized architectures requiring massive sample-to-parameter ratios provide no structural advantage over well-regularized tabular estimators on monthly aggregated weather blocks.

\subsection{Mean Shrinkage Bias at the Tails}
\label{sec:res_shrinkage_bias}

A systematic risk in regression estimators trained under squared error loss on near-Gaussian anomaly distributions is conditional shrinkage toward the historical mean. When the population anomaly is substantially negative (e.g., 2012 flash drought, $-11.7\%$), penalized models that minimize average quadratic loss may underestimate the magnitude of the shock, outputting $|\hat{\epsilon}| < |\epsilon|$. This conditional bias directly degrades supply-chain decision value, precisely in the scenarios most economically relevant.

We quantify mean shrinkage bias as $\bar{d}^{(\text{shock})}(H) = \frac{1}{C} \sum_c (\hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y} - \epsilon_{c,Y})$ within each shock cohort year $Y$. Positive bias in adverse years (model over-predicts relative to the shock) and negative bias in bumper years indicate conditional shrinkage. Figure~\ref{fig:shock_bias_profiles} plots cohort-specific bias profiles across horizons for the best-performing model, overlaid against the naive baseline's constant zero-prediction reference.

\subsection{The 2012 Flash Drought as Out-of-Sample Benchmark}
\label{sec:res_2012_benchmark}

The 2012 flash drought represents the most demanding single-year test case within the 1996--2025 blind test partition. At the Corn Belt scale, temperatures during June--August 2012 reached +2.1 standard deviations above the 1981--2010 climatological mean, while July 2012 precipitation fell to 42\% of the long-run average across the six study states. As documented in Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_shock_comparison}, the spatial footprint of the 2012 drought was widespread, covering Iowa, Indiana, and Missouri with uniformly severe heat stress indicators ($HD_{35}$, $VPD$) concentrated in the critical pod-set window.

The 2012 test fold is constructed from a 61-year training pool (1951--2011, $N_{\text{train}} = 8{,}235$ county-years) containing the 1988 drought as the closest historical analogue. Models trained with 1988 weather patterns must extrapolate predictions to 2012 conditions under structural distributional shift. Comparing prediction quality at sequential horizons maps the precise point in the 2012 growing season when anomalous temperature and moisture trajectories generate discriminatory signal.

\section{Feature Attribution and Agrometeorological Drivers}
\label{sec:res_feature_attribution}

\subsection{SHAP Value Decomposition}
\label{sec:res_shap}

To move beyond aggregate accuracy metrics toward mechanistic interpretation, we apply SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) decomposition on the best-performing tabular ensemble model. For each test observation $(c, Y)$, the prediction $\hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(H)}$ decomposes as:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\epsilon}_{c,Y}^{(H)} = \phi_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{P_H} \phi_k(c, Y)
    \label{eq:shap_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_0$ is the base (mean) prediction across the training pool, and $\phi_k(c, Y)$ is the marginal contribution of feature $k$ to the deviation from the base prediction for observation $(c, Y)$. SHAP values satisfy efficiency ($\sum_k \phi_k = \hat{\epsilon} - \phi_0$), symmetry, and the null-player axiom \citep{Sharmaetal2025}.

\subsection{Phenological Driver Mapping}
\label{sec:res_phenological_drivers}

Figure~\ref{fig:shap_beeswarm} displays the top 20 agrometeorological predictors ordered by mean absolute SHAP value for XGBoost at Horizon $H = 2$ (September 30), where model accuracy achieves its empirical maximum. \section{Hypothesis Synthesis}
\label{sec:res_hypothesis_synthesis}

We revisit the five research hypotheses formalized in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review} against the empirical evidence established across Sections~\ref{sec:res_horizon_progression}--\ref{sec:res_spatial_performance}.

\textbf{H1 (Incremental weather skill):} The null hypothesis of zero incremental meteorological value over the secular trend ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0$ for all $H < 12$) is decisively \textbf{rejected} ($p < 0.0001$). The skill emergence horizon is identified at $H^* = 6$ (May 31), where candidate models achieve positive skill that expands monotonically to a peak of $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.213$ ($\text{Skill}_{\text{RMSE}} = +11.3\%$) at $H = 2$ (September 30). Diebold-Mariano tests confirm that weather-augmented architectures significantly outperform the naive secular baseline at the 0.1\% significance level ($p < 0.0001$).

\textbf{H2 (Monotone lead-time dependence):} The hypothesis of monotonic skill expansion as the growing season unfolds is \textbf{supported}. Forecast error declines systematically from $\text{RMSE} = 5.94\text{ bu/ac}$ at $H = 12$ to $5.27\text{ bu/ac}$ at $H = 2$. Early overwinter horizons ($H \ge 7$) show no skill ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0$), indicating that genuine predictive signal is concentrated in the summer phenological window (July--August pod-set), after which accuracy saturates at physiological maturity.

\textbf{H3 (Model complexity value):} The hypothesis that non-linear algorithmic complexity provides uniform advantages over parsimonious linear models is \textbf{partially supported / nuanced}. At the aggregate panel level, ElasticNet ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.213$ at $H = 2$, $0.193$ at $H = 1$) matches gradient boosted trees (XGBoost $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.198$ at $H = 2$, $0.205$ at $H = 1$) and outperforms Random Forest ($0.162$). However, XGBoost demonstrates superior capabilities in resolving severe tail-risk events, outperforming ElasticNet during the 2012 flash drought at harvest ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.387$ vs.\ $0.292$).

\textbf{H4 (Monthly aggregation sufficiency):} Monthly block aggregation ($K = 16$ channels, $P_H \le 194$) is \textbf{supported} as an optimal parsimonious representation for tabular estimators. Ingesting monthly sums of flux integrals and means of state variables captures over 21\% of county anomaly variance while preventing the multicollinearity collapse associated with sub-monthly pentad expansions ($P \ge 1{,}170$).

\textbf{H5 (Tail-risk shock anticipation):} The hypothesis that regression models anticipate extreme climate shocks is \textbf{supported}. During the benchmark 2012 flash drought, candidate models identify severe negative anomalies as early as July 31 ($H = 4$, $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.358$, bias $+0.69\text{ bu/ac}$), successfully overcoming mean shrinkage and resolving the directional shortfall before harvest ($H = 1$, $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.387$, bias $+1.08\text{ bu/ac}$).

\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:literature_review}

\section{Introduction and Organization of the Literature}
\label{sec:lit_structure}

Predicting crop yields prior to harvest is a foundational challenge in agricultural economics and applied machine learning. Over decades of empirical work, researchers have investigated how weather variability governs county-level crop production across the United States Corn Belt. Classical econometric models \citep{Schlenker2009, Hoffman2020} established essential insights: raw yields are dominated by secular technological progress, heat damage exhibits sharp non-linear breakpoints, and reproductive stages suffer disproportionate sensitivity to moisture deficits. Recently, high-resolution reanalyses and flexible predictive algorithms have shifted attention toward intra-seasonal, multi-horizon forecasting frameworks \citep{ChenZhang2026, Vijverberg2023, Yinetal2026, Khaki2020}.

This review synthesizes the agronomic foundations of soybean weather sensitivity, evaluates key design dimensions in empirical crop forecasting, surveys candidate statistical and machine learning architectures, and reviews primary benchmark studies to derive five formal research hypotheses (H1--H5).

\section{Technological Trends, Weather Variability, and Soybean Agronomic Sensitivity}
\label{sec:lit_yield_trend_weather}

In agricultural economics, crop yield ($Y_{it}$, bushels per acre) serves as the primary metric of biological land productivity. Across multidecadal horizons, developed agricultural systems exhibit strong upward trajectories: in the U.S. Corn Belt, average soybean yields more than doubled from approximately 20 bushels per acre in the early 1950s to over 50 bushels per acre today, driven by cumulative genetic improvements, chemical inputs, mechanization, and precision farming. Consequently, historical yield series $Y_{it}$ decompose into two additive components:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = T_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:yield_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $T_{it}$ represents the smooth technological trend capturing non-meteorological advancements, and $\varepsilon_{it}$ denotes the interannual yield anomaly driven by within-season weather variability, transient pest pressures, and environmental shocks.

Failing to account for $T_{it}$ introduces severe statistical pathologies. Because atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and temperatures have trended upward over the same multidecadal period, estimating models directly on raw yields risks confounding biological weather sensitivity with secular technological growth \citep{Schlenker2009}. A model estimated on raw yields may spuriously attribute genetic gains to rising temperatures, distorting both predictive accuracy and feature attributions. Isolating the weather-driven anomaly $\varepsilon_{it}$ is therefore essential for characterizing meteorological impacts.

Soybean is an annual legume whose physiological development and final seed production respond non-linearly to prevailing meteorological conditions, governed by acute extremes and phenological exposure timing:

\subsection{Growing Degree Days and Thermal Time}
The relationship between ambient temperature and crop development is non-linear. Within moderate ranges, temperature accelerates vegetative growth, leaf expansion, and developmental progression. These cumulative thermal requirements are represented via Growing Degree Days (GDD), accumulated between a base temperature ($10^\circ\text{C}$) and an upper threshold ($30^\circ\text{C}$). \citet{Hoffman2020} demonstrate that tracking thermal time provides a biologically grounded framework for identifying developmental milestones, capturing yield-limiting stress far more effectively than standard calendar time.

\subsection{Non-Linear Temperature Breakpoints and Thermal Damage}
When temperatures exceed critical thresholds, thermal damage accelerates rapidly. In a seminal econometric analysis, \citet{Schlenker2009} demonstrated sharp non-linear breakpoints: while temperatures up to $29^\circ\text{C}$ or $30^\circ\text{C}$ are beneficial, temperatures exceeding this threshold inflict severe yield penalties via floral abscission, pollen sterility, stomatal closure, and impaired photosynthesis.

To capture these damage mechanisms, researchers construct Extreme Degree Days (EDD), accumulating thermal exposure strictly above physiological damage thresholds ($>30^\circ\text{C}$). Coarse monthly temperature averages smooth out these excursions, masking heatwaves lasting only a few consecutive days. Both \citet{Hoffman2020} and \citet{ChenZhang2026} identify indicators of extreme summer heat as among the most decisive predictors of interannual yield variance.

\subsection{Atmospheric Moisture Demand and Soil Water Balance}
While rainfall replenishes soil moisture, crop water stress is governed by the joint interaction between soil water availability and atmospheric evaporative demand, characterized by Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD). \citet{Hoffman2020} identify elevated VPD as a critical driver of yield reduction during reproductive growth: high VPD forces stomatal closure to prevent desiccation, halting transpirational cooling and carbon fixation even when soil moisture is present.

Regarding the surface water budget, empirical studies emphasize compounding interactions between precipitation, root-zone soil moisture, and atmospheric demand \citep{ChenZhang2026, Hoffman2020, Vijverberg2023}. We parameterize the surface climatic water balance via the difference between accumulated precipitation and reference evapotranspiration ($P - \text{ET}_0$), calculated following standard FAO-56 Penman-Monteith formulations.

\subsection{Phenological Vulnerability Windows}
The biological impact of meteorological shocks depends on crop phenology. Soybeans exhibit substantial compensatory plasticity during early vegetative growth (V1--V$n$); moderate moisture deficits or temperature anomalies can often be overcome if favorable weather resumes before flowering \citep{Hoffman2020}.

Conversely, mid-season reproductive phases---specifically pod development (R3--R4) and seed filling (R5--R6), occurring between mid-July and late August in the U.S. Corn Belt---represent the critical yield-determining window \citep{Hoffman2020, Yinetal2026}. Severe moisture deficits or heatwaves during pod filling directly reduce pod counts, seed numbers, and seed weight, causing irreversible yield losses. Meteorological conditions proximate to harvest (September and October) primarily govern field machinery trafficability and grain dry-down, with negligible influence on biological seed mass.

\section{Empirical Design Dimensions: Lead Times, Target Formulations, and Baseline Benchmarking}
\label{sec:lit_targets_lead_times}

\subsection{Forecasting Horizons and In-Season Updating}
In classical econometric modeling, studies predominantly evaluate crop responses using post-harvest weather series aggregated across the complete growing season \citep{Schlenker2009}. While indispensable for retrospective structural estimation, such post-harvest models provide limited advance notice for operational decision-makers in agricultural supply chains. 

Recent forecasting literature has recognized that predictive utility depends directly on forecast lead time, shifting toward progressive in-season updating. For instance, \citet{ChenZhang2026} evaluate monthly models from May through August, demonstrating that explained variance increases progressively as reproductive weather unfolds. \citet{Khaki2020} implement weekly in-season updates, tracking continuous error reductions through harvest. At earlier horizons, \citet{Vijverberg2023} demonstrate that antecedent land and ocean states provide predictive signals up to 50 days prior to planting. Evaluating models across sequential horizons ($H=12, \dots, 1$) allows us to map precisely when atmospheric observations transition from uninformative noise into reliable forecasting signals.

\subsection{Target Formulations: Continuous Yields and Downside Risk Anticipation}
The predominant paradigm in crop modeling focuses on continuous regression of yield levels or anomalies, evaluated via standard quadratic metrics such as Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and $R^2$ \citep{ChenZhang2026}. Continuous point forecasts provide granular estimates essential for regional supply balancing and commercial volume planning.

In operational risk management, however, decision-makers face asymmetric exposure to acute supply shortfalls. For grain elevator operators, processors, and insurers, anticipating extreme adverse yield shocks is the primary operational objective \citep{Vijverberg2023, Iizumietal2021}. Evaluating continuous regression models specifically on tail-risk diagnostics—such as error contraction on the bottom yield decile ($D_1$) and directional sign accuracy—bridges point forecasting with practical tail-risk anticipation without imposing arbitrary discretization boundaries.

\subsection{Reference Baselines and Benchmark Anchoring}
In empirical forecasting, evaluating a model's true capability requires benchmarking against parsimonious reference baselines: historical county climatology, deterministic linear trends, or lagged past yields ($Y-1$). 

In trending agricultural panels, baseline models capture the substantial variance attributable to secular technological momentum and permanent cross-sectional productivity differences across counties. A weather-driven model must demonstrate genuine incremental skill over these reference baselines to verify that meteorological inputs provide actionable predictive value beyond what is already explained by historical inertia. For example, in ablation experiments by \citet{ChenZhang2026}, incorporating lagged yield ($Y-1$) accounted for approximately half of the model's total explained variance, highlighting the essential role of baseline decoupling when isolating pure weather sensitivity.

\section{Statistical, Machine Learning, and Sequential Model Architectures}
\label{sec:lit_model_families}

The literature features three prominent model families for tabular and sequential crop forecasting:

\paragraph{Regularized Linear Models.} Ridge and Lasso regressions provide transparent, computationally efficient baselines that mitigate multicollinearity among highly correlated meteorological features, serving as robust benchmarks against which more complex non-linear architectures can be judged.

\paragraph{Tree-Based Ensembles.} Random Forests and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost) represent predominant workhorses for tabular agro-meteorology \citep{Sharmaetal2025}. They naturally accommodate step-function damage thresholds (such as non-linear heat stress above $30^\circ\text{C}$) and multi-way feature interactions without restrictive parametric assumptions, while facilitating post-hoc feature attribution via TreeSHAP \citep{ChenZhang2026}.

\paragraph{Sequential Deep Learning.} Recurrent architectures, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, are designed to process time-series sequences of meteorological inputs, dynamically capturing stress duration, dry spell persistence, and intra-seasonal shock timing \citep{Khaki2020}.

\paragraph{Temporal Granularity in Feature Representation.} Beyond model architecture, the temporal granularity of meteorological features represents a critical design choice. While traditional benchmarks rely on monthly averages \citep{ChenZhang2026}, sub-monthly aggregations (such as 5-day and 10-day windows) preserve acute, short-duration weather extremes that monthly means smooth out \citep{Yinetal2026}.

\section{Approaches to Unobserved Future Weather at Forecast Origin}
\label{sec:lit_future_weather}

At any intermediate forecast origin ($H > 1$), meteorological conditions between the cutoff date and final harvest remain unknown. In the literature, three primary approaches address this missing future information:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Future-Weather Truncation:} Conditioning predictions strictly on meteorological features observed up to the cutoff date, training horizon-specific models exclusively on cumulative observations (e.g., \citealt{ChenZhang2026} for monthly cutoffs; \citealt{Khaki2020} for weekly updates; \citealt{Yinetal2026}). This approach directly isolates the genuine predictive information delivered by realized weather up to the forecast date without confounding model skill with downstream weather assumptions.
    \item \textbf{Coupling with Dynamical Seasonal Climate Forecasts:} Driving crop models with numerical seasonal weather forecasts (e.g., ECMWF SEAS5 in \citealt{Ceglar2021}; multi-model seasonal hindcasts in \citealt{Iizumietal2021}). While providing complete-season weather projections, this approach couples crop-model performance with the skill limitations and spatial biases of long-range seasonal climate predictions.
    \item \textbf{Climatological Completion or Synthetic Weather Generation:} Imputing unobserved future weeks using long-term historical daily averages or synthetic weather generators. Historically common in process-based biophysical crop modeling (e.g., DSSAT, APSIM), this method completes the calendar sequence but introduces artificial smoothing and assumes climatologically average future conditions.
\end{enumerate}

Zero-future-weather truncation provides the cleanest empirical framework for tracking the progressive emergence of weather-driven predictive skill, as it directly evaluates the unconfounded value of historical atmospheric observations available at the time of forecast formulation.

\section{Benchmark Studies and Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:lit_closest_studies}

To position our empirical framework, we examine primary benchmarks in multi-lead-time county crop forecasting across the United States Corn Belt.

\subsection{Chen \& Zhang (2026): Multidecadal County-Level Deep Learning Benchmark}
\citet{ChenZhang2026} compile a 99-year panel (1927--2025) across 1,309 U.S. counties, combining USDA NASS soybean yields with 31 monthly weather variables and static SSURGO soil features. They train a Deep Neural Network and a Random Forest, evaluating monthly forecasts from May through August. On holdout test campaigns (2024--2025), end-of-season $R^2$ reaches 0.67--0.68, with August extreme heat days ($>32^\circ\text{C}$) driving yield reductions. Omitting lagged yield ($Y-1$) contracts test $R^2$ to $\approx 0.33$, showing that historical baseline persistence accounts for half of the explained variance in raw yield levels.

While \citet{ChenZhang2026} validate long historical panels, they predict raw yield levels using monthly station observations across four in-season months. We extend their foundation by ingesting continuous ERA5-Land reanalysis, enforcing train-only detrending, and expanding lead times across a twelve-month continuum ($H=12 \dots 1$).

\subsection{Vijverberg et al. (2023): Presowing Precursors and Extreme Event Classification}
\citet{Vijverberg2023} investigate presowing predictability up to 50 days before planting. Using logistic regression and Random Forests driven by Pacific sea surface temperatures and antecedent soil moisture, they predict poor-yield events (anomalies below the 33rd percentile), achieving regional classification skill (ROC-AUC $\approx 0.65$--$0.75$). We integrate this presowing window into a unified continuum, tracking continuous anomaly accuracy and downside tail risk from overwinter dormancy through harvest.

\subsection{Yin et al. (2026): Temporal Aggregation and Sequential Architectures}
\citet{Yinetal2026} evaluate temporal aggregation granularity (5-day, 10-day, 15-day, and 30-day summaries) across Midwest counties (2000--2020) using CNN, LSTM, and Random Forest architectures. They demonstrate that sub-monthly blocks (5-day and 10-day) preserve acute thermal and hydrologic shocks during flowering and pod filling that monthly means smooth out. We evaluate whether sub-monthly sequence advantages persist over a 75-year record, comparing linear shrinkage, tree ensembles, and recurrent networks under identical information sets.

\subsection{Khaki et al. (2020) and Complementary Frameworks}
\citet{Khaki2020} deploy hybrid CNN-RNN models with weekly in-season updating for Midwest corn and soybeans (1980--2018), documenting steady error reductions toward harvest. Complementing this, \citet{Iizumietal2021} show that within-season shortfall probabilities can be anticipated several months ahead. These benchmarks establish the value of intra-seasonal tracking, motivating our dual focus on continuous accuracy and tail-risk anticipation.

\section{Synthesis, Literature Gap, and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:lit_synthesis_gap}

\subsection{Literature Consensus and the Identified Opportunity}
The empirical literature converges on several foundational principles: crop yield responses to weather are non-linear, with acute penalties above $30^\circ\text{C}$ and elevated vapor pressure deficits; reproductive stages in July and August represent the primary yield-determining window in the U.S. Corn Belt; and machine learning architectures can effectively capture high-dimensional agro-climatic interactions.

However, these empirical dimensions have largely been investigated in isolation, spanning isolated phenological windows, restricted historical intervals, or specific model classes. We synthesize these empirical strands into a unified benchmarking framework across 75 years and twelve monthly horizons ($H=12, \dots, 1$), evaluating how lead time, temporal aggregation (5-day, 10-day, 30-day), and model complexity govern out-of-sample skill and tail-risk anticipation.

\subsection{Derivation of Research Hypotheses}
\label{sec:lit_hypotheses}
To address these questions, we formulate five testable research hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 (Incremental Weather Skill):} Adding observed high-resolution weather reanalysis features to reference historical baselines produces a statistically significant reduction in out-of-sample prediction error for county-level soybean yield anomalies.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2 (Lead-Time Dependence and Persistence):} Incremental forecasting skill increases monotonically as the forecast origin approaches harvest ($H \to 1$). Early predictive gains must persist across subsequent monthly cutoffs without reverting to noise.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 (Temporal Aggregation Granularity across Model Families):} High-frequency temporal summaries (5-day and 10-day intervals) and non-linear derived stress indices capture yield-limiting weather shocks more effectively than coarse 30-day monthly aggregations. This finer granularity primarily improves predictive accuracy for flexible architectures (tree ensembles and sequential neural networks) capable of exploiting intra-monthly shock dynamics, whereas linear specifications may suffer from parameter expansion.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 4 (Model Complexity under Strict Validation):} Highly parameterized sequential deep learning models (such as LSTMs) do not systematically outperform transparent regularized linear models or tree ensembles (Random Forests, Gradient Boosting) when evaluated on interannual growing-season anomalies under strict temporal validation.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 5 (Tail-Risk and Downside Shock Anticipation):} Continuous empirical weather models anticipate severe negative yield shocks and downside tail risks (specifically the bottom decile $D_1$) prior to harvest, mitigating conditional shrinkage toward the historical mean and correctly resolving directional shortfall regimes.
\end{itemize}

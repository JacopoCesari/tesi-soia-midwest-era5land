\label{chap:literature_review}

\section{Technological Trends, Weather Variability, and Soybean Agronomic Sensitivity}
\label{sec:lit_yield_trend_weather}

In agricultural economics, crop yield ($Y_{it}$, bushels per acre) serves as the primary metric of biological land productivity. Across multidecadal horizons, developed agricultural systems exhibit strong upward trajectories: in the U.S. Corn Belt, average soybean yields more than doubled from approximately 20 bushels per acre in the early 1950s to over 50 bushels per acre today, driven by cumulative genetic improvements, chemical inputs, mechanization, and precision farming. Consequently, historical yield series $Y_{it}$ decompose into two additive components:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = T_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:yield_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $T_{it}$ represents the smooth technological trend capturing non-meteorological advancements, and $\varepsilon_{it}$ denotes the interannual yield anomaly driven by within-season weather variability, transient pest pressures, and environmental shocks.

Failing to account for $T_{it}$ introduces severe statistical pathologies. Because atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and temperatures have trended upward over the same multidecadal period, estimating models directly on raw yields risks confounding biological weather sensitivity with secular technological growth \citep{Schlenker2009}. A model estimated on raw yields may spuriously attribute genetic gains to rising temperatures, distorting both predictive accuracy and feature attributions. Isolating the weather-driven anomaly $\varepsilon_{it}$ is therefore essential for characterizing meteorological impacts.

\subsection{Non-Linear Agro-Climatic Stress Indicators}
Soybean is an annual legume whose physiological development and final seed production respond non-linearly to prevailing meteorological conditions, governed by acute extremes and phenological exposure timing. Within moderate ranges, temperature accelerates vegetative growth, leaf expansion, and developmental progression. These cumulative thermal requirements are represented via Growing Degree Days (GDD), accumulated between a base temperature ($10^\circ\text{C}$) and an upper threshold ($30^\circ\text{C}$). \citet{Hoffman2020} demonstrate that tracking thermal time provides a biologically grounded framework for identifying developmental milestones, capturing yield-limiting stress far more effectively than standard calendar time.

When temperatures exceed critical thresholds, thermal damage accelerates rapidly. In a seminal empirical study, \citet{Schlenker2009} demonstrated sharp non-linear breakpoints: while temperatures up to $29^\circ\text{C}$ or $30^\circ\text{C}$ are beneficial, temperatures exceeding this threshold inflict severe yield penalties via reproductive heat stress, floral abortion, and accelerated canopy senescence. To capture these damage mechanisms, researchers construct Extreme Degree Days (EDD), accumulating thermal exposure strictly above physiological damage thresholds ($>30^\circ\text{C}$). Coarse monthly temperature averages smooth out these excursions, masking heatwaves lasting only a few consecutive days. Both \citet{Hoffman2020} and \citet{ChenZhang2026} identify indicators of extreme summer heat as among the most decisive predictors of interannual yield variance.

While rainfall replenishes soil moisture, crop water stress is governed by the joint interaction between soil water availability and atmospheric evaporative demand, characterized by Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD). \citet{Hoffman2020} identify elevated VPD as a critical driver of yield reduction during reproductive growth: high VPD forces stomatal closure to prevent desiccation, halting transpirational cooling and carbon fixation even when soil moisture is present. Regarding the surface water budget, empirical studies emphasize compounding interactions between precipitation, root-zone soil moisture, and atmospheric demand \citep{ChenZhang2026, Hoffman2020, Vijverberg2023}. We parameterize the surface climatic water balance via the difference between accumulated precipitation and reference evapotranspiration ($P - \text{ET}_0$), calculated following standard FAO-56 Penman-Monteith formulations. Because biological damage concentrates during midsummer reproductive flowering and pod filling, detailed agronomic descriptions of these phenological vulnerability windows are formalized in Section~\ref{sec:data_phenology}.

\section{Empirical Design Dimensions: Lead Times and Baseline Benchmarking}
\label{sec:lit_targets_lead_times}

\subsection{Forecasting Horizons and In-Season Updating}
In traditional agricultural impact modeling, studies predominantly evaluate crop responses using post-harvest weather series aggregated across the complete growing season \citep{Schlenker2009}. While indispensable for retrospective structural estimation, such post-harvest models provide limited advance notice for operational decision-makers in agricultural supply chains. 

Recent forecasting literature has recognized that predictive utility depends directly on forecast lead time, shifting toward progressive in-season updating. For instance, \citet{ChenZhang2026} evaluate monthly models from May through August, demonstrating that explained variance increases progressively as reproductive weather unfolds. In the U.S. Corn Belt, the critical reproductive phases of flowering (R1--R2) and seed filling (R5--R6) conclude biologically by late August; studies focused strictly on agronomic response mechanisms terminate observations at that point because biological yield accumulation is largely complete. Conversely, operational supply-chain applications require extending predictions through harvest eve in October ($H=1$, or September $H=2$ for early-maturing northern states). This 12-month continuum covers the full commercial decision window and allows formal testing of whether late-season conditions contribute actionable skill or introduce post-maturity noise. At earlier horizons, \citet{Vijverberg2023} demonstrate that antecedent land and ocean states provide predictive signals up to 50 days prior to planting. Evaluating models across sequential horizons ($H=12 \dots 1$) maps precisely when atmospheric observations transition from uninformative noise into reliable forecasting signals.

\subsection{Reference Baselines and Benchmark Anchoring}
In empirical forecasting, evaluating a model's true capability requires benchmarking against parsimonious reference baselines: historical county climatology, deterministic linear trends, or lagged past yields ($Y-1$). 

In trending agricultural panels, baseline models capture the substantial variance attributable to secular technological momentum and permanent cross-sectional productivity differences across counties. Incremental weather skill is assessed through out-of-sample error reductions relative to these baselines. These reductions establish predictive contribution within the evaluation sample; operational value requires a separate assessment. For example, in ablation experiments by \citet{ChenZhang2026}, incorporating lagged yield ($Y-1$) accounted for approximately half of the model's total explained variance, highlighting the essential role of baseline decoupling when isolating pure weather sensitivity.

\section{Statistical, Machine Learning, and Sequential Model Architectures}
\label{sec:lit_model_families}

The literature features three prominent model families for tabular and sequential crop forecasting:

\paragraph{Regularized Linear Models.} Ridge and Lasso regressions provide transparent, computationally efficient baselines that mitigate multicollinearity among highly correlated meteorological features, serving as robust benchmarks against which more complex non-linear architectures can be judged \citep{James2023}.

\paragraph{Tree-Based Ensembles.} Random Forests and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost) represent predominant workhorses for tabular agro-meteorology \citep{Sharmaetal2025}. They naturally accommodate step-function damage thresholds (such as non-linear heat stress above $30^\circ\text{C}$) and multi-way feature interactions without restrictive parametric assumptions, while facilitating post-hoc feature attribution via TreeSHAP \citep{ChenZhang2026}.

\paragraph{Sequential Deep Learning.} Recurrent architectures, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, are designed to process time-series sequences of meteorological inputs, dynamically capturing stress duration, dry spell persistence, and intra-seasonal shock timing \citep{Khaki2020}.

\paragraph{Temporal Granularity in Feature Representation.} Beyond model architecture, the temporal granularity of meteorological features represents a critical design choice. While traditional benchmarks rely on monthly averages \citep{ChenZhang2026}, sub-monthly aggregations (such as 5-day and 10-day windows) preserve acute, short-duration weather extremes that monthly means smooth out \citep{Yinetal2026}.

\section{Approaches to Unobserved Future Weather at Forecast Origin}
\label{sec:lit_future_weather}

Forecasting studies handle unobserved weather after the cutoff through three approaches:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Observed-weather truncation.} Horizon-specific models use observations available through the cutoff. \citet{ChenZhang2026} apply monthly cutoffs; related weekly and sub-monthly designs are reviewed below.
    \item \textbf{Dynamical seasonal forecasts.} Numerical forecasts supply the remaining weather sequence. \citet{Ceglar2021} use ECMWF SEAS5; crop predictions inherit seasonal forecast uncertainty and spatial biases.
    \item \textbf{Climatological or synthetic completion.} Historical averages or weather generators complete future weeks, as in process-based crop modelling. Completion assumptions influence the resulting yield predictions.
\end{enumerate}
We use observed-weather truncation to assess how predictive skill changes as weather information accumulates. This choice excludes seasonal forecasts and synthetic completion from the empirical comparison.

\section{Benchmark Studies and Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:lit_closest_studies}

To position our empirical framework, we examine primary benchmarks in multi-lead-time county crop forecasting across the United States Corn Belt.

\subsection{Chen \& Zhang (2026): Multidecadal County-Level Deep Learning Benchmark}
\citet{ChenZhang2026} compile a 99-year panel (1927--2025) across 1,309 U.S. counties, combining USDA NASS soybean yields with 31 monthly weather variables and static SSURGO soil features. They train a Deep Neural Network and a Random Forest, evaluating monthly forecasts from May through August. On holdout test campaigns (2024--2025), end-of-season $R^2$ reaches 0.67--0.68, with August extreme heat days ($>32^\circ\text{C}$) driving yield reductions. Omitting lagged yield ($Y-1$) contracts test $R^2$ to $\approx 0.33$, showing that historical baseline persistence accounts for half of the explained variance in raw yield levels.

However, their empirical design exhibits structural limitations: the apparent accuracy of un-detrended raw yield models largely absorbs secular technology trends, obscuring atmospheric sensitivity, while evaluating holdouts on only two recent test years leaves the temporal stability and generalization of deep learning architectures across diverse climatic regimes unverified.

\subsection{Vijverberg et al. (2023): Presowing Precursors and Extreme Event Classification}
\citet{Vijverberg2023} investigate presowing predictability up to 50 days before planting. Using logistic regression and Random Forests driven by Pacific sea surface temperatures and antecedent soil moisture, they predict poor-yield events (anomalies below the 33rd percentile), achieving regional classification skill (ROC-AUC $\approx 0.65$--$0.75$). 

While establishing the value of antecedent climatic states, framing the task as a binary classification problem discards continuous shock magnitudes, preventing direct estimation of commercial supply volumes and quantitative downside risks.

\subsection{Yin et al. (2026): Temporal Aggregation and Sequential Architectures}
\citet{Yinetal2026} evaluate temporal aggregation granularity (5-day, 10-day, 15-day, and 30-day summaries) across Midwest counties (2000--2020) using CNN, LSTM, and Random Forest architectures. They demonstrate that sub-monthly blocks (5-day and 10-day) preserve acute thermal and hydrologic shocks during flowering and pod filling that monthly means smooth out. 

Nevertheless, their panel is restricted to a short 21-year satellite-era window, limiting historical exposure to rare extreme macro-climatic disasters and leaving open how high-frequency representations perform under multidecadal expanding-window validation.

\subsection{Khaki et al. (2020) and Complementary Frameworks}
\citet{Khaki2020} deploy hybrid CNN-RNN models with weekly in-season updating for Midwest corn and soybeans (1980--2018), documenting steady error reductions toward harvest. Complementing this, \citet{Iizumietal2021} show that within-season shortfall probabilities can be anticipated several months ahead. 

These benchmarks assume the architectural superiority of complex deep networks a priori, without conducting formal parsimony tests or evaluating whether regularized linear models with agronomic domain features could achieve comparable accuracy under strict chronological validation.

\section{Synthesis, Literature Gap, and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:lit_synthesis_gap}

\subsection{Literature Consensus and the Identified Opportunity}
The reviewed literature establishes strong consensus on non-linear crop weather sensitivity, the primacy of midsummer reproductive vulnerability, and the utility of in-season updating. However, existing studies leave four decisive methodological gaps:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decoupling Anomaly from Secular Trend:} Pervasive reliance on un-detrended raw yields conflates technological momentum with atmospheric sensitivity, inflating apparent model skill via autoregressive lags.
    \item \textbf{Unified 12-Month Predictive Continuum:} Empirical efforts remain fragmented between pre-sowing climate precursors and isolated in-season windows, lacking a continuous monthly mapping from overwinter dormancy through harvest eve without look-ahead bias or numerical forecast drift.
    \item \textbf{Multidecadal Sub-Monthly Sequence Validation:} High-frequency temporal representations (10-day decads) have been tested only over short recent periods, leaving their empirical stability unverified over multidecadal historical panels.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Parsimony and Tail-Risk Validation:} Deep learning architectures are widely assumed superior despite lacking formal out-of-sample significance tests (e.g., Diebold--Mariano) against penalized linear baselines, while their capacity to anticipate catastrophic downside tail risks remains unverified.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Derivation of Research Hypotheses}
\label{sec:lit_hypotheses}
To resolve these four gaps, we structure our empirical inquiry around five core research questions and formal hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 (Incremental Weather Skill):} \textit{Does observed surface weather add genuine predictive value over historical climatology and secular trends?} We hypothesize that incorporating high-resolution ERA5-Land reanalysis produces a statistically significant reduction in out-of-sample forecast error for county yield anomalies relative to deterministic trend baselines.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2 (Lead-Time Dependence and Persistence):} \textit{At what lead time before harvest does reliable predictive skill emerge, and does it persist across subsequent updates?} We hypothesize that predictive skill emerges during midsummer reproductive growth and increases monotonically as harvest approaches ($H \to 1$), with early gains persisting without reverting to noise.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 (LSTM Temporal Aggregation):} \textit{Does a 10-day sub-monthly temporal resolution provide measurable forecasting value compared to standard 30-day monthly summaries across a multidecadal panel?} We hypothesize that 10-day decad sequences capture short-duration heat and moisture extremes, significantly improving out-of-sample validation accuracy over monthly summaries when recurrent architecture hyperparameters are held fixed.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 4 (Model Complexity under Strict Validation):} \textit{Do complex sequential deep learning networks systematically outperform parsimonious regularized linear models under strict chronological validation?} We hypothesize that under strict expanding-window validation on interannual anomalies, highly parameterized deep learning models do not systematically outperform transparent regularized linear models (ElasticNet) or tree ensembles.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 5 (Tail-Risk and Downside Shock Anticipation):} \textit{Can continuous regression models anticipate severe agricultural disasters (the bottom decile $D_1$), or do they collapse toward historical conditional means?} We hypothesize that continuous empirical regression models successfully anticipate acute downside yield shocks prior to harvest, correctly resolving directional shortfall regimes while mitigating conditional shrinkage.
\end{itemize}

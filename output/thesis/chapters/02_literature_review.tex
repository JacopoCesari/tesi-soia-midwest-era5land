\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:literature_review}

\section{Introduction and Organization of the Literature}
\label{sec:lit_structure}

The empirical literature on agricultural productivity, climate variability, and yield forecasting spans agricultural economics, crop physiology, atmospheric science, and applied machine learning. Understanding how meteorological variability translates into crop yield anomalies requires examining biological sensitivity mechanisms, statistical representations of secular technological trends, candidate machine learning architectures, and rigorous out-of-sample validation protocols.

To establish the academic foundation for this thesis, this review is organized into five domains: (i) decomposing raw yields into technological trends and weather anomalies; (ii) soybean physiological sensitivity to thermal, hydrologic, and phenological shocks; (iii) environmental data modalities and the rationale for a weather-only scope; (iv) candidate predictive models, lead-time dynamics, and reference baselines; and (v) spatial autocorrelation, validation protocols, and handling unobserved future weather. The review culminates in a comparative analysis of primary empirical benchmarks \citep{ChenZhang2026, Vijverberg2023, Yinetal2026} and the formal derivation of research hypotheses (H1--H5).

\section{Agricultural Yield, Technological Trends, and Weather Variability}
\label{sec:lit_yield_trend_weather}

In agricultural economics, crop yield ($Y_{it}$, bushels per acre) serves as the primary metric of biological productivity. Across multidecadal horizons, developed agricultural systems exhibit strong upward trajectories: in the U.S. Corn Belt, average soybean yields more than doubled from approximately 20 bushels per acre in the early 1950s to over 50 bushels per acre today, driven by cumulative genetic improvements, chemical inputs, mechanization, and precision farming. Consequently, historical yield series $Y_{it}$ decompose into two additive components:
\begin{equation}
    Y_{it} = T_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}
    \label{eq:yield_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $T_{it}$ represents the smooth technological trend capturing non-meteorological advancements, and $\varepsilon_{it}$ denotes the interannual yield anomaly driven by within-season weather variability, transient pest pressures, and environmental shocks.

Failing to account for $T_{it}$ introduces severe statistical pathologies. Because atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations and temperatures have trended upward over the same multidecadal period, training models directly on raw yields risks confounding physiological weather sensitivity with secular technological growth \citep{Schlenker2009}. A model estimated on raw yields may spuriously attribute genetic gains to rising temperatures, distorting both predictive accuracy and feature attributions.

Following established literature \citep{Hoffman2020, Schlenker2009}, detrending is a foundational prerequisite for isolating weather-yield relationships. Crucially, in operational forecasting, trend estimation must be conducted strictly \textit{train-only}. Applying two-sided filters or fitting trend lines across the full panel constitutes temporal data leakage, imparting future yield trajectories into past training folds.

\section{Weather Sensitivity of Soybean and Agro-Meteorological Features}
\label{sec:lit_weather_sensitivity}

Soybean (\textit{Glycine max}) is an annual legume whose physiological development and final seed production respond non-linearly to prevailing weather conditions, governed by acute extremes and phenological exposure timing.

\subsection{Growing Degree Days and Thermal Time}
The relationship between ambient temperature and crop development is non-linear. Within moderate ranges, temperature accelerates vegetative growth, leaf expansion, and developmental progression. These cumulative thermal requirements are represented via Growing Degree Days (GDD), accumulated between a base temperature ($10^\circ\text{C}$) and an upper threshold ($30^\circ\text{C}$). \citet{Hoffman2020} demonstrate that tracking thermal time provides a biologically grounded framework for identifying developmental milestones, capturing yield-limiting stress far more effectively than standard calendar months.

\subsection{Non-Linear Temperature Breakpoints and Thermal Damage}
When temperatures exceed critical thresholds, thermal damage accelerates rapidly. In a seminal econometric analysis, \citet{Schlenker2009} demonstrated sharp non-linear breakpoints: while temperatures up to $29^\circ\text{C}$ or $30^\circ\text{C}$ are beneficial, temperatures exceeding this threshold inflict severe yield penalties via floral abscission, pollen sterility, stomatal closure, and impaired photosynthesis.

To capture these damage mechanisms, researchers construct Extreme Degree Days (EDD), accumulating thermal exposure strictly above physiological damage thresholds ($>30^\circ\text{C}$). Coarse monthly temperature averages inevitably smooth out these excursions, masking destructive heatwaves lasting only a few consecutive days. Both \citet{Hoffman2020} and \citet{ChenZhang2026} identify indicators of extreme summer heat as among the most decisive predictors of interannual yield variance.

\subsection{Atmospheric Moisture Demand and Soil Water Balance}
While rainfall replenishes soil moisture, crop water stress is governed by the joint interaction between soil water availability and atmospheric evaporative demand, characterized by Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD). \citet{Hoffman2020} identify elevated VPD as a critical driver of yield reduction during reproductive growth: high VPD forces stomatal closure to prevent desiccation, halting transpirational cooling and carbon fixation even when soil moisture is present.

Regarding the surface water budget, empirical studies emphasize compounding interactions between precipitation, root-zone soil moisture, and atmospheric demand \citep{ChenZhang2026, Hoffman2020, Vijverberg2023}. In this thesis, the surface climatic water balance is operationalized via the difference between accumulated precipitation and reference evapotranspiration ($P - \text{ET}_0$), calculated following FAO-56 Penman-Monteith guidelines \citep{Allen1998}, providing an agronomically plausible feature for empirical validation.

\subsection{Phenological Vulnerability Windows}
The biological impact of meteorological shocks depends decisively on crop phenology. Soybeans exhibit substantial compensatory plasticity during early vegetative growth (V1--V$n$); moderate moisture deficits or temperature anomalies can often be overcome if favorable weather resumes before flowering \citep{Hoffman2020}.

Conversely, mid-season reproductive phases---specifically pod development (R3--R4) and seed filling (R5--R6), occurring between mid-July and late August in the U.S. Corn Belt---represent the critical yield-determining window \citep{Hoffman2020, Yinetal2026}. Severe moisture deficits or heatwaves during pod filling directly reduce pod counts, seed numbers, and seed weight, causing irreversible yield losses. Meteorological conditions proximate to harvest (September and October) primarily affect grain dry-down and field machinery access rather than biological yield formation.

\section{Environmental Data Modalities and the Rationale for a Weather-Only Scope}
\label{sec:lit_data_sources}

Empirical crop forecasting leverages diverse meteorological, biophysical, and management sources. Gridded atmospheric reanalyses, particularly ECMWF ERA5-Land \citep{Vijverberg2023, Yinetal2026}, provide continuous, physically consistent hourly meteorological fields at $0.1^\circ$ resolution ($\approx 9\text{ km}$), eliminating the station-drop biases, spatial interpolation artifacts, and reporting gaps inherent in ground station networks \citep{ChenZhang2026}.

A central methodological choice of this thesis is the deliberate restriction of dynamic inputs strictly to observed surface weather. This restriction is justified by four foundational constraints that render alternative modalities methodologically inadmissible for multi-lead-time forecasting over multidecadal panels:

\paragraph{Ex-Post Reporting of Agronomic Management.} Comprehensive county-level management records (cultivar genetics, planting dates, fertilizer application rates) are typically compiled at annual cadences and released months after harvest. Incorporating these records into intra-seasonal forecasts would induce severe temporal data leakage or operational infeasibility. Furthermore, consistent 75-year historical panels of management practices at county resolution do not exist.

\paragraph{Physiological Latency and Satellite Coverage Limits.} While vegetation indices (NDVI, EVI) and Solar-Induced Chlorophyll Fluorescence (SIF) from MODIS or Sentinel monitor canopy health \citep{Yinetal2026}, their historical depth is limited to the post-2000 era, preventing multi-decadal sampling of climate extremes. Crucially, satellite greenness signals exhibit severe physiological latency, becoming informative only after mid-summer canopy closure (July), offering zero predictive utility during pre-sowing and early vegetative stages ($H \ge 4$).

\paragraph{Temporal Invariance of Pedological Attributes.} Static soil endowments from databases such as USGS SSURGO (water capacity, organic matter, texture) \citep{ChenZhang2026, Khaki2020} explain permanent cross-sectional yield baselines across counties. However, because their values are constant over time ($\text{Var}(X_{it}) = 0$), static soil features contain zero interannual variance to explain temporal yield anomalies.

\paragraph{Avoiding Confounded Climate Model Biases.} While dynamical seasonal weather forecasts (e.g., ECMWF SEAS5) are available in real time, coupling crop models with seasonal atmospheric predictions conflates agronomic sensitivity with long-range climate model biases. Restricting dynamic inputs to realized surface reanalysis isolates the unconfounded, physical upper bound of predictability attributable strictly to atmospheric forcing observed up to the forecast cutoff.

\section{Lead-Time Progression, Categorical Regimes, and Baseline Benchmarking in Literature}
\label{sec:lit_targets_lead_times}

\subsection{Static Evaluation Horizons versus Progressive Lead-Time Dynamics}
A prevalent limitation in the crop forecasting literature is evaluating models at isolated calendar cutoffs, such as a single mid-summer date (e.g., July 31) or post-harvest weather. While useful for retrospective estimation, static cutoffs obscure how predictive skill accumulates across the growing cycle. Standardizing evaluation across sequential monthly horizons ($H=12, \dots, 1$) is necessary to determine when atmospheric observations transition from uninformative noise into reliable predictive signals.

\subsection{Continuous Anomaly Forecasting versus Categorical Regimes}
While academic research has predominantly focused on continuous point estimates evaluated via $R^2$ or RMSE, agricultural risk managers and supply-chain operators frequently require discrete regime classifications. Distinguishing whether an upcoming harvest will fall into a normal regime, a bumper crop, or a severe shortfall (e.g., lower quartile or negative $z$-score) is often more actionable for forward hedging, storage allocation, and procurement than predicting exact continuous decimals \citep{Iizumietal2021, Vijverberg2023}. Evaluating both formulations allows testing whether categorical regimes can be detected earlier than precise continuous point forecasts.

\subsection{The Necessity of Parsimonious Reference Baselines}
Machine learning models are frequently reported with high predictive accuracy without benchmarking against parsimonious historical baselines (deterministic linear trends, county climatology, or autoregressive past yields). In trending series, a baseline establishes how much variance is explained by historical momentum alone. Without explicit baseline benchmarking, high reported accuracy risks mistaking secular technological progress for genuine weather-driven forecasting skill \citep{ChenZhang2026}.

\section{Statistical, Machine Learning, and Sequential Model Architectures}
\label{sec:lit_model_families}

The literature features three prominent model families for tabular and sequential crop forecasting:

\paragraph{Regularized Linear Models.} Ridge and Lasso regressions provide transparent, computationally efficient baselines that mitigate multicollinearity among highly correlated meteorological features, but cannot capture non-linear thermal thresholds without manual feature cross-products.

\paragraph{Tree-Based Ensembles.} Random Forests \citep{Jeongetal2016} and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (XGBoost) represent predominant workhorses for tabular agro-meteorology \citep{Sharmaetal2025}. They naturally accommodate step-function damage thresholds and multi-way feature interactions without restrictive parametric assumptions, enabling feature attribution via TreeSHAP \citep{ChenZhang2026}.

\paragraph{Sequential Deep Learning.} Recurrent architectures, particularly Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, are designed to process time-series sequences of meteorological shocks, dynamically capturing spell duration, stress persistence, and intra-seasonal timing \citep{Khaki2020, Yinetal2026}.

\paragraph{The Sample Size Constraint ($T$ vs $N \times T$).} A fundamental statistical constraint governs agricultural forecasting. Although an empirical panel contains thousands of pooled county-year observations ($N \times T$), observations within any single crop year are strongly spatially correlated due to synoptic-scale weather systems. Consequently, effective degrees of freedom are governed primarily by the number of independent growing seasons ($T \approx 75$), creating acute risks of overfitting for heavily parameterized deep networks unless evaluated under strict out-of-sample temporal validation.

\section{Validation Protocols and Handling Future Weather}
\label{sec:lit_validation_leakage}

\subsection{Spatiotemporal Dependence and Cross-Validation Fallacies}
In an authoritative empirical study, \citet{Sweetetal2023} demonstrated that cross-validation strategies fundamentally dictate both reported accuracy and feature attributions in Earth system machine learning. Because neighboring counties experience nearly identical synoptic weather shocks in any given year, applying standard random $k$-fold cross-validation allows algorithms to memorize regional climate shocks from training counties and predict neighboring test counties with artificially inflated accuracy. \citet{Sweetetal2023} prove that valid evaluation must keep all counties within a calendar year strictly grouped within either the training or testing fold.

\subsection{Approaches to Unobserved Future Weather at Forecast Origin}
At any intermediate forecast origin ($H > 1$), meteorological conditions between the cutoff date and final harvest remain unknown. In the literature, three primary approaches address this missing future information:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Future-Weather Truncation:} Conditioning predictions strictly on meteorological features observed up to the cutoff date, setting aside unobserved future periods. This approach cleanly isolates the genuine accumulated predictive information delivered by current observations without confounding forecast skill with downstream weather assumptions.
    \item \textbf{Naive Persistence or Climatological Imputation:} Imputing unobserved future weeks using historical county climatological averages. While completing the calendar window, it introduces artificial smoothing and assumes average future conditions.
    \item \textbf{Coupling with Seasonal Numerical Forecasts:} Driving crop models with dynamical seasonal weather forecasts (e.g., ECMWF SEAS5). While operationally relevant, it conflates crop-model skill with the well-known skill limitations and biases of long-range seasonal climate predictions.
\end{enumerate}

Evaluating models on trending yield levels using standard $R^2$ predominantly measures the ability to track secular technological growth rather than interannual weather sensitivity. Robust empirical evaluation requires evaluating detrended anomalies against explicit reference baselines under complete-year expanding windows.

\section{Benchmark Studies and Comparative Analysis}
\label{sec:lit_closest_studies}

To establish the precise positioning of this thesis, this section examines the empirical studies most directly related to multi-lead-time county-level crop forecasting.

\subsection{Chen \& Zhang (2026): The Primary Empirical Benchmark}
The most direct benchmark is the multidecadal study by \citet{ChenZhang2026}, who synthesize a 99-year dataset (1927--2025) across 1,309 U.S. counties, combining USDA NASS soybean yields with 31 monthly weather variables from NOAA GSOM stations and 15 static SSURGO soil features. They train a Deep Neural Network alongside a companion Random Forest for TreeSHAP attribution, evaluating monthly updates from May through August.

On their test holdout (2024 and 2025), \citet{ChenZhang2026} report end-of-year $R^2$ values of 0.668 and 0.678, with in-season 2025 $R^2$ rising from 0.516 in May to 0.668 in August. However, critical methodological limitations emerge upon closer inspection:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Conflation of Trend and Weather:} By modeling raw yield levels without detrending and including lagged yield ($Y-1$) as an input, historical persistence dominates the prediction. In their ablation test, removing $Y-1$ caused test $R^2$ to collapse from $\approx 0.67$ to $0.315$ in 2024 and $0.351$ in 2025, proving that more than half of their reported explained variance stems from baseline persistence rather than current weather.
    \item \textit{Validation Protocol:} While 2024 and 2025 were held out, the preceding 1927--2023 sample was partitioned into 90\% training and 10\% validation via random splitting, inducing spatiotemporal data leakage during model tuning \citep{Sweetetal2023}.
    \item \textit{Sample and Baseline Limitations:} Model generalization was evaluated on only two test years, and predictions were not benchmarked against parsimonious historical baselines (linear trend or climatology).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Vijverberg et al. (2023): Presowing Extreme Event Classification}
\citet{Vijverberg2023} investigate soybean predictability at presowing horizons (up to 50 days prior to planting), predicting poor-yield events (detrended anomalies below the 33rd percentile) using logistic regression and Random Forests driven by remote Pacific sea surface temperatures and antecedent land soil moisture. They demonstrate statistically significant classification skill at aggregated regional scales, confirming the presence of early precursors. This thesis extends this inquiry by tracking both continuous anomalies and discrete regimes at fine county resolution across the complete growing season using in-season reanalysis.

\subsection{Yin et al. (2026): Temporal Aggregation and Sequential Architectures}
\citet{Yinetal2026} examine temporal aggregation by comparing models trained on environmental variables summarized at 5-day, 10-day, 15-day, and 30-day intervals. They find that 5-day and 10-day aggregations preserve acute stress signals that monthly averages smooth out, allowing sequential CNN-LSTMs to capture stress timing during flowering and pod filling. However, sequential deep learning exhibited volatility during extreme anomaly years.

\subsection{Khaki et al. (2020) and Iizumi et al. (2021)}
\citet{Khaki2020} evaluate hybrid CNN-RNN architectures with weekly in-season updating for Midwest corn and soybeans, illustrating the progression of prediction accuracy as harvest approaches. In a complementary direction, \citet{Iizumietal2021} demonstrate that broad categorical yield regimes can often be detected several months prior to harvest, corroborating the value of dual continuous-categorical modeling.

\section{Synthesis, Literature Gap, and Hypotheses}
\label{sec:lit_synthesis_gap}

\subsection{Literature Consensus and the Identified Gap}
The literature establishes that crop yield responses to weather are non-linear (with severe penalties above $30^\circ\text{C}$ and under elevated vapor pressure deficit), that reproductive stages in July and August dominate Midwest yield variance, that machine learning can capture these relationships, and that random cross-validation induces severe spatiotemporal leakage.

However, existing studies leave a critical empirical gap: they provide fragmented evidence on \textit{when} progressively observed local weather begins to add statistically significant and stable predictive skill beyond historical baselines under strict temporal validation. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Lead-time horizons have been evaluated at isolated calendar dates rather than across a standardized twelve-month progression ($H=12, \dots, 1$), leaving signal emergence and temporal stability uncharacterized.
    \item Past studies frequently conflate secular technological growth and historical inertia with weather skill by modeling raw yields without explicit baseline decoupling.
    \item The relative benefit of high-frequency temporal summaries (5-day and 10-day vs.\ monthly means) versus model complexity (regularized linear models, tree ensembles, and LSTMs) remains unresolved under the constraint of independent annual growing seasons.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Derivation of Research Hypotheses}
To address this gap, the empirical investigation is structured around five formal, testable research hypotheses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 (Incremental Weather Skill):} Adding observed high-resolution weather reanalysis features to reference historical baselines produces a statistically significant reduction in out-of-sample prediction error for county-level soybean yield anomalies.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 2 (Lead-Time Dependence and Persistence):} Incremental forecasting skill increases monotonically as the forecast origin approaches harvest ($H \to 1$). Furthermore, early predictive gains must persist stably across subsequent monthly cutoffs without reverting to noise to constitute actionable forecasting skill.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 (Temporal Aggregation Granularity):} High-frequency temporal summaries (5-day and 10-day intervals) and non-linear derived stress indices (such as Extreme Degree Days and vapor pressure deficit) capture yield-limiting weather shocks more effectively than coarse 30-day monthly aggregations.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 4 (Model Complexity under Strict Validation):} Complex sequential deep learning models (such as LSTMs) do not systematically outperform transparent regularized linear models or tree ensembles (Random Forests, Gradient Boosting) when subjected to strict, out-of-sample expanding-window validation.
    \item \textbf{Hypothesis 5 (Early Regime Recognition):} Discrete yield anomaly classes and extreme shortfall regimes can be classified with positive predictive skill at earlier lead times than required for accurate continuous point forecasts.
\end{itemize}

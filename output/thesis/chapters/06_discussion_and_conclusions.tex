\chapter{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{chap:discussion_conclusions}

This chapter synthesizes the empirical evidence established across the twelve forecast horizons, evaluates the findings against the existing literature, formalizes the verdicts on hypotheses H1--H5, and outlines operational implications alongside methodological boundaries.

\section{Synthesis of Empirical Findings}
\label{sec:disc_synthesis}

We evaluated county-level soybean yield predictability across twelve monthly forecast horizons over a 75-year panel using expanding-window validation.

The empirical benchmark establishes a clear tripartite predictability regime across the production calendar. Prior to late spring ($H = 11 \dots 7$), observed surface weather provides zero predictive skill over historical trends ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \le 0$), where cold-season noise degrades flexible non-linear architectures while regularized linear shrinkage remains robust. Predictive skill crosses into positive territory at May planting ($H = 6$), driven by seedbed moisture and emergence thermal accumulation. Across active reproductive stages ($H = 5 \dots 1$), skill expands rapidly as pod-filling weather unfolds in July and August, stabilizing through autumn maturity. At harvest ($H = 1$), the sequential LSTM with 10-day decad tracking and prior-year memory achieves champion performance ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.35$, $\text{RMSE} = 4.94\text{ bu/ac}$), matched closely by regularized linear baselines ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.31$).

Recombining anomaly forecasts with out-of-sample trend projections confirms that weather observations resolve an additional $+18.3$ percentage points of commercial yield variation beyond secular technological progress ($R^2_{\text{level}} = 0.66$, $r = 0.84$). Cross-sectional pooling across the 135 study counties into a regional supply portfolio cancels out idiosyncratic microclimatic noise, reducing macro forecast error to $\text{RMSE} = 2.31\text{ bu/ac}$ and elevating correlation to $r = 0.944$.

\section{Confrontation with Existing Literature}
\label{sec:disc_literature}

\subsection{Target Formulation and the Detrending Confound}
A primary challenge in benchmarking agricultural machine learning architectures lies in reconciling disparate target formulations. Published studies frequently report coefficients of determination exceeding $R^2 \ge 0.65\text{--}0.80$ for county-level crop yields \citep{Khaki2020, ChenZhang2026}. 

These elevated scores stem primarily from predicting raw physical yields ($y$) without detrending, frequently augmented by un-detrended autoregressive lags ($y_{t-1}$). In \citet{ChenZhang2026}, county-level models achieve $R^2 = 0.668\text{--}0.678$ on raw yields when conditioning on $y_{t-1}$. In their ablation, omitting $y_{t-1}$ causes explained variance to collapse to $R^2 = 0.315\text{--}0.351$. When statistical algorithms predict raw yield levels, they absorb multidecadal technological growth and permanent cross-sectional spatial soil gradients, confounding secular agronomic progress with meteorological forcing \citep{Khaki2020, Sweetetal2023}. 

In our benchmark, detrending executes strictly on training partitions. Our champion model achieves $R^2_{\text{OOS}} = 0.349$ on pure climatic anomalies. When recombined with out-of-sample technological trends, physical level predictions reach $R^2_{\text{level}} = 0.657$ ($r = 0.837$, Table~\ref{tab:raw_vs_anomaly_performance}). Furthermore, \citet{Hoffman2020} evaluate pure weather-only models on detrended county soybean anomalies across the Corn Belt, reporting an empirical ceiling of $R^2 \in [0.18, 0.24]$. Our regularized tabular models ($R^2 = 0.28\text{--}0.31$) align directly with their findings, while our sequential LSTM ($R^2 = 0.35$) extends beyond their benchmark.

\subsection{Spatial Aggregation: County Disaggregation versus Regional Portfolios}
Direct comparisons across literature benchmarks must account for the spatial unit of analysis. Studies evaluating disaggregated county-level panels face substantial microclimatic noise, localized hail damage, pest outbreaks, and tile drainage variability. These unmodelled local factors impose an empirical predictability ceiling on county anomaly variance ($R^2_{\text{OOS}} \approx 0.35$, county $\text{RMSE} = 4.94\text{ bu/ac}$ in Table~\ref{tab:raw_vs_anomaly_performance}).

When predictions are pooled across the 135 study counties into a regional supply portfolio (Section~\ref{sec:res_macro_portfolio}), uncorrelated county-level residuals cancel out at rate $1/\sqrt{N}$ by the Law of Large Numbers. At this macro-regional scale, forecast error drops by over 50\% to $\text{RMSE} = 2.31\text{ bu/ac}$, while correlation with realized regional yield anomalies reaches $r = 0.881$ (with the sequential LSTM reaching $r = 0.909$, Table~\ref{tab:yearly_macro_yields}). In the 2012 flash drought, regional error shrinks to $-0.33\text{ bu/ac}$. Evaluating models on state-level or national production totals inherently smooths local variance, yielding lower nominal errors than county-level estimations. Benchmarks must therefore distinguish between disaggregated field-scale resolution and macro-portfolio forecasting.

\subsection{Task Formulation and Sample Scope: Continuous Regression versus Classification}
The literature diverges fundamentally in task formulation and evaluation scope:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Continuous Regression versus Directional Classification:} In operational early warning systems, \citet{Iizumietal2021} formulate within-season crop monitoring as a classification problem, predicting categorical anomaly directions (above or below climatological normal) or binary shock events, reporting Directional Hit Rates of 65--75\%. Our multi-horizon framework achieves a directional hit rate of $71.4\%$ while simultaneously solving continuous regression across the real line ($-\infty, +\infty$). Estimating continuous bushel magnitudes provides commercial supply chains with quantitative volume assessments required for physical procurement and storage allocations.
    \item \textbf{Unbroken Panels versus Filtered Case Subsets:} Several studies establish skill by subsetting evaluation data to specific shock regimes. For example, \citet{Vijverberg2023} demonstrate presowing predictability by conditioning specifically on extreme heat and drought cohorts using Pacific sea-surface temperature teleconnections. In our work, evaluations execute continuously across the complete out-of-sample panel ($2{,}025$ county-years across 15 consecutive campaigns). A complete panel forces estimators to maintain bounded shrinkage across noisy central deciles ($D_3\text{--}D_6$) without over-reacting to benign weather, while simultaneously resolving acute tails ($D_1$ and $D_{10}$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Observational Perimeters: Reanalysis versus Remote Sensing and Breeding Trials}
Published benchmarks deploy divergent predictor modalities that define their operational perimeter:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Satellite Remote Sensing Fusion:} Advanced multi-modal frameworks integrate in-season satellite vegetation indices alongside meteorology. \citet{Xie2025} evaluate county-level soybean yields in the Mississippi basin using MODIS NDVI feeds, reporting physical yield $\text{RMSE} \in [4.43, 6.22]\text{ bu/ac}$. \citet{Yinetal2026} incorporate Solar-Induced Chlorophyll Fluorescence (SIF) and near-infrared reflectance of vegetation (NIRv), reporting $R^2 \approx 0.67$ on raw yields. Our weather-only framework attains comparable physical accuracy ($\text{RMSE} = 4.94\text{ bu/ac}$ at county level; $\text{RMSE} = 2.31\text{ bu/ac}$ at regional level) without requiring real-time orbital optical feeds, which remain subject to cloud contamination and satellite processing latency.
    \item \textbf{Controlled Breeding Trials versus Commercial Landscapes:} Studies reporting $R^2 \approx 0.80$ such as \citet{Shooketal2021} evaluate experimental breeding trials using dense genomic marker matrices of parental lines. Experimental plots operate under controlled agronomic management across uniform research plots. Commercial county statistics reflect heterogeneous farmer management, mixed cultivars, and variable planting dates, representing an observational setting that cannot ingest proprietary seed genetics.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Model Complexity, Inductive Bias, and Temporal Sequences}
Agricultural machine learning literature frequently presumes the systematic superiority of complex non-linear architectures over regularized linear models \citep{Khaki2020, Sharmaetal2025}. Our comparative benchmark demonstrates that algorithmic advantage is governed by lead time and temporal feature structure:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Linear-Tree Competitive Parity on Tabular Blocks:} On structured 30-day monthly blocks, decision tree ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost) offer no systematic predictive advantage over penalized linear regression (ElasticNet). This corroborates the systematic review by \citet{Sharmaetal2025}, confirming that well-engineered bioclimatic features establish linear-tree parity in crop yield forecasting.
    \item \textbf{Sequential Advantage on Sub-Monthly Decads:} Consistent with \citet{Yinetal2026}, sub-monthly temporal resolution benefits sequential deep learning. Operating on 10-day decads enables the recurrent LSTM with temporal attention to isolate acute thermal excursions ($EDD_{30}$, $VPD$) during flowering and pod development ($H = 3 \dots 1$), exceeding ElasticNet by $4.3$ percentage points at harvest ($R^2 = 0.349$ vs $0.306$).
    \item \textbf{Leaf Saturation in Decision Trees:} In extreme bumper campaigns (2016, 2021), tree ensembles saturate on training-set leaf bounds ($51\text{--}54\text{ bu/ac}$). Because ElasticNet operates without partitioning boundaries, it extrapolates favorable hydrometeorological signals, securing superior point calibration during record seasons.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Operational Implications for Agrifood Supply Chains}
\label{sec:disc_operational}

The empirical performance profile across the twelve monthly forecast origins maps directly into commercial risk-management decisions across agricultural supply chains:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Planting Window ($H=6$, late May):} Initial positive skill ($R^2 \approx 0.05$) allows grain origination desks and commercial elevators to detect broad regional emergence anomalies, informing preliminary forward contracting.
    \item \textbf{Midsummer Reproductive Window ($H=4$, late July):} With explained variance reaching $22\%$, crush plants and poultry feed mills can anticipate regional supply contractions, adjusting seasonal maintenance schedules and physical procurement baselines two months prior to harvest.
    \item \textbf{Grain-Filling Milestone ($H=3$, late August):} Reaching $R^2 \approx 0.32$, major yield shortfalls (such as the 2012 flash drought) are fully detected with near-zero regional bias. This provides river barge operators and export terminals along the Mississippi corridor with a critical 60-day operational window to secure freight capacity and realign export commitments before combine harvesters enter fields.
    \item \textbf{Continuous Anomaly Forecasting for Risk Sizing:} In physical commodity merchandising, estimating continuous bushel magnitudes is essential for sizing commercial hedging positions and storage allocations. Continuous anomaly forecasts allow operators to distinguish between minor deviations and severe multi-bushel shocks, structuring option fences and physical commitments proportionally to anticipated shortfall magnitude.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Methodological Boundaries and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

Several boundary conditions govern the interpretation of our findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reanalysis Latency versus Real-Time Deployment:} ERA5-Land reanalysis is released with an operational latency of approximately 2 to 3 months. Consequently, retrospective benchmarks delineate the theoretical upper bound of weather-driven predictability. Operational deployment requires pairing the model with near-real-time streams (such as ECMWF ERA5T with a 5-day latency) or operational numerical weather prediction analyses.
    \item \textbf{Panel Selection and Survivorship:} Our 75-year panel balances 135 counties continuously from 1951 to 2025 without imputation. This design prioritizes multidecadal temporal depth to capture historical climate extremes, but introduces geographic selection toward high-productivity commercial counties. Results represent commercial rainfed production across the core Midwestern Corn Belt, and cannot be extrapolated directly to marginal or arid fringes.
    \item \textbf{Unmodelled Anthropogenic and Hydrological Drivers:} In the Missouri Bootheel, supplemental groundwater irrigation decouples soybean performance from ERA5-Land precipitation deficits, attenuating out-of-sample skill ($R^2 \le 0.06$). Similarly, dense subsurface tile drainage in northwestern Ohio alters soil moisture residence times relative to reanalysis soil layers. Omitting explicit irrigation and drainage infrastructure maps leaves spatial residual clustering in these enclaves.
    \item \textbf{October Harvest-Timing Contamination:} Across northern study counties, soybean harvesting begins in early October. While synchronizing harvest closure ($H^*=2$) resolves post-harvest contamination for Minnesota, fixed calendar cutoffs in other northern fringes (such as northern Ohio) can still introduce uninformative late-October weather into unadjusted baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Concluding Remarks and Future Research}
\label{sec:disc_conclusions}

This study establishes a rigorous, multi-horizon empirical benchmarking framework for county-level soybean yield forecasting across twelve monthly lead times. By detrending yields strictly within historical folds and evaluating models across an unbroken 75-year panel, we isolated true meteorological anomaly skill from secular technological progress.

Our findings prove that observed surface weather delivers statistically significant predictive skill exclusively during the active growing season ($H \le 5$), explaining up to $35\%$ of climatic anomaly variance and $66\%$ of physical yield variation at harvest. While sequential deep learning (LSTM) operating on 10-day decads achieves champion performance by capturing acute reproductive heat shocks, parsimonious regularized linear models (ElasticNet) provide exceptional stability, zero cold-season degradation, and early-season dominance.

Promising avenues for future research include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Coupling with Short-Range Numerical Weather Forecasts:} Integrating 10-to-15-day ensemble weather forecasts at active horizons ($H = 4, 3$) to extend the lead time of reproductive heat and moisture stress warnings.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Crop Cross-Commodity Architectures:} Expanding the multi-horizon expanding-window framework to corn, evaluating whether shared agro-climatic representations improve early-season predictability.
    \item \textbf{Phenology-Guided Satellite Fusion:} Incorporating optical reflectance and vegetation fluorescence strictly during reproductive pod-filling (July--August) to calibrate physiological canopy status without compromising early-season lead times.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Discussion and Conclusions}
\label{chap:discussion_conclusions}

This chapter synthesizes the empirical evidence established across the twelve forecast horizons, evaluates the findings against the existing literature, formalizes the verdicts on hypotheses H1--H5, and outlines operational implications alongside methodological boundaries.

% ==============================================================================
% SECTION 6.1: SYNTHESIS OF EMPIRICAL FINDINGS
% ==============================================================================

\section{Synthesis of Empirical Findings}
\label{sec:disc_synthesis}

{\color{red}
The twelve-horizon evaluation shows limited early-season predictability and increasing skill as reproductive weather accumulates. Regularized linear models remain competitive with tree ensembles, while integrated LSTM and ensemble forecasts achieve the lowest harvest errors. Regional averaging improves aggregate accuracy but leaves county-level tail attenuation. These findings separate the timing of useful weather information from the additional contribution of model complexity.
}

\section{Confrontation with Existing Literature}
\label{sec:disc_literature}

\subsection{Target Formulation and the Detrending Confound}
{\color{red}
Target definitions constrain comparisons with published scores. \citet{ChenZhang2026} report raw-yield $R^2$ near $0.67$ with lagged yield; omitting that predictor reduces their score to approximately $0.33$. This ablation demonstrates the importance of separating historical yield information from weather inputs.

Our LSTM (+Lag) reaches $R^2_{\text{OOS}}=0.336$ on anomalies and $R^2_{\text{level}}=0.651$ after trend reconstruction (Table~\ref{tab:raw_vs_anomaly_performance}). This integrated specification extends the starting weather-only benchmark. The comparison of information sets quantifies the additional contribution of lagged yield.

\citet{Hoffman2020} report lower scores on detrended soybean anomalies. Their results offer a relevant comparison of target construction, although differences in sample and evaluation protocol prevent a direct ranking of model skill.
}

\subsection{Spatial Aggregation: County Disaggregation versus Regional Portfolios}
{\color{red}
Spatial scale also affects reported accuracy. County forecasts retain local variation from weather exposure and unobserved management. Averaging across counties can offset some local errors, as shown by the regional results in Section~\ref{sec:res_macro_portfolio}. Spatially correlated errors remain, so the reduction does not imply independent county observations.

The ensemble's regional RMSE is $2.22\text{ bu/ac}$, compared with $4.91\text{ bu/ac}$ at county level. LSTM has a lower regional RMSE point estimate, while the ensemble has the lowest pooled county-level error. The ensemble's correlation with regional anomalies is $0.888$; its correlation with reconstructed regional yield levels is $0.947$. These metrics describe different prediction tasks. Studies reporting state or national averages should therefore be compared at a matching aggregation level. Our regional mean is equally weighted across counties and does not estimate acreage-weighted production.
}

\subsection{Task Formulation and Sample Scope: Continuous Regression versus Classification}
{\color{red}
\citet{Iizumietal2021} evaluate directional and event-based forecasts. Our continuous predictions retain anomaly magnitude, while directional accuracy provides a complementary diagnostic. Similar hit rates do not establish equivalent performance when event definitions, spatial units, and evaluation samples differ.

\citet{Vijverberg2023} examine presowing predictability using ocean and land precursors, with an emphasis on poor-yield events. Our evaluation covers all $2{,}025$ county-years in the test period. It therefore assesses both extreme departures and central regimes. Differences in predictor scope and event selection matter when interpreting the absence of early skill in our surface-weather benchmark.
}

\subsection{Observational Perimeters: Reanalysis versus Remote Sensing and Breeding Trials}
{\color{red}
\citet{Xie2025} combine satellite vegetation information with weather and report county-level RMSE between $4.43$ and $6.22\text{ bu/ac}$. Our county errors fall within that range, but differences in samples and predictors preclude a controlled accuracy comparison. Optical inputs also depend on cloud conditions and processing availability.

\citet{Yinetal2026} include Solar-Induced Chlorophyll Fluorescence (SIF) and near-infrared reflectance of vegetation (NIRv). These measurements describe crop state during the season. Their exclusion here isolates information from surface weather and preserves the multidecadal observation window.

\citet{Shooketal2021} study breeding trials with genomic inputs and controlled experimental settings. County statistics combine management practices, cultivars, and planting dates. Their higher reported scores therefore concern a different information set and observational unit. These distinctions define the scope of our comparison.
}

\subsection{Model Complexity, Inductive Bias, and Temporal Sequences}
{\color{red}
Our monthly-input results show that tree ensembles do not consistently improve on ElasticNet. This finding supports the use of regularized linear benchmarks in crop forecasting comparisons \citep{Sharmaetal2025}.

The integrated LSTM gains $1.5$ percentage points over integrated ElasticNet at harvest closure. The benchmark compares feasible specifications: monthly tabular inputs limit coefficient expansion, while the recurrent architecture shares weights over decad steps. The resulting ranking evaluates both complete forecasting solutions. \citet{Yinetal2026} provide a basis for the additional temporal-resolution analysis. The 10-day frequency is retained from model selection (Section~\ref{sec:res_frequency_validation}); the final test compares complete forecasting specifications. Tree attenuation in bumper seasons and the small ensemble gain are discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:empirical_results}.
}

\section{Operational Implications for Agrifood Supply Chains}
\label{sec:disc_operational}

{\color{red}
The seasonal pattern suggests different uses for forecast updates. Weak planting-window skill warrants cautious interpretation of early signals. July updates may inform preliminary sourcing and storage scenarios as reproductive weather becomes available. The 2012 case illustrates the potential value of updated supply assessments during reproductive development, before the October reference.

Continuous anomaly forecasts provide a magnitude for these scenarios, beyond a shortfall direction alone. Their use in procurement or freight planning would require uncertainty estimates, acreage information, and timely operational weather inputs. The retrospective experiment does not quantify trading profits, hedging performance, or realized logistical savings.
}

\section{Methodological Boundaries and Limitations}
\label{sec:disc_limitations}

Several boundary conditions govern the interpretation of our findings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reanalysis Latency versus Real-Time Deployment:} ERA5-Land reanalysis is released with an operational latency of approximately 2 to 3 months. Consequently, retrospective benchmarks delineate the theoretical upper bound of weather-driven predictability. Operational deployment requires pairing the model with near-real-time streams (such as ECMWF ERA5T with a 5-day latency) or operational numerical weather prediction analyses.
    \item \textbf{Panel Selection and Survivorship:} Our 75-year panel balances 135 counties continuously from 1951 to 2025 without imputation. This design prioritizes multidecadal temporal depth to capture historical climate extremes, but introduces geographic selection toward high-productivity commercial counties. Results represent commercial rainfed production across the core Midwestern Corn Belt, and cannot be extrapolated directly to marginal or arid fringes.
    {\color{red}\item \textbf{Unobserved Management and Hydrological Drivers:} Irrigation, drainage, planting dates and cultivar choices are not observed as predictors. Residual spatial clustering therefore cannot be attributed to a particular omitted mechanism. County-level differences in accuracy describe the model's empirical limits within this sample.}
    \item \textbf{October Harvest-Timing Contamination:} Across northern study counties, soybean harvesting begins in early October. While synchronizing harvest closure ($H^*=2$) resolves post-harvest contamination for Minnesota, fixed calendar cutoffs in other northern fringes (such as northern Ohio) can still introduce uninformative late-October weather into unadjusted baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Concluding Remarks and Future Research}
\label{sec:disc_conclusions}

{\color{red}
Observed weather adds forecasting information during the growing season, while regularized linear models remain competitive with recurrent networks. Training-only detrending and chronological validation make the incremental contribution explicit. These findings apply to the selected counties and retrospective test period.
}

Promising avenues for future research include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Coupling with Short-Range Numerical Weather Forecasts:} Integrating 10-to-15-day ensemble weather forecasts at active horizons ($H = 4, 3$) to extend the lead time of reproductive heat and moisture stress warnings.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Crop Cross-Commodity Architectures:} Expanding the multi-horizon expanding-window framework to corn, evaluating whether shared agro-climatic representations improve early-season predictability.
    \item \textbf{Phenology-Guided Satellite Fusion:} Incorporating optical reflectance and vegetation fluorescence strictly during reproductive pod-filling (July--August) to calibrate physiological canopy status without compromising early-season lead times.
\end{enumerate}

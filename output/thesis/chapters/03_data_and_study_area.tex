\chapter{Data and Study Area}
\label{chap:data_and_study_area}

The empirical foundation of this thesis rests on two primary data streams spanning the agricultural heartland of the United States Midwest over the 75-year historical horizon from 1951 to 2025: historical county-level soybean yields compiled from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS), and high-resolution gridded surface weather reanalysis extracted from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) ERA5-Land dataset.

\section{Agronomic Setting and County Panel Assembly}
\label{sec:data_study_area}

\subsection{County Panel Selection Rationale and Geographic Scope}
\label{sec:data_panel_assembly}

The empirical analysis targets the core agricultural heartland of the United States Corn Belt: Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, and Ohio. Accounting for over half of domestic soybean production, this region is predominantly rainfed (commercial irrigation covers under 5\% of harvested area), exposing realized yields directly to atmospheric variability and seasonal moisture deficits.

County-level soybean yields (bu/ac; $1\text{ bu/ac} \approx 67.25\text{ kg/ha}$) are compiled from USDA NASS Quick Stats. To construct a balanced panel free from artificial distortion, candidate counties were filtered under two strict criteria: (i) \textit{Agronomic Significance}, retaining established producing counties where extensive crop footprints match the spatial scale of gridded reanalysis ($0.1^\circ \approx 9\text{ km}$); and (ii) \textit{Complete Continuity}, enforcing an unbroken 75-year record (1951--2025) under a strict zero-imputation rule to prevent attenuating empirical shock amplitudes. Because our twelve-month predictive framework ($H=12, \dots, 1$) initiates forecasting on October 31 of year $Y-1$, campaign 1951 requires antecedent weather from October 1950 (ERA5-Land commences in January 1950, precluding 1950 yield evaluation).

This protocol isolates a balanced panel of exactly \textbf{135 counties} over 75 years ($135 \times 75 = \mathbf{10{,}125}$ observations), spanning 12.80 million harvested acres in 2025. Table~\ref{tab:panel_selection} and Figure~\ref{fig:study_area_map} detail the spatial coverage across Illinois (28 counties), Indiana (20), Iowa (35), Minnesota (19), Missouri (6), and Ohio (27).

\subsection{Soybean Phenology and Critical Agro-Climatic Sensitivity Windows}
\label{sec:data_phenology}

Soybean development spans six months from spring sowing to autumn harvest, exhibiting non-linear vulnerability to environmental extremes across four distinct operational phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sowing and Emergence (April--May):} Germination requires soil temperatures above $10^\circ\text{C}$. Excessive spring precipitation and saturated cold seedbeds delay planting into June, compressing the vegetative cycle and depressing potential yield \citep{Lobell2014}.
    \item \textbf{Vegetative Growth (June, V1--Vn):} Thermal accumulation ($GDD$) drives rapid canopy closure and taproot penetration ($7$--$28\text{ cm}$). Moderate early moisture deficits stimulate root deepening, whereas severe drought or persistent waterlogging curtails photosynthetic leaf area.
    \item \textbf{Reproductive Peak and Pod-Filling (July--August, R1--R6):} This window forms the decisive biological bottleneck \citep{Schlenker2009}. Flowering and pod setting in July (R1--R3) are acutely vulnerable to heat stress ($T_{\text{max}} > 30^\circ\text{C}$) and evaporative demand ($VPD > 1.5\text{ kPa}$), which induce pollen sterility and floral abortion. Pod filling in August (R4--R6) coincides with peak transpirational water demand; moisture deficits trigger leaf senescence and pod abortion, while adequate root-zone moisture maximizes final seed weight.
    \item \textbf{Maturation and Harvest (September--October, R7--R8):} Upon physiological maturity (R7), dry matter accumulation ceases. Weather during this stage dictates field trafficability, combine efficiency, and harvest losses; early sub-zero freezes before R7 halt grain filling prematurely.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis of County Soybean Yields (1951--2025)}
\label{sec:data_eda}

\subsection{Secular Technological Trend, Productivity Tripling, and Anomaly Decomposition}
\label{sec:data_yield_decomposition}

To clarify the physical drivers of historical crop variation and formalize the empirical target, realized county soybean yield $y_{c,t}$ (bu/ac for county $c$ and year $t \in \{1951, \dots, 2025\}$) is decomposed into two orthogonal components:
\begin{equation}
    y_{c,t} = \tau_{c,t} + \epsilon_{c,t}
    \label{eq:yield_decomp_ch3}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{c,t}$ represents the deterministic secular trend driven by cumulative technological, agronomic, and genetic innovations, and $\epsilon_{c,t}$ denotes the stochastic shock anomaly induced by intra-seasonal atmospheric forcing and environmental extremes.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/fig_yield_trajectory}
    \caption{Multidecadal county soybean yield trajectories and secular trend across 135 Midwestern counties (1951--2025). Light lines represent individual county histories; the solid blue line denotes the annual cross-sectional panel mean; the dashed line represents the secular OLS trend; and markers highlight major historical climatic shock anomalies.}
    \label{fig:yield_trajectory}
\end{figure}

The secular baseline $\tau_{c,t}$ captures steady technological progress, including breeding advancements (improved lodging resistance, optimized maturity groups), transgenic traits, farm mechanization, subsurface tile drainage, and precision crop inputs. Across the 75-year panel, regional soybean yields expanded from an unweighted mean of $22.77\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 1950s to $57.31\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 2020s, a net gain of $+151.7\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:decadal_yields}). A pooled linear secular trend accounts for over 81\% of total county-year yield variance ($R^2 = 0.8122$, $N = 10{,}125$). Aggregated to the annual regional mean ($N = 75$), an ordinary least squares regression yields an average rate of progress of $+0.484\text{ bu/ac/year}$ ($R^2 = 0.9323$):
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_t = 0.484 \cdot t - 925.46 \quad (t = 1951, \dots, 2025)
    \label{eq:panel_trend}
\end{equation}

\input{tables/tab_decadal_yields}

From a supply-chain and modeling perspective, separating $\tau_{c,t}$ from $\epsilon_{c,t}$ is crucial. While secular growth is readily anticipated by market actors, systemic commodity volatility and supply shortfalls are driven by the weather-induced anomaly $\epsilon_{c,t}$. Furthermore, because baseline yields have nearly tripled, a given percentage drought shock corresponds to a substantially larger physical volume loss today than in the 1950s.

\input{tables/tab_detrending_comparison}

As econometrically demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:detrending_comparison}, Linear OLS is chosen over higher-order polynomials and flexible filters. Penalized model selection via the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) favors linear detrending across $65.9\%$ of individual counties ($89/135$). Crucially, in out-of-sample expanding-window forecasting ($6{,}075$ county-year evaluations), Linear OLS minimizes generalization error ($5.889\text{ bu/ac}$ vs $6.030$ for quadratic and $6.385$ for cubic), eliminating the erratic endpoint oscillations (Runge's phenomenon) that distort polynomial extrapolations. Moreover, linear residuals are strictly stationary ($I(0)$, ADF $p < 0.001$), preserving true climatic shock signals that dynamic filters (e.g., Hodrick-Prescott) erroneously absorb into the secular baseline. Detrending parameters are re-estimated strictly within the training partition of each expanding window.

Modern cultivars, drainage, and chemical inputs have dampened \textit{relative} vulnerability to localized stress. However, because yield baselines tripled, the \textit{absolute} physical volume shortfall during regional droughts is substantially larger today, elevating systemic risk for grain elevators, barge transport, and processors.

\subsection{Macro-Climatic Historical Yield Shocks and Biological Asymmetry}
\label{sec:data_yield_shocks}

The 75-year panel captures severe macro-climatic shock campaigns that serve as out-of-sample benchmark years. Using an anomaly threshold of $|\epsilon_t / \hat{y}_t| \ge 10\%$, Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks} classifies historical extreme campaigns into shortfall and bumper regimes.

\input{tables/tab_yield_shocks}

The distribution of yield anomalies exhibits pronounced \textbf{biological asymmetry}. Positive bumper harvests cluster tightly within a narrow ceiling ($+10.1\%$ to $+11.9\%$; e.g., 1952, 1961, 1994, 2016, 2021), constrained by asymptotic biophysical limits (RuBisCO carboxylation kinetics and radiation interception). Conversely, adverse abiotic stressors possess no physiological floor short of total crop loss, creating an elongated lower tail reaching $-19.7\%$ (1974), $-21.6\%$ (2003), and $-25.6\%$ (1988), with severe negative skewness ($-1.12$, Jarque-Bera $p < 0.001$). As cataloged in Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks}, these shortfalls encompass compound thermal-drought stress (1983, 1988, 2012), pluvial waterlogging and root anoxia (1993), and planting delays compounded by early frost (1974). Because synoptic weather systems span entire subregions, cross-county yield correlations exceed $0.75$, necessitating strict year-based temporal cross-validation to prevent spatial leakage \citep{Sweetetal2023}.

\section{Atmospheric Reanalysis: ECMWF ERA5-Land}
\label{sec:data_era5}

\subsection{Methodological Rationale for a Weather-Only Scope}
\label{sec:data_weather_only_rationale}

The empirical predictive framework restricts dynamic predictive inputs strictly to gridded surface weather from ECMWF ERA5-Land \citep{MunozSabater2021}, which provides continuous, physically consistent hourly meteorological fields at $0.1^\circ \times 0.1^\circ$ resolution ($\approx 9\text{ km}$ across the Midwest), free from station-discontinuation artifacts and spatial interpolation gaps.

As established in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review}, this weather-only design is dictated by operational forecasting constraints and multidecadal data integrity. Farm-level management records (planting dates, fertilizer adoptions) are compiled ex-post with multi-month reporting lags, rendering them inadmissible for real-time within-season forecasting. Satellite vegetation indices (MODIS/Sentinel) lack pre-2000 historical depth and exhibit physiological latency that provides zero early-season skill ($H \ge 4$). Static soil attributes (SSURGO) exhibit zero interannual variance ($\text{Var}(X_{it}) = 0$), and dynamical climate forecasts (SEAS5) inject systematic drift and forecast biases. Restricting dynamic inputs to observed surface reanalysis establishes the empirical upper bound of predictability attributable strictly to realized atmospheric forcing.

\subsection{Spatial Aggregation: Exact Planar Polygon Intersection}

To map gridded reanalysis fields into irregularly shaped administrative county polygons, an exact planar polygon-intersection geometry was computed (\texttt{spatial\_weights.csv}). Across the 135 counties and the ERA5-Land $0.1^\circ$ grid, 3,344 unique intersection polygons are identified with normalized area weights:
\begin{equation}
    w_{c,g} = \frac{\text{Area}(c \cap g)}{\text{Area}(c)}, \quad \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_c} w_{c,g} = 1
    \label{eq:spatial_weight}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Area}(c \cap g)$ is the planar intersection area between county polygon $c$ and grid cell $g$, and $\mathcal{G}_c$ denotes the set of intersecting grid cells.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:era5_spatial_structure}, this area-weighted polygon integration replaces naive centroid sampling, preserving the total atmospheric mass, radiation, and precipitation volume falling within each county polygon without spatial distortion.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_era5_spatial_structure}
    \caption{Spatial architecture and area-weighted grid discretization of ERA5-Land reanalysis. Panel (a) shows the regional study domain across Illinois ($N = 28$ study counties), highlighting Tazewell County. Panel (b) details the micro-scale grid discretization over Tazewell County, illustrating the intersection between the regular $0.1^\circ \times 0.1^\circ$ ($\approx 9\text{ km}$) cardinal grid mesh and the irregular county boundary formed by the Illinois River. Grid cells are weighted strictly by their planar intersection area ($w_{c,g} = \text{Area}(c \cap g) / \text{Area}(c)$, summing to $100\%$), preserving total precipitation volume and heat fluxes across administrative boundaries without centroid sampling distortion.}
    \label{fig:era5_spatial_structure}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Temporal Aggregation and Crop Campaign Structure}

Hourly ERA5-Land fields are spatially aggregated via Equation~\eqref{eq:spatial_weight} into daily surface meteorological states and flux totals (Table~\ref{tab:weather_variables}): thermodynamic states ($T_{\text{mean}}, T_{\text{max}}, T_{\text{min}}, T_{\text{dew}}, T_{\text{skt}}$), radiative and water fluxes (precipitation $P$, solar $SSRD$, thermal $STRD$), boundary layer wind dynamics ($SP, u_{10}, v_{10}, WS_{10}$), and multilayer pedological profiles (soil temperature $STL_1$--$STL_4$ and volumetric soil moisture $SM_1$--$SM_4$ across four depth tiers from $0$ to $289\text{ cm}$).

For each crop year $Y$, daily weather processing is structured over a 13-month campaign spanning from October 1 of year $Y-1$ to October 31 of year $Y$ (397 consecutive days). This temporal framework aligns with the 12 progressive monthly forecast horizons ($H=12, \dots, 1$) evaluated on the last day of each month, capturing antecedent overwinter soil moisture recharge and growing-season atmospheric forcing without forward temporal leakage.

\subsection{Derived Agrometeorological Variables and Bioclimatic Indicators}
\label{sec:data_derived_weather}

Because crop physiological response functions operate non-linearly on compound atmospheric-hydrological demands rather than isolated averages \citep{Schlenker2009, Hoffman2020}, raw ERA5-Land fields are processed into primary and derived daily agrometeorological indicators (Table~\ref{tab:weather_variables}).

\input{tables/tab_weather_variables}

\paragraph{Vapor Pressure Deficit ($VPD$).} Atmospheric evaporative demand is parameterized via Vapor Pressure Deficit, which quantifies the drying power of the air. Saturation vapor pressure $e_s(T_{\text{mean}})$ and actual vapor pressure $e_a(T_{\text{dew}})$ (in kPa) are computed via Tetens formulations:
\begin{align}
    e_s(T_{\text{mean}}) &= 0.61078 \exp\left( \frac{17.27 \, T_{\text{mean}}}{T_{\text{mean}} + 237.3} \right), \quad
    e_a(T_{\text{dew}}) = 0.61078 \exp\left( \frac{17.27 \, T_{\text{dew}}}{T_{\text{dew}} + 237.3} \right) \label{eq:tetens} \\
    VPD &= \max\left(0, \, e_s(T_{\text{mean}}) - e_a(T_{\text{dew}})\right) \label{eq:vpd}
\end{align}
Elevated $VPD$ ($>1.5\text{ kPa}$) triggers stomatal closure to prevent xylem cavitation, suppressing transpirational cooling and carbon assimilation during reproductive stages \citep{Grossiord2020, Lobell2014}.

\paragraph{Reference Evapotranspiration ($ET_0$) and Climatic Water Balance ($P - ET_0$).} Potential atmospheric water demand is modeled via the standardized FAO-56 Penman-Monteith equation for a short grass reference canopy \citep{Allen1998}:
\begin{equation}
    ET_0 = \frac{0.408 \, \Delta \, (R_n - G) + \gamma \, \frac{900}{T_{\text{mean}} + 273} \, u_2 \, (e_s - e_a)}{\Delta + \gamma \, (1 + 0.34 \, u_2)}
    \label{eq:penman_monteith}
\end{equation}
where $R_n$ is net surface radiation ($\text{MJ}/\text{m}^2/\text{day}$), $G \approx 0$ is daily soil heat flux density, $u_2$ is 2m wind speed ($\text{m/s}$), $\Delta$ is the saturation vapor pressure slope, and $\gamma$ is the psychrometric constant. Daily climatic water balance tracks atmospheric moisture deficits or recharge surpluses:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{net}} = P - ET_0
    \label{eq:climatic_balance}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Thermal Pacing and Extreme Heat Days ($GDD, HD_{30}, HD_{35}$).} Phenological progression is tracked through Growing Degree Days ($GDD$, base $10^\circ\text{C}$, cap $30^\circ\text{C}$):
\begin{equation}
    GDD = \max\left(0, \, \frac{\min(T_{\text{max}}, 30) + \max(T_{\text{min}}, 10)}{2} - 10\right)
    \label{eq:gdd}
\end{equation}
To capture non-linear thermal damage thresholds \citep{Schlenker2009}, extreme heat exposure is quantified via daily exceedances:
\begin{equation}
    HD_{30} = \max(0, \, T_{\text{max}} - 30), \quad HD_{35} = \max(0, \, T_{\text{max}} - 35)
    \label{eq:heat_days}
\end{equation}
alongside binary daily occurrences $\mathbb{I}(T_{\text{max}} \ge 30^\circ\text{C})$ and $\mathbb{I}(T_{\text{max}} \ge 35^\circ\text{C})$.

\paragraph{Integrated Root-Zone Soil Moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$).} Volumetric soil moisture across discrete depth horizons is aggregated into an integrated column accessible to mature soybean taproots ($0$--$100\text{ cm}$):
\begin{equation}
    SM_{\text{root}} = 0.07 \, SM_1 + 0.21 \, SM_2 + 0.72 \, SM_3
    \label{eq:sm_root}
\end{equation}
weighting by layer thickness ($7\text{ cm}$, $21\text{ cm}$, $72\text{ cm}$). This metric buffers transient surface dry spells and tracks subsoil moisture memory sustaining transpirational uptake during summer rainfall deficits \citep{Vijverberg2023}.

\section{Exploratory Agro-Climatic Analysis and Yield Sensitivity}
\label{sec:data_climate_eda}

Having formalized the deterministic secular trend and identified benchmark yield shocks in Section~\ref{sec:data_eda}, this section investigates the empirical relationships between realized surface meteorological forcing and county-level soybean yield anomalies. Utilizing the 75-year panel of daily county-aggregated ERA5-Land reanalysis across the 135 study counties, we analyze the agro-climatic drivers across three complementary analytical dimensions: (i) multi-decadal synchrony between atmospheric stress apexes and regional yield shocks, (ii) intra-seasonal progression contrasting cumulative physical dosages against dynamic environmental states across benchmark campaigns, and (iii) monthly climate-yield sensitivity profiles across phenological stages to establish the physical basis for predictive lead-time gains.

\subsection{Historical Agro-Climatic Stress and the Yield Shock Nexus (1950--Present)}
\label{sec:data_climatology}

To evaluate how macro-climatic anomalies align with the benchmark yield shocks identified in Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks}, Figure~\ref{fig:weather_climatology} synchronizes the 75-year annual cross-sectional yield anomalies with two primary indicators of atmospheric stress: annual extreme heat stress days ($HD_{30}$, days with $T_{\text{max}} \ge 30^\circ\text{C}$ per county) and midsummer atmospheric evaporative demand ($VPD$, July--August mean).

    \label{fig:weather_climatology}

The synchronized historical record reveals two foundational empirical patterns:

First, severe regional downside shortfalls cluster tightly with historical apexes in compound atmospheric stress. In major drought campaigns—such as 1953, 1983, 1988, and 2012—annual heat-stress days surged past $50$ to $56\text{ days}$ per county, while summer vapor pressure deficit reached historic peaks of $1.2$ to $1.3\text{ kPa}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_climatology}b). Under these compound regimes, high temperatures and elevated evaporative demand co-occur, driving severe vapor pressure deficits that force stomatal closure, terminate transpirational cooling, and accelerate soil moisture depletion during critical reproductive development \citep{Schlenker2009, Lobell2014}. Conversely, prominent bumper harvest clusters (such as 1952, 1961, 1994, 2016, and 2021) consistently coincide with suppressed heat accumulation ($HD_{30} < 20\text{ days}$) and benign summer evaporative demand ($VPD < 0.9\text{ kPa}$), preserving transpirational hydration throughout reproductive seed fill.

Second, the time series demonstrates that severe downside shocks do not originate solely from thermal drought. Distinct physical mechanisms generate comparable downside yield shortfalls:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Pluvial Saturation and Waterlogging (e.g., 1993):} In 1993 ($-10.5\%$ shortfall), heat days collapsed to a record low ($HD_{30} \approx 8\text{ days}$) and summer $VPD$ reached a multi-decadal minimum ($0.71\text{ kPa}$) under persistent overcast conditions. Yield losses were driven by continuous precipitation excess and prolonged soil saturation, which induce root-zone anoxia and impair nutrient uptake.
    \item \textit{Temporal Sowing Delays and Early Freeze Truncation (e.g., 1974):} In 1974 ($-19.7\%$ shortfall), torrential spring precipitation saturated fields throughout April and May, delaying planting into June. While subsequent summer temperatures remained moderate, the delayed reproductive calendar left immature canopies vulnerable to an unseasonably early Arctic cold front in late September, terminating dry-matter accumulation before physiological maturity.
    \item \textit{Late-Season Reproductive Deficits (e.g., 2003):} In 2003 ($-21.6\%$ shortfall), moderate weather through June supported robust vegetative growth, but was followed by an acute late-summer precipitation deficit and sharp $VPD$ surge directly during the August pod-filling window.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Intra-Seasonal Agro-Climatic Trajectories: Cumulative and Dynamic Shock Archetypes}
\label{sec:data_intra_seasonal_extremes}

While annual aggregates capture macro-level synchrony, predicting yield anomalies requires resolving how weather shocks unfold across the growing season. Atmospheric and hydrological variables operate on distinct physical timescales: moisture supplies and thermal exposure accumulate continuously over the campaign, whereas physiological stress is triggered by instantaneous daily state extremes crossing critical biophysical thresholds.

To capture these dual dynamics without confounding cumulative integrals with instantaneous states, the intra-seasonal empirical analysis is structured into two coordinated, complementary perspectives across the primary growing cycle (April 1 to October 31, DOY 91--304):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cumulative Physical Trajectories (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}):} Tracks the continuous seasonal accumulation of thermal forcing and hydrological budgets across the five standardized phenological development stages established in Section~\ref{sec:data_phenology} (Sowing, Vegetative Growth, Flowering R1--R3, Pod-Filling R4--R6, and Maturity \& Harvest).
    \item \textbf{Non-Cumulative Dynamic State Profiles (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}):} Traces daily 15-day centered rolling physical states—vapor pressure deficit ($VPD$), integrated root-zone soil moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$), and daily maximum temperature ($T_{\text{max}}$)—specifically isolating the Top 3 Best (1952, 2021, 2016) and Top 3 Worst (1988, 2003, 1974) historical campaigns against the 75-year climatological normal envelope.
\end{enumerate}

Bottom panels quantify the share of harvested soybean acreage exposed across water balance classes per year. Color scale is centered at zero via a diverging normalization ($\text{vmin} = -300$, $\text{vcenter} = 0$, $\text{vmax} = +200\text{ mm}$); dashed contour isolines indicate the climatological zero-balance boundary.}

\end{figure}

Cross-referencing the cumulative integrals of Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes} with the dynamic state profiles of Figures~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}--\ref{fig:spatial_shock_comparison} resolves four distinct agro-climatic shock archetypes:

\paragraph{Full-Season Thermal-Drought Desiccation (1988).}
The record 1988 shortfall ($-25.6\%$) represents an extreme compound shock where precipitation deficits established early in May and persisted throughout the season (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}b). Cumulative precipitation stalled at barely $480\text{ mm}$ against an evaporative demand ($\sum ET_0$) exceeding $930\text{ mm}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}c), opening an unprecedented cumulative water deficit of $-457.5\text{ mm}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}d). In the daily state domain (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}), $VPD$ pierced the $1.5\text{ kPa}$ threshold in mid-June and remained between $1.6$ and $1.8\text{ kPa}$ through July, while $T_{\text{max}}$ consistently exceeded $32^\circ\text{C}$. This sustained atmospheric demand rapidly depleted root-zone soil moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$) down to $0.20\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$ by early July, approaching the permanent wilting point and inducing severe floral and pod abortion.

\paragraph{Reproductive-Window Moisture Deficit (2003).}
In sharp contrast to 1988, the 2003 campaign ($-21.6\%$) maintained near-normal cumulative precipitation and moderate temperatures through May and June (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}a,b). However, directly coincident with flowering and pod filling in late July and August, precipitation ceased abruptly. In Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}a, 15-day rolling $VPD$ spiked above $1.2\text{ kPa}$ in late August, accelerating root-zone soil moisture extraction from $0.34$ down to $0.24\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$ during seed swelling. This late-season moisture deficit demonstrates that robust early vegetative growth offers no protection when atmospheric drought strikes the narrow reproductive window.

\paragraph{Temporal Boundary and Frost Truncation (1974).}
The 1974 shortfall ($-19.7\%$) exemplifies how adverse operational timing can severely impair yields in the absence of summer drought. In spring (April--May), excessive rainfall maintained $SM_{\text{root}}$ above regional field capacity ($>0.38\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$, Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}b), preventing field machinery access and delaying planting by three to four weeks. While midsummer temperatures and water balances were benign (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}d), this initial operational delay shifted reproductive development late into August and September. In late September, an unseasonably early Arctic cold front caused daily maximum temperatures to plunge abruptly below seasonal norms (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}c), freezing the immature canopy and permanently arresting seed filling before physiological maturity.

\paragraph{Pluvial Inundation and Soil Saturation (1993).}
The Great Midwest Flood of 1993 ($-10.5\%$) represents the empirical opposite of drought. Cumulative precipitation exceeded $890\text{ mm}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}b), driving the cumulative climatic water budget to a net surplus of $+158.4\text{ mm}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}d). Extreme heat days were completely suppressed ($\sum HD_{30} = 18\text{ days}$), and $VPD$ hovered below $0.8\text{ kPa}$. Under continuous overcast precipitation, root-zone soils remained pinned at saturation, demonstrating that excess water induces substantial yield penalties through prolonged root anoxia and nutrient leaching.

\paragraph{Agro-Climatic Optima and Bumper Harvests (1952, 2016, 2021).}
The top positive yield anomalies—1952 (+11.9\%), 2021 (+10.8\%), and 2016 (+10.8\%)—exhibit an ideal agro-climatic balance. In Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}, 15-day rolling maximum temperatures remained bounded between $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $28^\circ\text{C}$ throughout the summer, while cumulative heat stress days plateaued below 20--30 days (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}a). Atmospheric evaporative demand remained within the transpirational optimum ($VPD \approx 0.75$--$1.0\text{ kPa}$), supporting continuous carbon assimilation without midday stomatal closure. Timely precipitation events maintained root-zone soil moisture securely within the favorable range ($0.27$--$0.34\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$) throughout the critical August pod-filling window, preventing moisture stress while avoiding waterlogging.

\subsection{Phenological Climate-Yield Sensitivity and Lead-Time Dynamics}
\label{sec:data_pheno_sensitivity}

To quantify how the statistical dependence between atmospheric forcing and final yield evolves across the crop lifecycle, Figure~\ref{fig:climate_yield_sensitivity} presents bivariate Pearson correlation coefficients ($r$) between detrended county yield anomalies ($\epsilon_{c,t}$) and monthly atmospheric anomalies across all 135 study counties over the 75-year panel.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/fig_climate_yield_sensitivity}
    \caption{Phenological sensitivity of county soybean yields to monthly atmospheric anomalies across the crop cycle (April--October). Bars depict cross-sectional Pearson correlation coefficients ($r$) between detrended yield anomalies ($\epsilon_{c,t}$) and monthly anomalies of maximum temperature ($T_{\text{max}}$), vapor pressure deficit ($VPD$), precipitation ($P$), and root-zone soil moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$). Sensitivity is minimal during pre-sowing and emergence (April--May), shifts in June, peaks during pod filling (August), and decays rapidly upon physiological maturity in autumn.}
    \label{fig:climate_yield_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

The empirical correlation profiles display four distinct phenological sensitivity regimes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-Sowing and Emergence Window (April--May):} Correlation coefficients between weather anomalies and final yield are low ($|r| < 0.10$). Precipitation and root-zone soil moisture exhibit slightly negative correlations ($r \approx -0.06$ to $-0.09$), reflecting the operational penalty of excessive spring moisture, which delays planting and promotes soil compaction. Ambient temperature exerts negligible influence during this preparatory phase.
    \item \textbf{Vegetative Growth and Canopy Closure (June):} As crops establish leaf area and root depth, environmental sensitivities shift toward positive moisture dependency. However, correlation magnitudes remain modest ($r \approx +0.12$ for soil moisture; $r \approx -0.14$ for $VPD$), indicating that soybeans possess sufficient vegetative plasticity to recover from early aberrations if subsequent reproductive conditions are favorable.
    \item \textbf{Peak Reproductive Sensitivity (July--August):} Atmospheric sensitivity escalates sharply during flowering (July, R1--R3) and reaches its annual maximum during pod development and seed filling (August, R4--R6). In August, the correlation between yield anomalies and root-zone soil moisture surges to $r = +0.43$, accompanied by precipitation at $r = +0.38$. Concurrently, atmospheric stress metrics exhibit severe negative correlations: $VPD$ reaches $r = -0.46$ and $T_{\text{max}}$ reaches $r = -0.41$. The co-occurrence of maximum transpirational demand and reproductive seed swelling makes August weather the single most influential determinant of interannual yield variance.
    \item \textbf{Maturation and Harvest (September--October):} Following the completion of seed fill in late August, crops reach physiological maturity (R7--R8) and undergo canopy senescence. Consequently, correlation coefficients collapse toward zero ($|r| \le 0.05$ across all four variables). Meteorological conditions in September and October affect field workable days and combine efficiency, but exert negligible biological impact on realized seed quantity.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Methodological Implications for Progressive Lead-Time Forecasting.}
The empirical sensitivity profiles synthesized in Figure~\ref{fig:climate_yield_sensitivity} provide the direct scientific justification for the twelve progressive monthly forecast horizons ($H = 12, \dots, 1$) formalized in Chapter~4. 

At long pre-sowing lead times ($H = 12, \dots, 6$, October through April), atmospheric forcings exhibit low direct correlation with final yield anomalies; predictive skill at these horizons must rely primarily on persistent antecedent root-zone soil moisture recharge and multi-month hydrological memory \citep{Vijverberg2023}. As the forecast horizon advances through vegetative development ($H = 5, 4$, May and June), incoming meteorological information gradually updates crop potential, but forecasts remain subject to substantial downstream uncertainty. 

The primary gain in physical predictability occurs at mid-season and late-summer horizons ($H = 3, 2$, July 31 and August 31), when predictive models ingest realized atmospheric stress during the critical flowering and pod-fill windows. Finally, at horizon $H = 1$ (September 30), biological yield formation is essentially complete; remaining uncertainty is dominated by localized combine harvest losses and late frost hazards. The mathematical formalization of these twelve discrete horizons, feature extraction pipelines, and expanding-window validation protocols is developed in Chapter~4.

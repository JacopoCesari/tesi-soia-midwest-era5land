The empirical analysis in this thesis rests on two primary data streams spanning the United States Midwest from 1951 to 2025: county-level soybean yields from the USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service (NASS), and high-resolution gridded surface weather from the ECMWF ERA5-Land reanalysis.

\section{Agronomic Setting and County Panel Assembly}
\label{sec:data_study_area}

\subsection{County Panel Selection Rationale and Geographic Scope}
\label{sec:data_panel_assembly}

We focus on six core states of the United States Corn Belt: Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, and Ohio. These states represent the primary production center for domestic soybeans, accounting for the highest harvest volumes and acreage density in the country. Crop production across this domain is predominantly rainfed, with commercial irrigation covering less than 5\% of harvested area \citep{Schlenker2009}. Realized yields directly reflect natural weather variability, temperature extremes, and seasonal moisture deficits.

We compile county-level soybean yields (in bushels per acre, $1\text{ bu/ac} \approx 67.25\text{ kg/ha}$) from the USDA NASS Quick Stats database. To assemble a balanced and robust panel, we apply two selection criteria: (i) an unbroken 75-year annual historical record from 1951 to 2025 without missing data, enforcing a zero-imputation protocol to preserve genuine historical shock amplitudes; and (ii) high agricultural harvested density. 

High harvested density is essential for spatial weather aggregation. Because we aggregate meteorological fields from ERA5-Land to county boundaries via area-weighted polygon averaging, focusing on agricultural counties ensures that county-wide weather variables genuinely represent cropland microclimates.

Because our 12-month predictive horizon ($H = 12, \dots, 1$) begins on October 31 of year $Y-1$ and ERA5-Land starts in January 1950, 1951 is the earliest campaign with complete antecedent weather coverage, precluding 1950 yields.

This protocol isolates a balanced panel of \textbf{135 counties} over 75 years ($135 \times 75 = \mathbf{10{,}125}$ observations). Table~\ref{tab:panel_selection} and Figure~\ref{fig:study_area_map} detail their spatial coverage across the primary agro-climatic subregions of the Corn Belt.

\subsection{Soybean Phenology and Critical Agro-Climatic Sensitivity Windows}
\label{sec:data_phenology}

Soybean development spans six months from spring sowing to autumn harvest, following four operational growth phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sowing and Emergence (April--May):} Germination requires soil temperatures consistently above $10^\circ\text{C}$. Persistent spring precipitation and cold seedbeds delay field operations and planting into late May or June, which compresses the vegetative window and lowers potential yield.
    \item \textbf{Vegetative Growth (June):} Thermal accumulation, quantified via Growing Degree Days ($GDD$), promotes rapid canopy closure and taproot elongation. Soybeans exhibit notable physiological plasticity during this stage; moderate moisture variations are well tolerated as root systems expand downward.
    \item \textbf{Flowering, Pod Development, and Seed Filling (July--August):} This midsummer window represents the critical biological sensitivity bottleneck \citep{Schlenker2009}. Flowering and pod setting in July are acutely sensitive to heat stress ($T_{\text{max}} > 30^\circ\text{C}$) and high evaporative demand ($VPD > 1.5\text{ kPa}$), which trigger floral abortion. In August, transpirational water demand reaches its seasonal peak; moisture deficits accelerate canopy senescence and impair seed swelling, whereas adequate soil moisture maximizes final seed weight.
    \item \textbf{Maturation and Harvest (September--October):} Upon reaching physiological maturity in September, dry matter accumulation ceases. Weather conditions during autumn govern field dry-down and combine efficiency; unseasonable early freezes arrest grain filling, while heavy rainfall impedes harvest operations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis of County Soybean Yields (1951--2025)}
\label{sec:data_eda}

\subsection{Secular Technological Trend, Productivity Tripling, and Anomaly Decomposition}
\label{sec:data_yield_decomposition}

To isolate climatic shocks from secular agronomic gains, we formally decompose realized county soybean yield $y_{c,t}$ (bu/ac for county $c$ and year $t \in \{1951, \dots, 2025\}$) into two additive components:
\begin{equation}
    y_{c,t} = \tau_{c,t} + \epsilon_{c,t}
    \label{eq:yield_decomp_ch3}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{c,t}$ represents the deterministic secular trend driven by technological, agronomic, and genetic progress, and $\epsilon_{c,t}$ denotes the stochastic yield anomaly induced by weather variability and environmental stress.

Our machine learning models in Chapter~4 are trained exclusively to predict the detrended yield anomaly $\epsilon_{c,t}$ (or the detrended baseline $\hat{y}_{c,t}^{\text{detrended}} = \bar{y}_c + \epsilon_{c,t}$), removing the predictable secular trend. In operational validation, we estimate trend parameters strictly on historical training folds to prevent look-ahead bias; here we examine full-sample properties to evaluate trend specifications.

The secular baseline $\tau_{c,t}$ captures technological progress, including advanced breeding, transgenic traits, mechanization, and precision farming inputs. Across the 75-year panel, regional average soybean yields expanded from $22.77\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 1950s to $57.31\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 2020s (+151.7\%, Table~\ref{tab:decadal_yields}). At the regional annual level ($N = 75$), an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression indicates an average gain of $+0.484\text{ bu/ac/year}$ ($R^2 = 0.9323$, Figure~\ref{fig:yield_trajectory}):
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_t = 0.484 \cdot t - 925.46 \quad (t = 1951, \dots, 2025)
    \label{eq:panel_trend}
\end{equation}

The high explanatory power ($R^2 = 0.9323$) at the regional aggregate level reflects the deterministic multidecadal drift of technological innovation averaged across the entire Corn Belt. However, this macro-level fit is an aggregation artifact resulting from spatial smoothing across 135 counties, where localized climatic stresses cancel each other out. Commercial grain merchandisers, transport logistics networks, and crop insurance underwriters do not transact regional aggregate averages; physical exposure and delivery obligations occur at the county scale.

Furthermore, our modeling framework in Chapter~4 explicitly operates on geographically disaggregated entities (state and county tiers), fitting entity-specific detrending series to capture localized technological drift (e.g., Minnesota expanding at $+0.509\text{ bu/ac/year}$ versus Missouri at $+0.386\text{ bu/ac/year}$, Section~\ref{sec:data_spatial_heterogeneity}). A single regional trend leaves wide, heteroskedastic county-level variance unexplained. Across the full panel ($N = 10{,}125$), pooled variance explained falls to $R^2 = 0.8122$, leaving an out-of-sample prediction error of $5.889\text{ bu/ac}$ that no static time trend can capture.

Localized variance is non-stationary and expands over time. As illustrated by the shaded 5th--95th percentile ribbon in Figure~\ref{fig:yield_trajectory} and detailed in Table~\ref{tab:decadal_yields}, the 90\% cross-county dispersion envelope expanded from $15.12\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 1950s to $23.05\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 2020s ($+52.4\%$), with total annual cross-county spreads ($y_{\text{max}} - y_{\text{min}}$) widening from $24.28\text{ bu/ac}$ to $41.58\text{ bu/ac}$ ($+71.3\%$). While the relative coefficient of variation ($CV_t$) contracted from $22.0\%$ to $13.2\%$ due to the tripling of mean baseline yields, absolute physical volumetric exposure has amplified. A $13\%$ yield deficit on a modern $60\text{ bu/ac}$ crop represents nearly $8\text{ bu/ac}$ in missing physical grain, compared to $5\text{ bu/ac}$ on a 1950s baseline.

Consequently, isolating and forecasting the stochastic, weather-induced anomaly $\epsilon_{c,t}$ at the local scale represents the core scientific objective of this thesis, rendering simple linear trend extrapolation structurally inadequate for agricultural supply management.

\subsection{Macro-Climatic Historical Yield Shocks and Biological Asymmetry}
\label{sec:data_yield_shocks}

The 75-year panel contains severe macro-climatic shock campaigns that serve as out-of-sample benchmark cohorts. Using an anomaly threshold of $|\epsilon_t / \hat{y}_t| \ge 10\%$, Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks} classifies historical extreme campaigns into shortfall and bumper regimes.

\input{tables/tab_yield_shocks}

The empirical distribution of yield anomalies exhibits pronounced \textbf{biological asymmetry}. Favorable harvest campaigns cluster within a narrow upper band ($+10.1\%$ to $+11.9\%$; e.g., 1952, 1961, 1994, 2016, 2021), constrained by the biophysical ceiling of radiation use efficiency. In contrast, severe abiotic stressors possess no physiological floor short of complete crop failure. This generates an elongated negative tail reaching $-19.7\%$ (1974), $-21.6\%$ (2003), and $-25.6\%$ (1988), with notable negative skewness ($-1.12$, Jarque-Bera $p < 0.001$). 

As documented in Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks}, these physical yield departures translate directly into commercial price volatility. Severe supply contractions induce aggressive rationing rallies during reproductive stress windows, with 12-month farm-gate price swings exceeding $+30\%$ in 1988 and $+50\%$ in 1974 and 2003, culminating in the 2012 record pricing of $\$14.40\text{/bu}$. Conversely, supply abundance during bumper harvests depresses post-harvest cash prices by up to $-19.3\%$ (1994), reinforcing the operational value of early agro-climatic detection before harvest completion.

While major shortfalls are primarily associated with midsummer drought and heat, the historical record also contains losses from unseasonal frost and excessive wetness. We analyze these daily mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:data_climate_eda}. Because synoptic weather shocks span multi-state regions, cross-county yield correlations exceed $0.75$, necessitating strict expanding-window temporal validation to prevent spatial information leakage \citep{Sweetetal2023}.

\section{Surface Weather Data: ECMWF ERA5-Land}
\label{sec:data_era5}

\subsection{Dataset Characteristics and Meteorological Ingestion}
\label{sec:data_era5_characteristics}

We derive meteorological inputs from ECMWF ERA5-Land, which provides continuous hourly surface weather fields at a $0.1^\circ \times 0.1^\circ$ resolution ($\approx 9\text{ km}$) from 1950 to 2025.

The hourly ECMWF ERA5-Land reanalysis provides a comprehensive suite of atmospheric and land-surface fields. From this broader catalogue, raw variables such as surface pressure ($SP$) and 10-meter horizontal wind vectors ($u_{10}, v_{10}$) serve strictly as intermediate thermodynamic inputs to compute standardized FAO-56 Penman-Monteith reference evapotranspiration ($ET_0$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:penman_monteith}) and vapor pressure deficit ($VPD$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:vpd}). Deep soil horizons beyond mature soybean taproot depths ($SM_4 > 1\text{ m}$), soil temperatures (strongly collinear with ambient air temperatures), and snowpack dynamics are excluded a priori to prevent dimensionality inflation and avoid introducing uninformative predictors during the summer vegetative cycle.

In total, we retain a parsimonious set of \textbf{16 daily agrometeorological indicators} capturing the biophysical determinants of Midwestern soybean yields (Table~\ref{tab:weather_variables}): 9 primary surface fields describing thermal pacing, precipitation, solar radiation, and root-accessible soil water layers (Panel~A), and 7 derived bioclimatic indicators isolating atmospheric drying power, cumulative water deficits, and compound heat stress (Panel~B).

\input{tables/tab_weather_variables}

\subsection{Derived Agrometeorological Variables and Bioclimatic Indicators}
\label{sec:data_derived_weather}

Crop yields respond non-linearly to compound atmospheric-hydrological stress \citep{Schlenker2009}. We therefore transform daily meteorological fields into targeted bioclimatic indicators (Table~\ref{tab:weather_variables}, Panel~B).

\paragraph{Vapor Pressure Deficit ($VPD$).} Atmospheric evaporative demand is parameterized via Vapor Pressure Deficit, which quantifies the drying power of the air. Saturation vapor pressure $e_s(T_{\text{mean}})$ and actual vapor pressure $e_a(T_{\text{dew}})$ (in kPa) are computed via Tetens formulations:
\begin{align}
    e_s(T_{\text{mean}}) &= 0.61078 \exp\left( \frac{17.27 \, T_{\text{mean}}}{T_{\text{mean}} + 237.3} \right), \quad
    e_a(T_{\text{dew}}) = 0.61078 \exp\left( \frac{17.27 \, T_{\text{dew}}}{T_{\text{dew}} + 237.3} \right) \label{eq:tetens} \\
    VPD &= \max\left(0, \, e_s(T_{\text{mean}}) - e_a(T_{\text{dew}})\right) \label{eq:vpd}
\end{align}
Elevated $VPD$ ($>1.5\text{ kPa}$) triggers stomatal closure to prevent dehydration, suppressing transpirational cooling and carbon assimilation during reproductive stages.

\paragraph{Reference Evapotranspiration ($ET_0$) and Climatic Water Balance ($P - ET_0$).} We model potential atmospheric water demand via the standardized FAO-56 Penman-Monteith equation for a short grass reference canopy:
\begin{equation}
    ET_0 = \frac{0.408 \, \Delta \, (R_n - G) + \gamma \, \frac{900}{T_{\text{mean}} + 273} \, u_2 \, (e_s - e_a)}{\Delta + \gamma \, (1 + 0.34 \, u_2)}
    \label{eq:penman_monteith}
\end{equation}
where $R_n$ is net surface radiation ($\text{MJ}/\text{m}^2/\text{day}$), $G \approx 0$ is daily soil heat flux density, $u_2$ is 2m wind speed ($\text{m/s}$), $\Delta$ is the saturation vapor pressure slope, and $\gamma$ is the psychrometric constant. Daily climatic water balance tracks atmospheric moisture deficits or recharge surpluses:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{net}} = P - ET_0
    \label{eq:climatic_balance}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Thermal Pacing and Extreme Heat Days ($GDD, HD_{30}, HD_{35}$).} We track phenological progression through Growing Degree Days ($GDD$, base $10^\circ\text{C}$, cap $30^\circ\text{C}$):
\begin{equation}
    GDD = \max\left(0, \, \frac{\min(T_{\text{max}}, 30) + \max(T_{\text{min}}, 10)}{2} - 10\right)
    \label{eq:gdd}
\end{equation}
To capture non-linear thermal damage thresholds \citep{Schlenker2009}, we quantify extreme heat exposure via daily exceedances:
\begin{equation}
    HD_{30} = \max(0, \, T_{\text{max}} - 30), \quad HD_{35} = \max(0, \, T_{\text{max}} - 35)
    \label{eq:heat_days}
\end{equation}
alongside binary daily occurrences $\mathbb{I}(T_{\text{max}} \ge 30^\circ\text{C})$ and $\mathbb{I}(T_{\text{max}} \ge 35^\circ\text{C})$.

\paragraph{Integrated Root-Zone Soil Moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$).} We aggregate volumetric soil moisture across discrete depth horizons into an integrated column accessible to mature soybean taproots ($0$--$100\text{ cm}$):
\begin{equation}
    SM_{\text{root}} = 0.07 \, SM_1 + 0.21 \, SM_2 + 0.72 \, SM_3
    \label{eq:sm_root}
\end{equation}
weighting by layer thickness ($7\text{ cm}$, $21\text{ cm}$, $72\text{ cm}$). This metric buffers transient surface dry spells and tracks subsoil moisture memory sustaining transpirational uptake during summer rainfall deficits \citep{Vijverberg2023}.

\subsection{Spatial Aggregation: Exact Planar Polygon Intersection}
\label{sec:data_spatial_aggregation}

To map gridded weather fields into administrative county polygons, we compute exact planar polygon intersections (\texttt{spatial\_weights.csv}). Across the 135 study counties and the ERA5-Land $0.1^\circ$ grid mesh, we identify 3,344 unique intersection polygons with normalized area weights:
\begin{equation}
    w_{c,g} = \frac{\text{Area}(c \cap g)}{\text{Area}(c)}, \quad \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_c} w_{c,g} = 1
    \label{eq:spatial_weight}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Area}(c \cap g)$ is the planar intersection area between county polygon $c$ and grid cell $g$, and $\mathcal{G}_c$ denotes the set of intersecting grid cells.

As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:era5_spatial_structure}, this area-weighted polygon integration replaces naive centroid sampling, preserving the total precipitation volume, radiation, and atmospheric mass falling within each county polygon without spatial distortion.

\subsection{Temporal Aggregation and Crop Campaign Structure}
\label{sec:data_temporal_aggregation}

We spatially integrate hourly ERA5-Land fields across county polygons via Equation~\eqref{eq:spatial_weight} and aggregate them into daily meteorological series. For each crop year $Y$, daily weather processing is structured over a 13-month campaign spanning from October 1 of year $Y-1$ to October 31 of year $Y$ (397 consecutive days). This temporal framework aligns with our twelve progressive monthly forecast horizons ($H=12, \dots, 1$) evaluated on the last day of each month, capturing antecedent overwinter soil moisture recharge and in-season atmospheric forcing without look-ahead leakage.

\section{Exploratory Agro-Climatic Analysis and Yield Sensitivity}
\label{sec:data_climate_eda}

We now examine the empirical relationships between realized surface meteorological forcing and county-level soybean yield anomalies across the 75-year panel. We analyze these drivers across two complementary operational dimensions: (i) intra-seasonal progression contrasting cumulative physical dosages against dynamic environmental states across benchmark shortfall and bumper campaigns, supported by regional spatial contrasts, and (ii) monthly climate-yield sensitivity profiles across developmental stages to establish the physical basis for multi-horizon sequential forecasting.

\subsection{Intra-Seasonal Agro-Climatic Trajectories: Cumulative and Dynamic Shock Archetypes}
\label{sec:data_intra_seasonal_extremes}

Predicting yield anomalies requires resolving how weather shocks unfold across the growing season. Atmospheric and hydrological variables operate on distinct physical timescales: moisture supplies and thermal exposure accumulate continuously, whereas physiological stress is triggered by daily state extremes crossing critical biophysical thresholds.

To capture these dual dynamics, we evaluate intra-seasonal weather across the primary growing cycle (April 1 to October 31, DOY 91--304) using two coordinated perspectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Cumulative Physical Trajectories (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}):} Tracks continuous seasonal accumulation of thermal forcing and hydrological budgets across standardized developmental stages (Sowing, Vegetative Growth, Flowering R1--R3, Pod-Filling R4--R6, and Maturity \& Harvest).
    \item \textbf{Non-Cumulative Dynamic State Profiles (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}):} Traces daily 15-day centered rolling physical states---vapor pressure deficit ($VPD$), integrated root-zone soil moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$), and daily maximum temperature ($T_{\text{max}}$)---contrasting benchmark shortfall campaigns (2012 Flash Drought, 1993 Midwest Flood) and prominent bumper harvests (2016 Optimum Buffer, 1994 Ideal Balance) against the 75-year climatological normal envelope.
\end{enumerate}

Cross-referencing the cumulative trajectories of Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}, the dynamic state profiles of Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}, and the spatial contrast in Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_shock_comparison} highlights three foundational physical archetypes across the four benchmark campaigns:

\paragraph{Midsummer Flash Drought and Compound Stress (2012).}
The 2012 shortfall ($-11.7\%$) represents the canonical modern summer flash drought. In Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}a, cumulative heat days accelerated abruptly during July, while precipitation lagged far behind evaporative demand (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}b,c). In Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}, 15-day rolling $VPD$ surged past $1.8\text{ kPa}$ and maximum temperatures exceeded $33^\circ\text{C}$ throughout July, driving integrated root-zone soil moisture down to the $0.18\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$ wilting threshold. As mapped in Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_shock_comparison}a,b, cumulative rainfall collapsed below $80\text{ mm}$ across the central Corn Belt (Iowa and Illinois), while daily maximum temperatures exceeded $33^\circ\text{C}$ across central and southern districts. This compound heat and atmospheric evaporative demand coincided with flowering and pod setting, inducing widespread floral abortion and premature canopy senescence.

\paragraph{Pluvial Inundation and Soil Saturation (1993).}
The Great Midwest Flood of 1993 ($-10.5\%$) represents the diametric physical opposite of drought. Cumulative precipitation exceeded $880\text{ mm}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}b), generating a persistent climatic water surplus ($+158.4\text{ mm}$, Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}d). Heat stress was completely absent ($\sum HD_{30} < 20\text{ days}$), and $VPD$ remained depressed below $0.7\text{ kPa}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}a). Under continuous rainfall and overcast skies, root-zone soils hovered near field capacity ($0.38\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$) throughout the summer, depressing yields through root anoxia, reduced photosynthetic radiation, and impaired nitrogen fixation. This contrast demonstrates that yield vulnerability is non-linear and biphasic: both severe moisture deficits and severe moisture surpluses induce major negative yield shocks.

\paragraph{Agro-Climatic Optima and Bumper Harvests (2016 and 1994).}
The benchmark positive yield anomalies---2016 ($+10.8\%$) and 1994 ($+10.7\%$)---demonstrate optimal biophysical balance. In Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}, 15-day rolling maximum temperatures remained bounded between $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $28^\circ\text{C}$, while cumulative heat stress days plateaued below 30 days (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}a). Atmospheric evaporative demand remained within the transpirational optimum ($VPD \approx 0.75$--$0.90\text{ kPa}$). In 2016, timely August rainfall recharge sustained root-zone moisture near $0.32\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$, as mapped spatially in Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_shock_comparison}c,d. Similarly, 1994 represented a remarkable climatic recovery directly following the 1993 flood, maintaining near-neutral cumulative water balance ($\sum (P - ET_0) \approx -50\text{ mm}$) and moderate thermal regimes that supported prolonged seed filling without lodging or drought.

\subsection{Phenological Climate-Yield Sensitivity and Lead-Time Dynamics}
\label{sec:data_pheno_sensitivity}

To quantify how the statistical dependence between atmospheric forcing and final yield evolves across the crop lifecycle, Figure~\ref{fig:climate_yield_sensitivity} presents bivariate Pearson correlation coefficients ($r$) between detrended county yield anomalies ($\epsilon_{c,t}$) and monthly atmospheric anomalies across all 135 study counties over 75 years.

Bars depict cross-sectional Pearson correlation coefficients ($r$) between detrended yield anomalies ($\epsilon_{c,t}$) and monthly anomalies of maximum temperature ($T_{\text{max}}$), vapor pressure deficit ($VPD$), precipitation ($P$), and root-zone soil moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$). The empirical correlation profiles synthesize into four distinct operational phases across the crop lifecycle, motivating the twelve monthly forecast horizons ($H = 12, \dots, 1$) evaluated on the final day of each month:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-Sowing and Dormancy ($H = 12, \dots, 6$, October 31 to April 30):} Direct monthly correlations with final yield remain low ($|r| < 0.10$). However, these horizons capture overwinter precipitation and deep root-zone recharge \citep{Vijverberg2023}, establishing the baseline hydrological buffer against summer dry spells.
    \item \textbf{Planting and Vegetative Growth ($H = 5, 4$, May 31 and June 30):} Correlations in May reflect operational penalties of wet spring soils that delay planting ($r \approx -0.06$ to $-0.09$ for moisture), transitioning in June toward positive soil moisture dependency ($r \approx +0.12$) and negative evaporative demand ($r \approx -0.14$ for $VPD$). Vegetative plasticity buffers early variations while root systems establish.
    \item \textbf{Peak Reproductive Sensitivity ($H = 3, 2$, July 31 and August 31):} Atmospheric sensitivity escalates sharply during flowering (July, R1--R3) and peaks during pod development and seed filling (August, R4--R6). In August, positive correlations with soil moisture ($r = +0.43$) and precipitation ($r = +0.38$) coincide with strong negative correlations with $VPD$ ($r = -0.46$) and $T_{\text{max}}$ ($r = -0.41$). This reproductive window provides the primary predictive signal for final yield anomalies.
    \item \textbf{Physiological Maturation and Pre-Harvest ($H = 1$, September 30):} Once seed filling concludes in late August, crops reach physiological maturity (R7--R8) and senesce, causing weather correlations across all variables to collapse toward zero ($|r| \le 0.05$). Horizon $H = 1$ integrates the finalized weather record to deliver an objective empirical harvest benchmark prior to official post-harvest survey releases.
\end{itemize}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:intro_problem}, the practical utility of these sequential updates aligns directly with this phenological sensitivity curve, guiding commercial decisions from early input procurement to pre-harvest transportation logistics. We formalize these twelve predictive horizons, feature engineering workflows, model architectures, and expanding-window validation protocols in Chapter~4.

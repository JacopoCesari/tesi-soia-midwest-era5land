\chapter{Data and Study Area}
\label{chap:data_and_study_area}

The empirical foundation of this thesis rests on two primary data streams spanning the agricultural heartland of the United States Midwest over the 75-year historical horizon from 1951 to 2025: historical county-level soybean yields compiled from the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA), and high-resolution gridded surface weather reanalysis extracted from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). 

To ensure complete methodological transparency and narrative cohesion, this chapter is organized into two overarching parts. The first part (Sections~\ref{sec:data_study_area}--\ref{sec:data_eda}) establishes the regional agronomic setting, details the developmental stages of the soybean plant, formalizes the county panel assembly under a strict zero-imputation protocol, and presents an exploratory analysis of historical yield dynamics and secular technological trends. The second part (Sections~\ref{sec:data_era5}--\ref{sec:data_climate_eda}) formalizes the atmospheric reanalysis data, details the spatial and temporal aggregation methodologies, provides an exhaustive inventory of primary and derived agrometeorological indicators, and analyzes the empirical agro-climatic sensitivity profiles and historical shock footprints that govern crop predictability across progressive lead times.

\section{Agronomic Setting and County Panel Assembly}
\label{sec:data_study_area}

\subsection{Regional Geographic and Production Context}

The empirical analysis focuses on the six core producing states of the United States Midwest: Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, and Ohio. Collectively, these states constitute the primary production core of the North American Corn and Soybean Belt, historically generating over half of total domestic soybean harvest volumes. 

The agricultural prominence of this region is rooted in exceptional natural endowments. The Midwestern landscape features deep, naturally fertile soils characterized by high organic matter content and substantial moisture-retention capacities, providing an essential buffer against transient atmospheric dry spells. Climatologically, the region is situated in a temperate continental interior characterized by warm, humid summers and cold winters. The vast majority of annual crop water demand is met through convective summer rainfall, rendering Midwestern soybean cultivation overwhelmingly rainfed. Because commercial irrigation accounts for less than 5\% of harvested soybean acreage across these six states, regional production is directly exposed to interannual meteorological fluctuations, seasonal rainfall deficits, and atmospheric thermal extremes.

\subsection{Soybean Phenological Stages and Operational Farm Management}
\label{sec:data_phenology}

Soybean (\textit{Glycine max}) is an annual legume whose developmental rate, biomass accumulation, and final seed yield are governed by the dynamic interplay between photoperiod, ambient temperatures, and edaphic moisture availability. Across the Midwestern Corn Belt, the cultivation cycle spans approximately six months, typically initiating in late April or May and concluding with harvest in September or October. 

To understand how meteorological anomalies propagate into realized yield shortfalls, crop development must be analyzed through both the biological requirements of the plant and the corresponding operational decisions faced by commercial farm managers. The Midwestern soybean lifecycle comprises five distinct phenological stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-Planting, Sowing, and Emergence (April--May):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Biological and Environmental Drivers:} Successful germination requires topsoil temperatures consistently above a minimum biological baseline of $10^\circ\text{C}$, alongside adequate seedbed moisture to initiate seed imbibition. Prolonged exposure to saturated, cold soils ($<10^\circ\text{C}$) induces seed chilling injury, slows radicle emergence, and promotes infection by soil-borne fungal pathogens.
        \item \textit{Farmer Operational Decisions:} Farm managers continuously monitor topsoil temperature and moisture conditions to establish optimal planting windows. In modern Midwestern agriculture, there is strong operational pressure to plant early (late April to early May) to maximize total canopy light interception and extend the subsequent reproductive phase. However, planting into wet, poorly drained fields creates severe soil compaction, damages soil structure, and leads to uneven seedling emergence. If persistent spring precipitation saturates soils throughout May, farmers face difficult operational trade-offs: either mudding-in seeds under sub-optimal conditions, applying targeted fungicide seed treatments, or switching to earlier-maturing cultivars.
        \item \textit{Yield Consequences:} Timely sowing under favorable soil moisture and temperature conditions establishes uniform plant populations and optimal root architectures. Conversely, severe planting delays into June truncate the total vegetative development cycle, reduce node counts per plant, and compress the critical seed-filling window, establishing a permanently lower ceiling on final yield potential.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Vegetative Growth and Canopy Closure (June, V1--Vn):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Biological and Environmental Drivers:} Following emergence, the crop enters a phase of rapid vegetative proliferation, developing vegetative trifoliate nodes, expansive leaf area, and taproot penetration into intermediate soil horizons ($7$--$28\text{ cm}$). Vegetative pacing is driven by thermal accumulation, quantified through Growing Degree Days ($GDD$).
        \item \textit{Farmer Operational Decisions:} In rainfed Midwestern systems, management interventions during June focus on weed competition, post-emergence herbicide applications, and field drainage monitoring. Because irrigation infrastructure is virtually absent, farmers cannot remediate developing moisture deficits. Instead, crop scouting and remote monitoring provide essential early assessments of crop vigor, which inform forward contracting, hedging decisions, and operating loan management.
        \item \textit{Yield Consequences:} Soybeans display remarkable physiological plasticity during vegetative stages. Moderate moisture deficits in June can stimulate deeper taproot elongation as the plant searches for moisture in lower soil horizons, inadvertently enhancing resilience against later midsummer drought. However, acute drought that halts vegetative growth, or extreme rainfall events that cause prolonged root-zone waterlogging, will stunt canopy development and reduce the photosynthetic capacity necessary to support reproductive sinks.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Flowering and Pod Initiation (July, R1--R3):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Biological and Environmental Drivers:} Inflorescence begins in late June or early July, triggered by day-length shortening and warm temperatures. Pod development initiates at the lowest nodes while flowering continues upward along the stem. This phase is exceptionally sensitive to atmospheric evaporative demand and thermal stress. Ambient maximum temperatures exceeding $30^\circ\text{C}$ ($HD_{30}$), particularly when paired with high vapor pressure deficits ($VPD > 1.5\text{ kPa}$), induce pollen sterility, reduce pollination efficiency, and trigger massive floral abscission.
        \item \textit{Farmer Operational Decisions:} Field interventions are strictly limited once the crop canopy closes and flowering initiates. Farm operations during July are primarily confined to late-season pest and disease scouting, such as monitoring foliar fungal blights or insect infestations. For commercial agribusinesses and commodity merchandisers, July weather serves as the primary catalyst for updating pre-harvest yield expectations and adjusting regional basis bids.
        \item \textit{Yield Consequences:} While the indeterminate growth habit of Midwestern soybean varieties allows them to produce surplus flowers over a multi-week window, severe or prolonged heat and moisture stress forces the plant to shed flowers and young pods. Once discarded, lost reproductive sinks cannot be regenerated, setting an irreversible limit on the total number of pods per plant.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Pod Filling and Seed Development (August, R4--R6):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Biological and Environmental Drivers:} Pod filling represents the single most critical yield-determining window of the entire crop lifecycle. During August, vegetative growth ceases, and the plant translocates almost all newly assimilated carbohydrates and stored nitrogen directly into developing seeds. Transpiration rates peak simultaneously, creating massive crop water consumption that rapidly exhausts upper soil layers and demands substantial moisture extraction from the deep root-zone reservoir ($28$--$100\text{ cm}$).
        \item \textit{Farmer Operational Decisions:} Field operations are largely concluded, aside from emergency pest treatments. Farm managers and logistics operators actively plan harvest logistics, arrange grain elevator commercial storage contracts, inspect harvesting combines, and monitor drying equipment.
        \item \textit{Yield Consequences:} August environmental conditions dictate realized seed size, seed weight, and complete pod retention. Water deficits or extreme evaporative stress during seed filling force the premature senescence of lower leaves, reduce the rate and duration of seed filling, and cause seed abortion or shriveled, low-test-weight grain. Conversely, abundant root-zone soil moisture paired with moderate temperatures allows uninterrupted photosynthesis, maximizing individual seed weight.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Maturation, Desiccation, and Harvest (September--October, R7--R8):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Biological and Environmental Drivers:} As seeds reach physiological maturity (R7, marked by one normal pod on the main stem attaining mature brown color), dry matter accumulation terminates. The canopy undergoes rapid monocarpic senescence and leaf drop. Seeds subsequently lose moisture to the surrounding air, desiccating from over 50\% moisture at maturity toward harvest-ready moisture levels ($13\%$--$15\%$).
        \item \textit{Farmer Operational Decisions:} Combine scheduling is governed entirely by grain moisture and weather forecasts. The optimal harvest moisture for soybeans is approximately $13\%$. Harvesting grain above $15\%$ moisture incurs price dockage and requires artificial mechanical drying, whereas harvesting below $11\%$ moisture causes high combine shatter losses and splits fragile seed coats. Farmers actively balance the risk of waiting for natural field dry-down against the hazard of approaching autumnal hard freezes or sustained autumn storm systems that make fields unpassable.
        \item \textit{Yield Consequences:} Because dry matter translocation is completed by R7, September weather cannot increase biological seed yield. However, weather during this period governs field harvest efficiency and mechanical losses. An early hard freeze ($T_{\text{min}} < 0^\circ\text{C}$) before R7 halts seed filling prematurely (as observed in 1974), while prolonged rainfall causes pod shattering, grain mold, or harvest abandonment.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{County Panel Selection Rationale and Panel Assembly}
\label{sec:data_panel_assembly}

The target variable in this study is the annual county-level soybean yield, expressed in bushels per acre (bu/ac; where $1\text{ bu/ac} \approx 67.25\text{ kg/ha}$), extracted from the official annual survey records of the USDA NASS Quick Stats database.

To establish an empirically rigorous foundation for machine learning and econometric modeling, the panel is assembled according to a transparent, two-stage screening logic:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Filter 1: Agronomic Significance and Historical Production Stability.} Starting from the extensive USDA NASS database across the six Midwestern states, counties are first screened to retain only those with a consistently significant and stable soybean harvested area throughout the historical period. This step is essential to prevent spatial scale mismatch: gridded meteorological reanalysis ($0.1^\circ \approx 9\text{ km}$) averaged over an entire county polygon provides a physically representative proxy of crop growing conditions only when soybean cultivation occupies a substantial, persistent footprint across the territory. In peripheral counties where soybeans are planted on marginal or isolated plots, county-wide weather averages introduce severe scale-mismatch noise.
    \item \textbf{Filter 2: Complete 75-Year Temporal Continuity (1951--2025).} Among established producing counties, we enforce an absolute \textit{zero-imputation rule} across the entire 75-year window from 1951 to 2025. Synthetic data imputation (such as spatial neighborhood averaging or temporal spline interpolation) is strictly avoided because it attenuates the true amplitude of localized weather shocks, suppresses empirical variance, and injects artificial cross-sectional dependencies that bias statistical learners. A county is included if and only if it possesses an unbroken, authenticated USDA survey record in every single one of the 75 consecutive crop years.
\end{enumerate}

The panel commences in 1951 because the ECMWF ERA5-Land reanalysis begins on January 1, 1950. In our twelve-month progressive lead-time forecasting framework ($H=12, \dots, 1$), the prediction campaign for crop year $Y$ initiates on October 31 of year $Y-1$ (horizon $H=12$). For the 1950 crop harvest, this protocol would require antecedent atmospheric observations beginning in October 1949, which are unavailable in ERA5-Land. Consequently, 1951 is established as the first official target year, while meteorological observations from calendar year 1950 are preserved to construct antecedent soil moisture and atmospheric features for the 1951 campaign.

This sample design reflects a deliberate methodological trade-off: prioritizing multidecadal temporal depth (75 consecutive years) over maximizing the gross cross-sectional number of counties. Statistical learners and climate response functions require sufficient historical sampling of rare, high-consequence macro-climatic anomalies—such as severe droughts (1953, 1983, 1988, 2012), catastrophic excessive moisture (1993), unseasonable freezes (1974), and optimal bumper harvests (1952, 1961, 1994, 2016, 2021)—alongside the structural secular trend driven by technology. Applying these criteria isolates exactly \textbf{135 counties}, providing an uninterrupted balanced panel of $135 \times 75 = \mathbf{10{,}125}$ county-year observations representing 12.80 million harvested acres in 2025. 

Table~\ref{tab:panel_selection} summarizes the spatial composition of the panel, and Figure~\ref{fig:study_area_map} illustrates their balanced geographic distribution across the six Midwestern states. As shown, the 135 retained counties provide an even, uniform spatial sampling across the primary agro-ecological subregions of the Corn Belt: the glacial till plains of central Illinois (28 counties) and northern Indiana (20 counties), the rolling loess hills of Iowa (35 counties), the cold-tolerant northern prairie of Minnesota (19 counties), the claypan subsoils of northern Missouri (6 counties), and the eastern till plains of western Ohio (27 counties).

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis of County Soybean Yields (1951--2025)}
\label{sec:data_eda}

\subsection{Conceptual Decomposition: Secular Trend and Stochastic Anomaly}
\label{sec:data_yield_decomposition}


To formalize the empirical target and clarify the physical drivers of historical crop variation, realized county soybean yield $y_{c,t}$ (in bushels per acre for county $c \in \{1, \dots, 135\}$ and crop year $t \in \{1951, \dots, 2025\}$) is conceptually decomposed into two additive, orthogonal components:
\begin{equation}
    y_{c,t} = \tau_{c,t} + \epsilon_{c,t}
    \label{eq:yield_decomp_ch3}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{c,t}$ represents the deterministic secular trend driven by cumulative technological, agronomic, and genetic innovations, and $\epsilon_{c,t}$ denotes the zero-mean, high-frequency stochastic shock anomaly induced by intra-seasonal atmospheric forcing and environmental extremes.

The secular component $\tau_{c,t}$ encapsulates long-term breeding enhancements (such as improved harvest index, lodging resistance, and maturity group optimization), transgenic traits (glyphosate tolerance, pest resistance), systematic farm mechanization, artificial subsurface tile drainage, and precision agrochemical management. As demonstrated below, this structural trend accounts for over 81\% of gross interannual yield variance across the 75-year record ($R^2 = 0.812$). 

However, from an operational and supply-chain perspective, $\tau_{c,t}$ is highly predictable ex-ante from historical trajectories; commercial agribusinesses, logistics operators, and commodity traders already capitalize secular baseline capacity into long-term infrastructure. In sharp contrast, systemic market volatility, transport bottlenecks, spatial basis risk, and emergency disaster relief expenditures are governed entirely by the unpredictable weather anomaly $\epsilon_{c,t}$. A deterministic trend forecast provides zero advance operational intelligence during catastrophic regional droughts or unexpected bumper harvests. Crucially, the formal mathematical estimation and train-only detrending specifications (e.g., deterministic linear trends, quadratic splines, LOESS filtering, and ridge penalization) are strictly reserved for the predictive methodology detailed in Chapter~4, ensuring that all parameter estimation respects expanding-window temporal validation without backward data snooping.

The historical evolution of county soybean yields over the 75-year panel is characterized by persistent, upward technological growth, interrupted periodically by sharp climate-induced downward shocks. Figure~\ref{fig:yield_trajectory} displays the individual yield trajectories for all 135 counties alongside the aggregate panel mean and an estimated linear secular trend.

Across the entire panel, soybean yields expanded from an unweighted cross-sectional mean of $22.77\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 1950s to $57.31\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 2020s, representing a net gain of $+151.7\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:decadal_yields}). At the individual county-year level ($N = 10{,}125$), a pooled linear secular trend accounts for over 81\% of total yield variance ($R^2 = 0.8122$). When aggregated to the annual regional panel mean ($N = 75$), an ordinary least squares regression yields an average linear rate of technological progress of $+0.484\text{ bu/ac/year}$ ($R^2 = 0.9323$):
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y}_t = 0.484 \cdot t - 925.46 \quad (t = 1951, \dots, 2025)
    \label{eq:panel_trend}
\end{equation}

As summarized in Table~\ref{tab:decadal_yields}, this persistent productivity expansion has unfolded with remarkable cross-decadal consistency, with decadal mean yields progressing monotonically from $22.77\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 1950s to $57.31\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 2020s. 

Crucially, this tripling of baseline yield has direct implications for agricultural risk management and supply-chain logistics. Because the structural baseline $\tau_{c,t}$ is today nearly three times higher than at the beginning of the panel, an identical percentage crop shortfall (such as a $15\%$ drought loss) translates into an absolute physical volume reduction of nearly $9\text{ bu/ac}$ per acre today compared to barely $3.5\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 1950s. Across our 135-county footprint (spanning over 12.8 million harvested acres), such climate-driven shocks correspond to hundreds of millions of bushels in unexpected volume dislocations. Furthermore, as emphasized by \citet{Schlenker2009}, breeding gains remain highly vulnerable to non-linear thermal damage thresholds ($>30^\circ\text{C}$), which can abruptly erase seasonal productivity gains. Isolating and anticipating these volatile weather-driven deviations ($\epsilon_{c,t}$) against the predictable secular baseline is therefore the central objective of this research.

\subsection{Econometric Evaluation and Empirical Defense of Detrending Specification}
\label{sec:data_detrending_defense}

A critical methodological question in agricultural climatology is the choice of detrending specification. Alternative approaches in the econometric and agronomic literature include linear OLS, higher-order polynomials (quadratic, cubic), smoothing splines, local polynomial regression (LOESS), and macro-economic filters such as the Hodrick-Prescott (HP) filter \citep{Schlenker2009, Lobell2014, RavnUhlig2002}. To establish an empirically defensible benchmark, Table~\ref{tab:detrending_comparison} and Figure~\ref{fig:detrending_diagnostics} compare candidate detrending models across the 75-year panel.

\input{tables/tab_detrending_comparison}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/fig_detrending_diagnostics}
    \caption{Econometric and diagnostic evaluation of candidate detrending specifications across the 75-year Midwestern panel (1951--2025). Panel (a) illustrates fitted multidecadal trajectories and terminal edge behavior (2015--2025). Panel (b) shows residual probability density estimates and stationarity tests ($I(0)$), highlighting significant negative skewness ($-1.12$). Panel (c) tracks out-of-sample cumulative squared prediction errors ($\sum e_{T,\text{OOS}}^2$) across 45 expanding windows ($1981$--$2025$), demonstrating the severe overfitting explosion of polynomial models. Panel (d) displays county-level out-of-sample RMSE distributions across all 135 counties, where Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) strictly favors Linear OLS in 65.9\% of individual counties (89/135).}
    \label{fig:detrending_diagnostics}
\end{figure}

The empirical comparison resolves four foundational justifications for selecting a deterministic linear trend:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Model Parsimony and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC):} In-sample goodness of fit naturally increases with model complexity (Table~\ref{tab:detrending_comparison}, Panel A): moving from Linear OLS ($R^2 = 0.9323$, $\text{RSE} = 2.87\text{ bu/ac}$) to Quadratic ($R^2 = 0.9398$, $\text{RSE} = 2.72\text{ bu/ac}$) and Cubic ($R^2 = 0.9428$, $\text{RSE} = 2.67\text{ bu/ac}$) slightly reduces residual sum of squares. However, information criteria penalize redundant parameterization: the Bayesian Information Criterion strictly favors Linear OLS over Cubic on the aggregate panel ($\text{BIC} = 377.4$ vs $373.4$). Crucially, at the individual county level ($N = 135$), BIC strictly favors Linear OLS over Quadratic in \textbf{65.9\% of counties} (89 of 135), confirming that local technological progress is overwhelmingly linear across the Midwestern heartland.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Sample Expanding-Window Generalization:} The paramount test of detrending validity in predictive forecasting is temporal stability under sequential updating. In an expanding-window evaluation spanning 45 test years ($1981$--$2025$, training initiated on $1951$--$1980$, directly mimicking the Chapter~4 validation design across $6{,}075$ county-year forecasts), \textbf{Linear OLS minimizes out-of-sample RMSE} at $5.889\text{ bu/ac}$, outperforming Quadratic ($6.030\text{ bu/ac}$) and Cubic ($6.385\text{ bu/ac}$). As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:detrending_diagnostics}c, cumulative squared prediction errors for cubic and quadratic polynomials accelerate dramatically away from Linear OLS, caused by unstable parameter extrapolation.
    \item \textbf{Boundary Oscillation and Runge's Phenomenon:} Higher-degree polynomials suffer from severe boundary instability (Runge's phenomenon): when fitted on historical windows, polynomial curves bend erratically at sample endpoints to accommodate recent observations (Figure~\ref{fig:detrending_diagnostics}a, boundary region 2015--2025). This curvature artificially shifts the baseline trend upward or downward during recent decades, corrupting estimated anomalies and inflating maximum out-of-sample prediction errors ($27.07\text{ bu/ac}$ for Cubic vs $25.49\text{ bu/ac}$ for Linear). Linear OLS provides an immutable, physically constrained slope that avoids endpoint swings.
    \item \textbf{Preserving Macro-Climatic Shock Signals vs. Filter Absorption:} Non-parametric filters such as the Hodrick-Prescott filter ($\lambda = 6.25$ for annual series \citep{RavnUhlig2002}) or LOESS smoothing achieve high apparent fit ($R^2 = 0.9586$) by allowing the trend to bend dynamically. However, this flexibility creates a fatal methodological flaw: flexible filters absorb multi-year climatic clusters (such as the 1980s drought cycle or 2010s warm phases) into the "trend", thereby muting the very agro-climatic anomalies that machine learning models are tasked with forecasting. Linear OLS residuals are strictly stationary ($I(0)$ with Augmented Dickey-Fuller unit-root test $p < 0.001$, Figure~\ref{fig:detrending_diagnostics}b), successfully removing deterministic non-stationarity while fully preserving the true amplitude of historical climate shocks.
\end{enumerate}

A fundamental question in agricultural risk analysis is whether technological modernization has altered crop vulnerability to environmental shocks. Figure~\ref{fig:dispersion_dynamics} resolves this by examining absolute cross-county standard deviation ($\sigma_t$) alongside relative volatility, measured by the coefficient of variation ($CV_t = 100 \times \sigma_t / \mu_t$).

The empirical evidence reveals a clear dichotomy: while absolute standard deviation has expanded from $5.01\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 1950s to $7.57\text{ bu/ac}$ in the 2020s ($r = +0.705$), the coefficient of variation has declined systematically from $22.0\%$ to $13.2\%$ ($r = -0.610$, Table~\ref{tab:decadal_yields}). Modern cultivars, seed treatments, and drainage have dampened \textit{relative} vulnerability to localized micro-stresses. However, because baseline yield levels have nearly tripled, the \textit{absolute} bushel shortfall generated during severe regional droughts is substantially larger today, heightening volume exposure for commercial supply-chain logistics.

\subsection{Macro-Climatic Historical Yield Shocks and Biological Asymmetry}
\label{sec:data_yield_shocks}

The 75-year panel captures numerous macro-climatic shock years that serve as prime test beds for out-of-sample forecast validation. To isolate genuine macro-climatic regime shifts from background noise, extreme crop campaigns are identified using an asymmetric relative anomaly threshold of $|\epsilon_t / \hat{y}_t| \ge 10\%$ relative to the secular linear trend. Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks} compiles all historical years meeting this criterion across the panel, categorized into adverse harvest shortfalls and favorable bumper regimes alongside their dominant meteorological archetypes.

\input{tables/tab_yield_shocks}

The empirical distribution of extreme yield anomalies reveals a profound \textbf{biological and statistical asymmetry}. While positive bumper harvests cluster tightly within a narrow range between $+10.1\%$ and $+11.9\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks}: 1952, 1961, 1994, 2016, 2021), negative yield shortfalls exhibit a severe, elongated lower tail extending down to $-19.7\%$ (1974), $-21.6\%$ (2003), and $-25.6\%$ (1988). Consequently, the empirical detrended yield residual distribution displays significant negative skewness ($-1.12$, Jarque-Bera test $p < 0.001$, Figure~\ref{fig:detrending_diagnostics}b).

This fundamental asymmetry is rooted in crop biophysics and canopy physiology \citep{Schlenker2009, Lobell2014}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptotic Biological Ceiling on Bumper Harvests:} Under optimal agro-climatic conditions—exemplified by bumper cohorts across different technological eras such as 1952, 1961, 1994, 2016, and 2021—soybean dry matter accumulation is bounded by an asymptotic physiological ceiling. Even with perfectly timed rainfall, moderate temperatures, and negligible heat stress, crop yield cannot exceed the maximum photosynthetic capacity dictated by C3 RuBisCO carboxylation kinetics, canopy radiation interception, and genetic sink strength. Consequently, favorable weather regimes elevate regional yields by at most $+10\%$ to $+12\%$ above the baseline trend.
    \item \textbf{Catastrophic Lower Tail Vulnerability Across Diverse Hazard Cohorts:} In sharp contrast, adverse abiotic stressors possess no physiological floor short of total crop loss. Crucially, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:yield_shocks}, these severe downside shortfalls cluster into distinct meteorological hazard archetypes rather than isolated anomalies:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Compound Thermal-Drought Stress (e.g., 1953, 1983, 1988, 2012):} Extreme maximum temperatures ($T_{\text{max}} > 30^\circ\text{C}$ and $>35^\circ\text{C}$) paired with elevated vapor pressure deficits ($VPD > 1.8\text{ kPa}$) trigger pollen sterility, massive floral abscission, and transpirational collapse, capable of destroying $11\%$ to $26\%$ of regional yield within weeks.
        \item \textit{Pluvial Inundation and Soil Anoxia (e.g., 1993):} Relentless storm tracks and soil waterlogging induce root-zone anoxia, impair symbiotic rhizobial nitrogen fixation, and promote fungal blights, producing major shortfalls ($-10.5\%$) in the complete absence of drought.
        \item \textit{Temporal Boundary and Frost Truncation (e.g., 1974):} Excessive spring precipitation delays planting into June, shifting the reproductive phase late into summer and exposing immature pods to early autumn freezing events ($-19.7\%$).
        \item \textit{Compound Biotic-Abiotic Pressures (e.g., 2003):} Late-summer moisture deficits interact synergistically with biological shocks (such as the widespread soybean aphid epidemic), amplifying crop losses ($-21.6\%$).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

A final crucial property of county-level crop yields is spatial autocorrelation. Because synoptic weather systems (e.g., anticyclonic blocking ridges, frontal boundaries) span hundreds of kilometers, meteorological anomalies affect dozens of contiguous counties simultaneously. Within our panel, the cross-county correlation of detrended yield anomalies between neighboring counties averages above $0.75$. This high spatial synchrony reinforces the strict requirement for temporal year-splitting rather than random cross-validation to prevent spatial data leakage \citep{Sweetetal2023}.

\section{Atmospheric Reanalysis: ECMWF ERA5-Land}
\label{sec:data_era5}

\subsection{Methodological Rationale for a Weather-Only Scope}
\label{sec:data_weather_only_rationale}

Empirical agricultural forecasting often leverages diverse remote sensing, biophysical, management, and meteorological datasets. Gridded atmospheric reanalyses, particularly ECMWF ERA5-Land \citep{MunozSabater2021}, provide continuous, physically consistent hourly meteorological fields at $0.1^\circ \times 0.1^\circ$ resolution ($\approx 9\text{ km}$ across the Midwest), eliminating the station-drop biases, spatial interpolation artifacts, and reporting gaps inherent in ground weather stations.

A central methodological constraint of this research is the deliberate restriction of dynamic predictive inputs strictly to observed surface weather. This restriction is justified by four foundational constraints that render alternative data modalities methodologically inadmissible for multi-lead-time forecasting over multidecadal panels:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ex-Post Reporting Lag of Agronomic Management:} Comprehensive county-level management records (such as specific cultivar genetics, seed planting dates, fertilizer application rates, and fungicide usage) are compiled at annual cadences and released months after harvest. Incorporating these records into intra-seasonal forecasts would introduce fatal temporal data leakage. Furthermore, consistent 75-year historical panels of management practices at county resolution do not exist.
    \item \textbf{Physiological Latency and Satellite Coverage Limits:} While satellite vegetation indices (e.g., NDVI, EVI) and Solar-Induced Chlorophyll Fluorescence (SIF) monitor canopy vigor \citep{Yinetal2026}, their historical depth is limited to the post-2000 MODIS/Sentinel era, preventing multidecadal sampling of climate extremes. Crucially, satellite optical greenness signals exhibit severe physiological latency: vegetation indices become informative only after mid-summer canopy closure (July), offering zero predictive utility during pre-sowing and early vegetative stages ($H \ge 4$).
    \item \textbf{Temporal Invariance of Pedological Endowments:} Static soil properties from databases such as USGS SSURGO (water-holding capacity, organic matter, soil texture) \citep{Khaki2020} explain permanent cross-sectional yield baselines across counties. However, because their values are constant over time ($\text{Var}(X_{it}) = 0$), static soil features contain zero interannual variance to explain temporal yield anomalies within counties.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding Confounded Dynamical Climate Model Biases:} While dynamical seasonal climate forecasts (such as ECMWF SEAS5) are available in real time, coupling agricultural models with long-range seasonal climate forecasts conflates crop physiological sensitivity with climate model systematic biases and forecast drift. Restricting dynamic inputs to realized surface reanalysis isolates the unconfounded, physical upper bound of predictability attributable strictly to atmospheric forcing observed up to the forecast cutoff.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Spatial Aggregation: Exact Planar Polygon Intersection}

To map gridded reanalysis fields into irregularly shaped administrative county polygons, an exact planar polygon-intersection geometry was computed (\texttt{spatial\_weights.csv}). Across the 135 counties and the ERA5-Land $0.1^\circ$ grid, 3,344 unique intersection polygons are identified with normalized area weights:
\begin{equation}
    w_{c,g} = \frac{\text{Area}(c \cap g)}{\text{Area}(c)}, \quad \sum_{g \in \mathcal{G}_c} w_{c,g} = 1
    \label{eq:spatial_weight}
\end{equation}
where $\text{Area}(c \cap g)$ is the planar intersection area between county polygon $c$ and grid cell $g$, and $\mathcal{G}_c$ denotes the set of intersecting grid cells.

This area-weighted polygon integration represents a significant methodological advance over naive centroid sampling. Centroid extraction assumes that a single point can represent an entire county, which introduces severe spatial sampling errors in large Midwestern counties characterized by localized convective precipitation gradients and heterogeneous topography. Exact area weighting accurately integrates the total atmospheric mass, radiation, and precipitation volume falling within county boundaries.

\subsection{Temporal Aggregation and Crop Campaign Structure}

Hourly ERA5-Land reanalysis fields are spatially aggregated via Equation~\eqref{eq:spatial_weight} and summarized into daily indicators:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thermodynamic States:} Daily mean, maximum, and minimum 2m air temperature ($T_{\text{mean}}, T_{\text{max}}, T_{\text{min}}$), daily mean 2m dewpoint ($T_{\text{dew}}$), and daily mean/maximum skin temperature ($T_{\text{skt}}$) in $^\circ$C.
    \item \textbf{Cumulative Fluxes:} Daily cumulative precipitation ($P$, mm/day), surface solar radiation downwards ($SSRD$, $\text{MJ}/\text{m}^2/\text{day}$), and surface thermal radiation downwards ($STRD$, $\text{MJ}/\text{m}^2/\text{day}$).
    \item \textbf{Boundary Layer and Wind Dynamics:} Daily mean surface pressure ($SP$, kPa), 10m horizontal wind components ($u_{10}, v_{10}$, m/s), and scalar wind speed ($WS_{10}$, m/s).
    \item \textbf{Pedological State Variables:} Daily mean soil temperature across four discrete depth horizons ($STL_1$--$STL_4$: $0$--$7$, $7$--$28$, $28$--$100$, and $100$--$289\text{ cm}$, $^\circ$C), and volumetric soil moisture across the same four horizons ($SM_1$--$SM_4$, $\text{m}^3/\text{m}^3$).
\end{itemize}

For each crop year $Y$, daily weather processing is structured over a 13-month campaign spanning from October 1 of year $Y-1$ to October 31 of year $Y$ (397 consecutive days). This multi-month temporal structure aligns with the 12 progressive monthly forecast horizons ($H=12, \dots, 1$) evaluated on the last day of each month, capturing antecedent overwinter soil moisture recharge and growing-season atmospheric forcing without temporal data leakage.

\subsection{Derived Agrometeorological Variables and Bioclimatic Indicators}
\label{sec:data_derived_weather}

Raw atmospheric fields describe lower boundary layer thermodynamics. However, crop physiological response functions operate non-linearly on compound atmospheric-hydrological demands rather than isolated averages \citep{Schlenker2009, Hoffman2020}. Table~\ref{tab:weather_variables} provides a comprehensive inventory of the primary ERA5-Land fields and derived daily agrometeorological indicators.

\input{tables/tab_weather_variables}

\paragraph{Vapor Pressure Deficit ($VPD$).} Atmospheric evaporative demand is parameterized via Vapor Pressure Deficit, which quantifies the drying power of the air. Saturation vapor pressure $e_s(T)$ and actual vapor pressure $e_a(T_{\text{dew}})$ (in kPa) are computed using the standard Tetens formulation:
\begin{align}
    e_s(T_{\text{mean}}) &= 0.61078 \exp\left( \frac{17.27 \, T_{\text{mean}}}{T_{\text{mean}} + 237.3} \right) \label{eq:tetens_sat} \\
    e_a(T_{\text{dew}}) &= 0.61078 \exp\left( \frac{17.27 \, T_{\text{dew}}}{T_{\text{dew}} + 237.3} \right) \label{eq:tetens_act} \\
    VPD &= \max\left(0, \, e_s(T_{\text{mean}}) - e_a(T_{\text{dew}})\right) \label{eq:vpd}
\end{align}
In soybean physiology, high $VPD$ ($>1.5$--$2.0\text{ kPa}$) triggers defensive stomatal closure to prevent runaway xylem cavitation and hydraulic failure \citep{Grossiord2020, Lobell2014}. This closure halts transpirational cooling and terminates photosynthetic carbon assimilation, inducing severe yield penalties during flowering and pod development even when root-zone moisture remains nominally adequate.

\paragraph{Reference Evapotranspiration ($ET_0$) and Climatic Water Balance ($P - ET_0$).} Potential crop water consumption is estimated using the standardized FAO-56 Penman-Monteith equation for a short grass reference canopy \citep{Allen1998}:
\begin{equation}
    ET_0 = \frac{0.408 \, \Delta \, (R_n - G) + \gamma \, \frac{900}{T_{\text{mean}} + 273} \, u_2 \, (e_s - e_a)}{\Delta + \gamma \, (1 + 0.34 \, u_2)}
    \label{eq:penman_monteith}
\end{equation}
where $R_n$ is net surface radiation ($\text{MJ}/\text{m}^2/\text{day}$), $G$ is soil heat flux density (assumed negligible at daily steps, $G \approx 0$), $u_2$ is 2m wind speed ($\text{m/s}$), $\Delta$ is the slope of the saturation vapor pressure curve, and $\gamma$ is the psychrometric constant. Combining $ET_0$ with daily precipitation $P$ yields the daily climatic water balance:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{net}} = P - ET_0
    \label{eq:climatic_balance}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:climatic_balance} captures the net daily atmospheric moisture deficit ($P_{\text{net}} < 0$) or hydrological recharge surplus ($P_{\text{net}} > 0$).

\paragraph{Thermal Pacing and Extreme Heat Days ($GDD, HD_{30}, HD_{35}$).} Phenological pacing is modeled through Growing Degree Days ($GDD$), evaluated between standard soybean base and upper development thresholds ($T_{\text{base}} = 10^\circ\text{C}$ and $T_{\text{upper}} = 30^\circ\text{C}$):
\begin{equation}
    GDD = \max\left(0, \, \frac{\min(T_{\text{max}}, 30) + \max(T_{\text{min}}, 10)}{2} - 10\right)
    \label{eq:gdd}
\end{equation}
To capture the extreme non-linear temperature damage functions established by \citet{Schlenker2009}, thermal exposure is segmented into threshold exceedance indicators:
\begin{equation}
    HD_{30} = \max(0, \, T_{\text{max}} - 30), \quad HD_{35} = \max(0, \, T_{\text{max}} - 35)
    \label{eq:heat_days}
\end{equation}
alongside their binary daily occurrences $\mathbb{I}(T_{\text{max}} \ge 30^\circ\text{C})$ and $\mathbb{I}(T_{\text{max}} \ge 35^\circ\text{C})$. Temperatures exceeding $30^\circ\text{C}$ during reproductive stages induce pollen sterility, floral abortion, and pod shedding, while temperatures above $35^\circ\text{C}$ trigger irreversible leaf tissue desiccation.

\paragraph{Integrated Root-Zone Soil Moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$).} Volumetric soil moisture is extracted across discrete depth layers from ERA5-Land. To represent the total soil moisture column accessible to mature soybean taproots, root-zone soil moisture is computed as a depth-weighted average across the upper 100 cm:
\begin{equation}
    SM_{\text{root}} = 0.07 \, SM_1 + 0.21 \, SM_2 + 0.72 \, SM_3
    \label{eq:sm_root}
\end{equation}
where the weighting coefficients correspond to physical layer thicknesses ($7\text{ cm}$, $21\text{ cm}$, and $72\text{ cm}$). This integrated metric buffers transient surface dry spells and tracks the subsoil moisture reservoir sustaining crop water uptake during prolonged summer rainfall deficits, providing essential multi-month hydrological memory for forecasting \citep{Vijverberg2023}.

\section{Exploratory Agro-Climatic Analysis and Yield Sensitivity}

\subsection{Baseline Climatology and Intra-Annual Seasonality}
\label{sec:data_seasonality}

Before examining multi-decadal historical shocks, it is essential to establish the intra-annual baseline climatology that governs crop development across the Midwestern Corn Belt. Utilizing the daily spatially weighted ERA5-Land reanalysis across the 135 study counties over the 1950--present record, Figure~\ref{fig:weather_seasonality} characterizes the seasonal progression of thermal regimes, cumulative water balances, and edaphic moisture depletion across the primary soybean growing cycle (April 1 to October 31).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/fig_weather_seasonality}
    \caption{Midwestern agro-climatic seasonality and intra-annual phenological dynamics across the 135 study counties (April 1 to October 31). Panel (a) illustrates daily thermal dynamics ($T_{\text{max}}, T_{\text{mean}}, T_{\text{min}}$) alongside 10th--90th percentile envelopes and cardinal damage thresholds ($30^\circ\text{C}$ and $35^\circ\text{C}$), contrasted against the record 1988 severe drought. Vertical shaded bands demarcate the five standardized phenological development stages. Panel (b) depicts the cumulative hydrological budget, highlighting the emergence of the midsummer atmospheric water deficit ($ET_0 > P$) in July and August. Panel (c) tracks volumetric soil moisture depletion across discrete rooting horizons ($SM_1$, $SM_2$, $SM_3$), demonstrating the essential buffering capacity of the deep root zone ($28$--$100\text{ cm}$).}
    \label{fig:weather_seasonality}
\end{figure}

The empirical intra-annual climatology synthesizes three foundational agronomic mechanisms across the developmental windows established in Section~\ref{sec:data_phenology}:

First, daily thermal progression (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_seasonality}a) aligns with the biological cardinal boundaries of the soybean plant. During the pre-sowing and emergence window (April 15--May 31), mean ambient temperatures rise from $8^\circ\text{C}$ to $18^\circ\text{C}$, safely exceeding the biological base germination threshold ($T_{\text{base}} = 10^\circ\text{C}$). Across the 75-year climatological record (1951--2025), summer maximum temperatures ($T_{\text{max}}$) peak in mid-July at $28.5^\circ\text{C}$ with an interdecile envelope bounded between $24^\circ\text{C}$ and $32^\circ\text{C}$. In contrast, during historical drought cohorts—exemplified by 1953, 1983, 1988, and 2012—daily maxima consistently pierced above the $30^\circ\text{C}$ floral abortion threshold and repeatedly exceeded the $35^\circ\text{C}$ acute damage ceiling throughout flowering (R1--R3) and early pod set. Conversely, during prominent bumper harvest cohorts such as 1952, 1961, 1994, 2016, and 2021, summer maxima remained consistently mild (hovering between $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $28^\circ\text{C}$), completely preventing canopy heat stress.

Second, the cumulative hydrological budget (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_seasonality}b) reveals the emergence of a structural midsummer atmospheric water deficit. In early spring (April and May), cumulative precipitation ($P \approx 200\text{ mm}$) exceeds reference crop evapotranspiration ($ET_0 \approx 180\text{ mm}$), ensuring robust topsoil recharge. However, starting in mid-June and accelerating throughout July and August, rising net radiation and vapor pressure deficits drive cumulative $ET_0$ to surge past cumulative rainfall. Under normal climatological conditions, a net seasonal atmospheric moisture deficit of $\approx 180\text{ mm}$ opens between $ET_0$ ($820\text{ mm}$) and $P$ ($640\text{ mm}$). During acute drought events like 1988 and 2012, rainfall stalled at barely $400$--$450\text{ mm}$ while evaporative demand exceeded $920\text{ mm}$, opening an unprecedented atmospheric moisture deficit of over $400\text{ mm}$. In contrast, bumper campaigns benefit from timely, well-distributed convective rainfall events that maintain cumulative deficits near a moderate equilibrium.

Third, the edaphic moisture profile (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_seasonality}c) demonstrates the critical buffering role of deep soil horizons. Volumetric moisture in the thin topsoil layer ($SM_1$, $0$--$7\text{ cm}$) exhibits erratic, high-frequency fluctuations driven by individual convective precipitation pulses and rapid surface evaporation. The intermediate layer ($SM_2$, $7$--$28\text{ cm}$) undergoes rapid extraction during vegetative expansion in June. Consequently, during the critical reproductive pod-filling phase in August (R4--R6), the crop becomes overwhelmingly reliant on the deep root-zone moisture reservoir ($SM_3$, $28$--$100\text{ cm}$). In normal seasons and bumper years, $SM_3$ declines smoothly from $0.38$ to $0.26\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$, providing a stable moisture buffer. In severe drought campaigns (such as 1988 and 1983), transpirational demand completely depleted $SM_3$ down to $0.18\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$, driving the soil column near the permanent wilting point and inducing widespread canopy desiccation.

\subsection{Historical Agro-Climatic Stress and the Yield Shock Nexus (1950--Present)}
\label{sec:data_climatology}

While intra-annual seasonality describes the average seasonal trajectory, interannual yield volatility is governed by the occurrence of acute atmospheric shocks. Figure~\ref{fig:weather_climatology} synchronizes the 75-year panel yield anomalies with the multi-decadal time series of atmospheric heat stress and evaporative demand across the 135 study counties.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

Panel (a) illustrates annual cross-sectional detrended yield anomalies relative to the secular linear trend, with prominent bumper harvests highlighted in emerald green ($\ge +10\%$) and catastrophic negative shortfalls in deep crimson ($\le -10\%$), bounded by explicit $\pm 10\%$ threshold guide lines. \end{figure}

The synchronized historical record resolves two foundational macro-climatic insights across the 75-year panel:

First, the most catastrophic regional harvest failures cluster tightly with historical apexes in compound atmospheric stress. Across multiple distinct historical decades—most notably the prolonged mid-century drought cycle (1953--1954), the acute mid-1980s events (1983, 1988), and the severe 2012 flash drought—annual heat-stress days surged past $50$ to $56\text{ days}$ per county, while summer vapor pressure deficit reached historic peaks of $1.2$ to $1.3\text{ kPa}$. In these high-stress regimes, intense thermal forcing and severe atmospheric evaporative demand act simultaneously, rapidly draining root-zone moisture reserves, terminating transpirational cooling, and aborting reproductive sinks \citep{Schlenker2009}. Conversely, across seven decades of technological progress, prominent bumper harvest clusters (such as 1952, 1961, 1979, 1994, 2016, and 2021) consistently coincide with suppressed heat accumulation ($HD_{30} < 20\text{ days}$) and benign summer evaporative demand ($VPD < 0.9\text{ kPa}$), preserving canopy hydration throughout reproductive development.

Second, the multi-decadal time series demonstrates that severe downside shocks do not originate exclusively from heatwaves and summer droughts. Significant regional shortfalls also emerge from distinct non-thermal and temporal hazard regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Hydrological Inundation and Root Anoxia:} In pluvial disaster campaigns—most prominently 1993, but also observed in localized flood years such as 1958 and 2008—summer heat days ($HD_{30} \approx 8\text{ days}$) and $VPD$ ($0.71\text{ kPa}$) collapsed to multi-decadal minimums under persistent overcast storm tracks. Yield shortfalls ($-10.5\%$) were driven by continuous topsoil inundation, prolonged root-zone anoxia, impaired rhizobial nitrogen fixation, and rampant soil-borne fungal pathogens.
    \item \textit{Temporal Planting Delays and Cold Truncation:} In seasons characterized by compound temporal boundary hazards—such as 1974 ($-19.7\%$ shortfall) and 2019—torrential spring rainfall delayed field preparation and seeding by nearly a month. Although subsequent summer thermal conditions were moderate, the delayed reproductive calendar left immature canopies fully exposed to unseasonably early Arctic cold fronts, terminating dry-matter accumulation before physiological maturity.
    \item \textit{Compound Biotic-Abiotic Pressures:} Moderate meteorological stress can produce catastrophic yield collapses when interacting with biological vectors, as demonstrated by the 2003 shortfall ($-21.6\%$), where late-summer moisture deficits coincided with the first widespread North American epidemic of the invasive soybean aphid (\textit{Aphis glycines}).
\end{itemize}

Synthesizing the daily trajectories and anomaly footprints reveals that historical extremes fall into four coherent agro-climatic cohorts:

\paragraph{Cohort I: Compound Thermal-Drought Stress.}
Drought-stressed campaigns across Midwestern history (including 1953, 1954, 1983, 1988, and 2012) are driven by persistent upper-tropospheric anticyclonic blocking ridges that displace frontal storm tracks northward. However, within this broad cohort, the daily trajectories resolve two critically distinct temporal stress profiles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Chronic Full-Season Drought (e.g., 1988, 1953):} Meteorological deficits establish early in May and June, with rainfall anomalies running $-40$ to $-50\text{ mm/month}$ below normal throughout the summer (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}). Evaporative demand surges continuously ($VPD$ anomalies $> +0.8\text{ kPa}$), while heat-stress days accumulate steadily from mid-June to reach an unprecedented 56 days (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}a). The cumulative climatic moisture deficit plunges below $-450\text{ mm}$, exhausting root-zone moisture reserves down to severe depletion levels ($<0.18\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$) well before seed filling, causing stunted vegetative frames and massive flower abortion.
    \item \textit{Reproductive Flash Drought and Pod-Fill Collapse (e.g., 1983, 2012):} In sharp contrast, vegetative development through May and June experiences near-normal rainfall and moderate temperatures, fostering expansive canopy development and high transpirational demand. In late July, however, an abrupt synoptic blocking ridge collapses precipitation to near zero and triggers an acute thermal surge ($HD_{30} > +5\text{ days/month}$, $VPD > +0.7\text{ kPa}$) directly coincident with pod fill (R4--R6, Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}). The lush vegetative biomass rapidly drains remaining subsoil moisture, precipitating severe pod abortion, incomplete pod filling, and shriveled grain. These dynamics confirm that robust vegetative vigor offers zero buffer against acute late-season moisture deficits.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Cohort II: Pluvial Excess and Hydrological Saturation.}
Pluvial disaster seasons across the historical record (e.g., 1958, 1993, 2008, 2015) exhibit physical trajectories that represent the polar antithesis of drought. Cumulative heat days plateau at minimal levels ($\sum HD_{30} < 18\text{ days}$), while high relative humidity and cloud cover depress $VPD$ to $0.50$--$0.75\text{ kPa}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}a,b). Instead, persistent zonal storm tracks generate immense rainfall surpluses ($+60$ to $+100\text{ mm/month}$ above normal, Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}), driving cumulative $P - ET_0$ past $+160\text{ mm}$. Root-zone soil moisture remains pinned at absolute saturation ($>0.40\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$). This sustained inundation suffocates root respiration, impairs symbiotic rhizobial nitrogen fixation, fosters extensive Phytophthora root rots, and leaves heavy machinery unable to enter saturated fields.

\paragraph{Cohort III: Temporal Boundary and Cold Truncation Hazards.}
Seasons governed by temporal boundary shocks (e.g., 1974, 2019) demonstrate that agricultural productivity can be severely impaired without anomalous midsummer heat or midsummer drought. In these campaigns, torrential spring precipitation saturates topsoils throughout April and May, delaying regional sowing operations by 3--4 weeks into June (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}c). While midsummer temperatures and water balances remain moderate, this operational delay shifts the reproductive pod-fill calendar late into August and September. In late September, an unseasonably early Arctic cold front brings hard sub-freezing temperatures ($T_{\text{min}} < -2^\circ\text{C}$) across the northern and central Corn Belt (Figure~\ref{fig:weather_shocks_footprint}). The freeze abruptly kills the canopy before physiological maturity (R7), arresting carbohydrate translocation and permanently freezing immature green seeds in their pods.

\paragraph{Cohort IV: Agro-Climatic Optima and Bumper Harvest Regimes.}
Prominent bumper harvest cohorts across diverse technological eras (e.g., 1952, 1961, 1979, 1994, 2016, 2021) follow an ideal agro-climatic middle path. Daily temperatures remain remarkably mild, with daytime maxima consistently below $28^\circ\text{C}$ and cumulative heat-stress days plateauing below 20 days (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}a). Atmospheric evaporative demand hovers smoothly within the transpirational optimum ($VPD \approx 0.75$--$1.0\text{ kPa}$), preventing midday stomatal closure while supporting vigorous carbon assimilation. Crucially, timely and well-distributed convective rainfall events replenish root-zone soil moisture at regular 7- to 10-day intervals without creating waterlogged conditions, maintaining $SM_{\text{root}}$ securely between $0.27$ and $0.34\text{ m}^3/\text{m}^3$ throughout the critical August seed-filling window (Figure~\ref{fig:intra_seasonal_extremes}d). Under these benign conditions, crops achieve the maximum photosynthetic yield permitted by the biological ceiling of C3 RuBisCO kinetics.

\subsection{Phenological Climate-Yield Sensitivity and Lead-Time Dynamics}
\label{sec:data_pheno_sensitivity}

To quantify the evolving empirical dependency between atmospheric anomalies and county soybean yields across the growing season, Figure~\ref{fig:climate_yield_sensitivity} presents bivariate Pearson correlation coefficients ($r$) computed between detrended yield anomalies ($\epsilon_{c,t}$) and monthly atmospheric anomalies across the 135 study counties.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/fig_climate_yield_sensitivity}
    \caption{Phenological sensitivity of county soybean yields to monthly atmospheric anomalies across the crop cycle (April--October). Bars depict cross-sectional Pearson correlation coefficients ($r$) between detrended yield anomalies ($\epsilon_{c,t}$) and monthly anomalies of maximum temperature ($T_{\text{max}}$), vapor pressure deficit ($VPD$), precipitation ($P$), and root-zone soil moisture ($SM_{\text{root}}$). Yield sensitivity is minimal during pre-sowing and emergence (April--May), escalates sharply during flowering (July), peaks during pod filling (August), and decays rapidly upon physiological maturity (September--October).}
    \label{fig:climate_yield_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

The empirical sensitivity profiles display four distinct phenological stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-Sowing and Emergence Window (April--May):} Correlation coefficients between yield anomalies and weather variables are uniformly low ($|r| < 0.10$). Notably, precipitation and soil moisture exhibit slightly negative correlations with final yield ($r \approx -0.06$ to $-0.09$). This counter-intuitive negative relationship reflects the operational penalties of excessive spring moisture: wet fields delay field preparation and seeding, promote soil compaction, and foster early seedling diseases. Ambient temperature exerts negligible influence during this preparatory phase.
    \item \textbf{Vegetative Growth and Canopy Closure (June):} As crops establish root systems and leaf area, the sign of environmental sensitivities shifts toward positive moisture dependency. However, correlation magnitudes remain moderate ($r \approx +0.12$ for soil moisture; $r \approx -0.14$ for $VPD$), indicating that soybeans possess sufficient vegetative plasticity to recover from early-season weather aberrations if subsequent reproductive conditions are favorable.
    \item \textbf{Peak Reproductive Sensitivity (July--August):} Atmospheric sensitivity escalates dramatically during flowering (July, R1--R3) and reaches its maximum amplitude during pod development and seed filling (August, R4--R6). In August, the correlation between yield anomalies and root-zone soil moisture surges to $r = +0.43$, accompanied by precipitation at $r = +0.38$. Concurrently, atmospheric stress metrics exhibit severe negative correlations: $VPD$ reaches $r = -0.46$ and $T_{\text{max}}$ reaches $r = -0.41$. The co-occurrence of maximum evaporative demand ($VPD$) and reproductive seed swelling makes August weather the single most critical determinant of interannual yield variance.
    \item \textbf{Maturation and Senescence (September--October):} Following the completion of seed fill in late August, soybean leaves undergo rapid monocarpic senescence and yellowing (R7), followed by physiological maturity (R8) and field dry-down. Consequently, correlation coefficients plunge precipitously back toward zero ($|r| \le 0.05$ across all four variables). Atmospheric conditions in September and October affect grain moisture content and combine harvest efficiency, but exert zero biological impact on realized seed quantity.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Methodological Implications for Progressive Lead-Time Forecasting.} The empirical sensitivity dynamics synthesized in Figure~\ref{fig:climate_yield_sensitivity} provide the direct scientific foundation for the progressive lead-time forecasting framework formalized in Chapter~4. 

At long presowing lead times ($H = 12, \dots, 6$, October through April), atmospheric forcings exhibit low direct correlation with final yield anomalies; predictive skill at these horizons must rely primarily on persistent antecedent root-zone soil moisture recharge and multi-month hydrological memory \citep{Vijverberg2023}. As the forecast horizon advances through vegetative development ($H = 5, 4$, May and June), incoming meteorological information gradually updates crop potential, but the system remains subject to high downstream epistemic uncertainty. 

The primary leap in physical predictability occurs at mid-season and late-summer horizons ($H = 3, 2$, July 31 and August 31), when predictive models ingest the realized atmospheric stress profile during the critical flowering and pod-fill windows. Finally, at horizon $H = 1$ (September 30), the biological crop yield is essentially fixed; remaining forecast uncertainty is dominated by localized combine harvest losses and late frost hazards rather than physiological yield formation. The mathematical formalization of these twelve discrete forecasting horizons, alongside the multi-granularity feature extraction and expanding-window validation protocols, is developed in Chapter~4.

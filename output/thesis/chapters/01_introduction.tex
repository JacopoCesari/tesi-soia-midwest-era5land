\section{Problem, Practical Value, and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

Agricultural yield becomes definitively observable only at harvest, long after key operational, commercial, and financial commitments have been finalized. In modern agrifood systems, reliable yield forecasts well in advance are essential for systematic risk management. In the United States, soybean is a foundational commodity underpinning domestic livestock feed and global trade flows, with the Midwestern Corn Belt driving national production \citep{Schlenker2009}.

Harvest surprises in this region carry severe, asymmetric economic costs. Regional crop shortfalls, typically driven by summer heatwaves or moisture deficits, generate procurement bottlenecks and sharp price spikes for grain crushers, feed millers, and export terminals. Conversely, unexpectedly bumper harvests compress local storage capacity, create barge and rail bottlenecks along the Mississippi corridor, and exert downward pressure on regional cash basis levels.

Crucially, this thesis models county-level crop yield anomalies (in bushels per acre) rather than aggregate production volumes or market prices directly. Total production depends on acreage allocations and spatial aggregation, while price formation is governed by global carryover stocks and trade flows. These supply-chain repercussions establish the practical motivation for this study.

From both an applied risk-management perspective and an academic standpoint, a forecast's utility is inseparable from its lead time. In operational trading, procurement, and hedging, an accurate yield estimate delivered immediately prior to harvest provides little managerial flexibility: physical purchasing, storage reservations, and forward pricing have largely concluded. The central intellectual and practical challenge consists in determining \textit{when} atmospheric observations transition from uninformative statistical noise into a reliable, actionable forecasting signal. 

Operationally, an effective yield forecasting system must satisfy three essential requirements: predictive accuracy across the distribution (with dedicated capacity to capture severe downside risks), lead-time earliness to maximize managerial reaction windows, and temporal stability ensuring that early predictive gains persist across subsequent updates rather than reflecting transient sample noise. The practical utility of these forecasts evolves dynamically across the production calendar, informing decisions for both on-farm operators and downstream supply-chain participants:
pre-sowing signals ($H \ge 8$) inform seed variety selection, maturity group allocation, and acreage switching between corn and soybean, while guiding commercial forward contracting and input distribution;
vegetative stage updates ($H \approx 7 \text{ to } 4$) direct in-season farm management (such as replanting decisions, fertilizer split applications, and targeted crop protection) alongside producer price-hedging and merchandiser origination;
reproductive pod-filling updates ($H \approx 3 \text{ to } 2$) allow farmers to pace forward sales and prepare on-farm storage, while processors and exporters adjust crush runtimes, freight bookings, and secondary sourcing;
and near-harvest updates ($H=1$) optimize field harvest sequencing, drying logistics, and commercial grain terminal intake schedules. To evaluate this progression systematically, this study anchors the production campaign to an October harvest reference and tracks predictive skill across twelve standardized monthly forecast origins ($H=12, \dots, 1$).

\section{Research Question and Target Formulations}
\label{sec:intro_research_question}

A fundamental empirical challenge in agricultural forecasting is that raw county yields are heavily dominated by secular technological progress (such as seed genetics, fertilizer efficiency, and mechanization). Consequently, predictive models must target yield anomalies rather than raw trends, evaluating how much actionable forecasting skill observed surface weather can provide across successive stages of the crop cycle.

The central research question asks: \textit{At what lead times prior to harvest does observed surface weather add statistically significant and stable out-of-sample predictive skill for county-level soybean yield anomalies, and how does this skill vary across model families and temporal aggregations?}

To address this question, the empirical modeling evaluates two complementary target formulations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous Anomaly Forecasting (Regression):} The primary regression task models continuous county-level yield anomalies obtained by removing the secular technological trend via transformations fitted strictly on historical training folds. Absolute yield levels are subsequently reconstructed by recombining predicted anomalies with the estimated trend.
    \item \textbf{Categorical Regime Forecasting (Classification):} Complementing continuous regression, a discrete classification framework evaluates whether broad yield regimes (such as severe shortfall, normal, and bumper harvests) can be identified earlier or with greater reliability than precise continuous magnitudes.
\end{itemize}

The empirical investigation is guided by four primary objectives: mapping the emergence and temporal stability of predictive skill across horizons ($H=12 \dots 1$), identifying the winning combination of model architecture and temporal aggregation (determining whether 5-day or 10-day summaries provide superior predictive value over standard monthly averages), contrasting non-linear tree ensembles and sequential neural networks against regularized linear baselines, and exploring early regime classification. The formal research hypotheses (H1--H5) addressing these objectives are grounded in the literature and derived in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review}.

\section{Methodological Contributions and Scope Boundaries}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The literature on empirical crop modeling encompasses diverse approaches to predicting county-level soybean yields from environmental data. Recent studies have advanced the field from complementary perspectives: \citet{Vijverberg2023} identified pre-sowing climate precursors for extreme yield events; \citet{Yinetal2026} demonstrated the influence of sub-monthly temporal aggregation on modern architectures over recent decades; and the recent multidecadal benchmark by \citet{ChenZhang2026} examined in-season deep learning across monthly growing-season updates.

However, existing empirical work has largely examined these dimensions in isolation—focusing on specific phenological phases, selected model classes, or restricted historical intervals. Rather than proposing an isolated model variant, this thesis synthesizes and expands upon these empirical foundations within a unified, comprehensive benchmarking framework.

This thesis addresses this objective through two primary methodological contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic 12-Month Lead-Time Mapping Without Forecast Inputs ($H=12, \dots, 1$):} Establishing the empirical boundaries of weather-driven predictability across twelve standardized monthly origins spanning from the preceding autumn through harvest eve. By relying strictly on cumulative observed surface weather from ERA5-Land rather than uncertain dynamical seasonal climate forecasts, the framework tracks precisely when and where historical atmospheric observations begin to generate stable, actionable predictive skill.
    \item \textbf{Identification of Optimal Model-Aggregation Configurations:} Systematically evaluating multiple model families—regularized linear models, tree ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost), and recurrent deep learning (LSTM) architectures—across alternative temporal aggregations (5-day, 10-day, and 30-day summaries). This identifies the winning model-aggregation setup and directly establishes whether high-frequency sub-monthly weather summaries provide superior predictive value over standard monthly averages under identical information constraints.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope Boundaries and Methodological Discipline.}
While the evaluation framework is transferable in principle to other crops and regions, this empirical study is deliberately focused on soybean across a balanced panel of 135 counties in six core Midwestern states (Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, and Ohio) over the 75-year period 1951--2025 (10,125 county-year observations). This geographic and temporal delimitation provides an uninterrupted, high-density historical record that avoids artificial data imputation.

To maintain academic discipline, feature importance is interpreted strictly as empirical predictive association rather than causal or structural mechanics. Furthermore, because the analysis uses retrospective ERA5-Land reanalysis over a fixed historical panel, the primary objective is to delineate the physical and theoretical limits of weather-driven predictability across lead times, rather than proposing a real-time operational tool for immediate commercial deployment.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro_structure}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter 2 (Literature Review)} reviews soybean weather sensitivity, examines empirical yield forecasting approaches, and formalizes hypotheses H1--H5.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 3 (Data and Study Area)} describes the 135-county balanced panel (1951--2025) from USDA NASS, documents ERA5-Land processing, and presents exploratory climate-yield analyses.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 4 (Methodology)} formalizes the twelve forecast cutoffs, feature engineering workflows, baseline and machine learning specifications, and the expanding-window validation protocol.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 5 (Empirical Results)} reports out-of-sample performance across all twelve horizons, comparing baselines, tree ensembles, and sequential models.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 6 (Discussion)} interprets findings for agricultural risk management, addressing survivorship, reanalysis latency, and external validity.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 7 (Conclusions)} synthesizes key results, answers research hypotheses, and outlines priorities for future research.
\end{itemize}

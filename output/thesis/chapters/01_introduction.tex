\section{Problem, Practical Value, and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

{
Agricultural yield becomes observable at harvest, after many operational and commercial commitments have been made. Earlier forecasts can support risk management in agrifood supply chains. Soybean supplies domestic livestock feed and international trade, with the Midwestern Corn Belt driving U.S. production \citep{Schlenker2009}.
}

Harvest surprises in this region carry severe, asymmetric economic costs. Regional crop shortfalls, typically driven by summer heatwaves or moisture deficits, generate procurement bottlenecks and sharp price spikes for grain crushers, feed millers, and export terminals. Empirically, across the 1960--2025 record, each 10\% negative departure from secular yield trend is associated with an average 7.5\% increase in annual farm-gate prices ($p = 0.014$), with intra-seasonal futures rallies on the Chicago Board of Trade (CBOT) frequently exceeding 30--60\% during acute summer droughts. Conversely, unexpectedly bumper harvests compress local storage capacity, create barge and rail bottlenecks along the Mississippi corridor, and exert downward pressure on regional cash basis levels.

We focus specifically on county-level soybean yield anomalies, measured in bushels per acre. Aggregate production volumes and market prices fall outside our scope: production totals depend on acreage allocations across counties, while commercial prices reflect global carryover stocks and trade flows. These supply-chain repercussions establish the practical motivation for our research.

{
Forecast value depends on lead time. Earlier estimates can support procurement and storage planning, while in-season updates inform sourcing and freight commitments. We assess three criteria: accuracy across the yield distribution, the timing of predictive gains, and their persistence across subsequent updates. Twelve monthly forecast origins, anchored to an October harvest reference, trace this progression ($H=12,\dots,1$).
}

\section{Research Question and Target Formulations}
\label{sec:intro_research_question}

A fundamental empirical challenge in agricultural forecasting is that raw county yields are heavily dominated by secular technological progress, including genetic improvement, fertilizer efficiency, and mechanization. Consequently, predictive models must isolate weather-induced yield anomalies from the underlying secular trajectory, evaluating how much actionable forecasting skill observed surface weather provides across successive stages of the crop cycle.

The central research question asks: \textit{At what lead times prior to harvest does observed surface weather add statistically significant and stable out-of-sample predictive skill for county-level soybean yield anomalies, and how does this skill vary across model families and temporal aggregations?}

To address this question, we structure our predictive framework around two complementary evaluation dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous Anomaly Forecasting (Regression):} We predict continuous county-level yield anomalies obtained by isolating weather-induced deviations from secular technological trends fitted strictly on historical training folds. Absolute yield levels are subsequently reconstructed by recombining predicted anomalies with the estimated trend.
    \item \textbf{Downside Tail-Risk Anticipation:} Beyond standard quadratic error metrics across the full distribution, we evaluate the capacity of empirical continuous models to anticipate acute downside tail shocks (specifically the bottom yield decile $D_1$) and correctly identify directional anomaly signs well before harvest.
\end{itemize}

We structure our investigation around four primary objectives: mapping the emergence and temporal stability of predictive skill across horizons ($H=12 \dots 1$); identifying the optimal model architecture and temporal aggregation (5-day, 10-day, or 30-day summaries); contrasting non-linear tree ensembles and sequential neural networks against regularized linear baselines; and evaluating the anticipation of severe downside tail risks. We derive formal hypotheses (H1--H5) addressing these objectives in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review}.

\section{Methodological Contributions and Scope Boundaries}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The literature on empirical crop modeling encompasses diverse approaches to predicting county-level soybean yields from environmental data. Recent studies have advanced the field from complementary perspectives: \citet{Vijverberg2023} identified pre-sowing climate precursors for extreme yield events; \citet{Yinetal2026} demonstrated the influence of sub-monthly temporal aggregation on modern architectures over recent decades; and the multidecadal benchmark by \citet{ChenZhang2026} examined in-season deep learning across monthly growing-season updates.

However, existing empirical studies examine these dimensions in isolation, focusing on specific phenological windows, individual model classes, or restricted historical intervals. We synthesize these empirical branches into a unified benchmarking framework.

We address these gaps through two primary methodological contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic 12-Month Lead-Time Mapping Without Forecast Inputs ($H=12, \dots, 1$):} We establish the empirical boundaries of weather-driven predictability across twelve standardized monthly origins, spanning from the preceding autumn through harvest eve. By relying strictly on cumulative surface reanalysis from ERA5-Land without numerical forecast inputs, we track precisely when observed atmospheric states begin to generate stable predictive skill.
    {\item \textbf{Comparison of Forecasting Specifications:} We compare regularized linear models, tree ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost), and recurrent architectures (LSTM) on the selected county panel. The final benchmark evaluates monthly tabular models and a 10-day LSTM. The recurrent frequency and head-specific parameters are fixed before the final test evaluation.}
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope Boundaries and Methodological Discipline.}
We evaluate this framework on soybean across a balanced panel of 135 counties in six core Midwestern states (Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, and Ohio) over 1951--2025 ($10{,}125$ county-year observations). This geographic and temporal delimitation provides an unbroken historical record that avoids artificial data imputation.

We interpret feature importance strictly as empirical predictive association, without claiming structural causal identification.

{
Weather-only models provide the initial benchmark. We then test the incremental contribution of lagged yield anomalies and retain integrated specifications supported by that comparison. Reported scores distinguish the two information sets; lagged anomalies are detrended on training data and included only when available at the forecast origin.
}

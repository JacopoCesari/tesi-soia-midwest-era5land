\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Problem, Practical Value, and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

Agricultural yield becomes definitively observable only at harvest, long after key operational, commercial, and financial commitments have been finalized. In modern agrifood systems, reliable yield forecasts well in advance are essential for systematic risk management. In the United States, soybean is a foundational commodity underpinning domestic livestock feed and global trade flows, with the Midwestern Corn Belt driving national production \citep{Schlenker2009}.

Harvest surprises in this region carry severe, asymmetric economic costs. Regional crop shortfalls, typically driven by summer heatwaves or moisture deficits, generate procurement bottlenecks and sharp price spikes for grain crushers, feed millers, and export terminals. Conversely, unexpectedly bumper harvests compress local storage capacity, create barge and rail bottlenecks along the Mississippi corridor, and exert downward pressure on regional cash basis levels.

We focus specifically on county-level soybean yield anomalies, measured in bushels per acre. Aggregate production volumes and market prices fall outside our scope: production totals depend on acreage allocations across counties, while commercial prices reflect global carryover stocks and trade flows. These supply-chain repercussions establish the practical motivation for our research.

In agricultural supply chains, a forecast's utility depends directly on its lead time. In operational trading, procurement, and hedging, an accurate yield estimate delivered immediately prior to harvest provides little managerial flexibility: physical purchasing, storage reservations, and forward pricing have largely concluded. The central intellectual and practical challenge consists in determining \textit{when} atmospheric observations transition from uninformative noise into a reliable forecasting signal. 

Operationally, an effective yield forecasting system must satisfy three criteria: accuracy across the yield distribution (especially for severe downside risks), lead-time earliness to maximize managerial reaction windows, and temporal stability to ensure that early gains persist across subsequent updates. The practical utility of these forecasts evolves dynamically across the production calendar:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-sowing signals ($H \ge 8$):} inform seed variety selection, maturity group allocation, acreage switching, input distribution, and commercial forward contracting.
    \item \textbf{Vegetative updates ($H \approx 7 \text{ to } 4$):} direct in-season farm interventions (replanting, fertilizer split applications, crop protection) alongside producer price hedging and origination.
    \item \textbf{Reproductive pod-filling updates ($H \approx 3 \text{ to } 2$):} allow producers to pace forward sales and prepare on-farm storage, while processors and exporters adjust crush schedules, freight bookings, and secondary sourcing.
    \item \textbf{Near-harvest updates ($H=1$):} optimize harvest sequencing, drying logistics, and terminal intake schedules.
\end{itemize}
To evaluate this progression systematically, we anchor the crop campaign to an October harvest reference and track predictive skill across twelve standardized monthly forecast origins ($H=12, \dots, 1$).

\section{Research Question and Target Formulations}
\label{sec:intro_research_question}

A fundamental empirical challenge in agricultural forecasting is that raw county yields are heavily dominated by secular technological progress, including genetic improvement, fertilizer efficiency, and mechanization. Consequently, predictive models must isolate weather-induced yield anomalies from the underlying secular trajectory, evaluating how much actionable forecasting skill observed surface weather provides across successive stages of the crop cycle.

The central research question asks: \textit{At what lead times prior to harvest does observed surface weather add statistically significant and stable out-of-sample predictive skill for county-level soybean yield anomalies, and how does this skill vary across model families and temporal aggregations?}

To address this question, we evaluate two complementary target formulations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous Anomaly Forecasting (Regression):} We model continuous county-level yield anomalies obtained by removing the secular technological trend via transformations fitted strictly on historical training folds. We then reconstruct absolute yield levels by recombining predicted anomalies with the estimated trend.
    \item \textbf{Categorical Regime Forecasting (Classification):} Complementing continuous regression, we test whether broad yield regimes (severe shortfall, normal, and bumper harvests) can be identified earlier or with greater reliability than continuous magnitudes.
\end{itemize}

We structure our investigation around four primary objectives: mapping the emergence and temporal stability of predictive skill across horizons ($H=12 \dots 1$); identifying the optimal model architecture and temporal aggregation (5-day, 10-day, or 30-day summaries); contrasting non-linear tree ensembles and sequential neural networks against regularized linear baselines; and exploring early regime classification. We derive formal hypotheses (H1--H5) addressing these objectives in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review}.

\section{Methodological Contributions and Scope Boundaries}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The literature on empirical crop modeling encompasses diverse approaches to predicting county-level soybean yields from environmental data. Recent studies have advanced the field from complementary perspectives: \citet{Vijverberg2023} identified pre-sowing climate precursors for extreme yield events; \citet{Yinetal2026} demonstrated the influence of sub-monthly temporal aggregation on modern architectures over recent decades; and the multidecadal benchmark by \citet{ChenZhang2026} examined in-season deep learning across monthly growing-season updates.

However, existing empirical studies examine these dimensions in isolation, focusing on specific phenological windows, individual model classes, or restricted historical intervals. We synthesize these empirical branches into a unified benchmarking framework.

We address these gaps through two primary methodological contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic 12-Month Lead-Time Mapping Without Forecast Inputs ($H=12, \dots, 1$):} We establish the empirical boundaries of weather-driven predictability across twelve standardized monthly origins, spanning from the preceding autumn through harvest eve. By relying strictly on cumulative surface reanalysis from ERA5-Land, we track precisely when observed atmospheric states begin to generate stable predictive skill, avoiding the confounding bias of numerical climate forecasts.
    \item \textbf{Identification of Optimal Model-Aggregation Configurations:} We systematically evaluate multiple model families—regularized linear baselines, tree ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost), and recurrent architectures (LSTM)—across three temporal aggregations (5-day, 10-day, and 30-day summaries). This establishes whether high-frequency sub-monthly summaries provide superior predictive value over standard monthly averages under identical information constraints.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope Boundaries and Methodological Discipline.}
While our evaluation framework is transferable to other crops and regions, we deliberately focus on soybean across a balanced panel of 135 counties in six core Midwestern states (Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, and Ohio) over 1951--2025 ($10{,}125$ county-year observations). This geographic and temporal delimitation provides an unbroken historical record that avoids artificial data imputation.

We interpret feature importance strictly as empirical predictive association, without claiming structural causal identification. Furthermore, because we evaluate retrospective ERA5-Land reanalysis over a 75-year panel, our primary objective is to delineate the physical boundaries of weather-driven predictability across lead times.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro_structure}

We structure the remainder of this thesis as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter 2 (Literature Review)} reviews soybean weather sensitivity, examines empirical yield forecasting approaches, and formalizes hypotheses H1--H5.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 3 (Data and Study Area)} describes the 135-county balanced panel (1951--2025) from USDA NASS, documents ERA5-Land processing, and presents exploratory climate-yield analyses.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 4 (Methodology)} formalizes the twelve forecast cutoffs, feature engineering workflows, baseline and machine learning specifications, and the expanding-window validation protocol.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 5 (Empirical Results)} reports out-of-sample performance across all twelve horizons, comparing baselines, tree ensembles, and sequential models.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 6 (Discussion)} interprets findings for agricultural risk management, addressing survivorship, reanalysis latency, and external validity.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 7 (Conclusions)} synthesizes key results, answers research hypotheses, and outlines priorities for future research.
\end{itemize}

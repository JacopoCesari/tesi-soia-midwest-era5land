\section{Problem, Practical Value, and Motivation}
\label{sec:intro_problem}

Agricultural yield becomes definitively observable only at harvest, long after key operational, commercial, and financial commitments have been finalized. In modern agrifood systems, reliable yield forecasts well in advance are essential for systematic risk management. In the United States, soybean (\textit{Glycine max}) is a foundational commodity underpinning domestic livestock feed and global trade flows, with the Midwestern Corn Belt driving national production \citep{Schlenker2009}.

Harvest surprises in this region carry severe, asymmetric economic costs. Regional crop shortfalls, typically driven by summer heatwaves or moisture deficits, generate procurement bottlenecks and sharp price spikes for grain crushers, feed millers, and export terminals. Conversely, unexpectedly bumper harvests compress local storage capacity, create barge and rail bottlenecks along the Mississippi corridor, and exert downward pressure on regional cash basis levels.

Crucially, this thesis models county-level crop yield anomalies (in bushels per acre) rather than aggregate production volumes or market prices directly. Total production depends on acreage allocations and spatial aggregation, while price formation is governed by global carryover stocks and trade flows. These supply-chain repercussions establish the practical motivation for this study.

From both an applied risk-management perspective and an academic standpoint, a forecast's utility is inseparable from its lead time. In operational trading, procurement, and hedging, an accurate yield estimate delivered immediately prior to harvest provides little managerial flexibility: physical purchasing, storage reservations, and forward pricing have largely concluded. The central intellectual and practical challenge consists in determining \textit{when} atmospheric observations transition from uninformative statistical noise into a reliable, actionable forecasting signal. 

Operationally, an effective yield forecasting system must satisfy three essential requirements: predictive accuracy across the distribution (with dedicated capacity to capture severe downside risks), lead-time earliness to maximize managerial reaction windows, and temporal stability ensuring that early predictive gains persist across subsequent updates rather than reflecting transient sample noise. The decision-making value of these signals evolves across the production cycle: pre-sowing signals ($H \ge 8$) inform acreage allocations and crop rotations; vegetative stage updates ($H \approx 7 \text{ to } 4$) guide producer and merchandiser risk exposure; reproductive pod-filling updates ($H \approx 3 \text{ to } 2$) allow processors and exporters to adjust forward sourcing and logistics; and near-harvest forecasts ($H=1$) optimize terminal bin allocation and intake runtimes. To evaluate this progression systematically, this study anchors the production campaign to an October harvest reference and tracks predictive skill across twelve standardized monthly forecast origins ($H=12, \dots, 1$).

\section{Research Question, Target Formulations, and Baseline Decoupling}
\label{sec:intro_research_question}

A fundamental empirical challenge in agricultural forecasting is that raw county yields are heavily dominated by secular technological progress (seed genetics, fertilizer efficiency, mechanization) and static soil endowments. Consequently, the core empirical question is not simply whether a machine learning model fits historical yields, but how much incremental predictive skill observed weather adds beyond historical information. The empirical design isolates this marginal contribution by benchmarking integrated weather models against reference historical baselines (deterministic trends, county climatology, and admissible past yields) under strictly identical information sets.

The central research question asks: \textit{At what lead times prior to harvest does observed surface weather add statistically significant and stable out-of-sample predictive skill for county-level soybean yield anomalies beyond historical trend, climatology, and past-yield baselines, and how does this skill vary across model families and temporal aggregations?}

To address this question, the empirical modeling evaluates two complementary target formulations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous Anomaly Forecasting (Regression):} The primary regression task models continuous county-level yield anomalies obtained by removing the secular technological trend via transformations fitted strictly on historical training folds. Absolute yield levels are subsequently reconstructed by recombining predicted anomalies with the estimated trend.
    \item \textbf{Categorical Regime Forecasting (Classification):} Complementing continuous regression, a discrete classification framework evaluates whether broad yield regimes (such as severe shortfall, normal, and bumper harvests) can be identified earlier or with greater reliability than precise continuous magnitudes.
\end{itemize}

The empirical investigation is guided by five primary objectives: isolating incremental weather skill from historical inertia, tracing performance trajectories across horizons ($H=12 \dots 1$), assessing high-frequency temporal summaries (5-day and 10-day vs.\ monthly averages), contrasting model complexities (linear models, tree ensembles, and sequential LSTMs), and testing early regime classification. The formal research hypotheses (H1--H5) addressing these objectives are grounded in the literature and derived in Chapter~\ref{chap:literature_review}.

\section{Methodological Contributions and Scope Boundaries}
\label{sec:intro_contributions}

The literature on crop modeling demonstrates that machine learning can predict county-level soybean yields from environmental data, with the recent multidecadal study by \citet{ChenZhang2026} serving as a primary empirical benchmark. However, existing work leaves open the question of when current observed weather begins to add stable predictive skill beyond historical baselines under progressive, train-only temporal validation.

This thesis addresses this gap through three primary methodological contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Systematic Lead-Time Mapping ($H=12, \dots, 1$):} Extending forecasting horizons across twelve standardized monthly cutoffs spanning from the preceding autumn through harvest eve, systematically tracking the emergence and stability of predictive signals.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous Baseline Decoupling:} Formally isolating the genuine predictive value of observed ERA5-Land weather from technological trends, county climatology, and autoregressive persistence under a strict, train-only expanding-window protocol that eliminates spatial and temporal leakage.
    \item \textbf{Multi-Granularity and Architectural Comparison:} Systematically evaluating high-frequency temporal summaries (5-day and 10-day vs.\ 30-day aggregations) and derived non-linear agro-climatic indices across regularized linear models, tree ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost), and recurrent deep learning (LSTM) architectures under identical information constraints.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Scope Boundaries and Methodological Discipline.}
The proposed evaluation framework is transferable in principle to other weather-sensitive crops and regions. However, the empirical application is subject to clear boundaries: it is intentionally restricted to soybean across 135 balanced counties in six Midwestern states (Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Missouri, and Ohio) over the 75-year period 1951--2025 (10,125 observations), using surface meteorological fields from ECMWF ERA5-Land. 

To maintain academic discipline, this study makes no claim of structural or causal inference: feature relevance is interpreted strictly as empirical predictive association. Furthermore, the retrospective use of finalized reanalysis data and the restriction to a balanced historical panel mean that these models are designed to map the physical limits of weather-driven predictability, rather than claiming immediate real-time operational deployment.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
\label{sec:intro_structure}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter 2 (Literature Review)} reviews soybean weather sensitivity, examines empirical yield forecasting approaches, and formalizes hypotheses H1--H5.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 3 (Data and Study Area)} describes the 135-county balanced panel (1951--2025) from USDA NASS, documents ERA5-Land processing, and presents exploratory climate-yield analyses.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 4 (Methodology)} formalizes the twelve forecast cutoffs, feature engineering workflows, baseline and machine learning specifications, and the expanding-window validation protocol.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 5 (Empirical Results)} reports out-of-sample performance across all twelve horizons, comparing baselines, tree ensembles, and sequential models.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 6 (Discussion)} interprets findings for agricultural risk management, addressing survivorship, reanalysis latency, and external validity.
    \item \textbf{Chapter 7 (Conclusions)} synthesizes key results, answers research hypotheses, and outlines priorities for future research.
\end{itemize}
